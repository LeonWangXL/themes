# Overview

This book distills what author Sun Yuzheng learned through years of hands-on experience at major technology companies and exploration in the business world. Centered on the idea of “real skills,” it lays out a systematic path from salaried employee to financial freedom: understand the market, refine your product, use leverage, strengthen your core competitiveness, and build a moat around your personal value.

The book argues that making money is a craft—one that can be learned. At its heart is an honest reckoning with how personal value is converted into income. Through two parts, “Cognitive Awakening” and “Uniting Knowledge with Action,” the author builds a radically simple model for advancement. He explains how to identify high-value work, how to manage yourself as if you were a product, and how a craftsperson’s mindset can free you from dependence on a single salary, leading to both financial and spiritual freedom.

Drawing on the author’s deep practical experience at Amazon, Meta, Tencent, and other companies, the book combines an international perspective with ideas that can be put to work locally. It is highly practical. It offers valuable guidance not only to professionals seeking a career breakthrough or a second growth curve, but also to young people who want to see through the logic of business and wealth, reject blind hard work, and find a way forward in life.

# Preface

From the “Chosen Corporate Worker” to Life as an Infinite Game

Before I turned thirty-five, I used to introduce myself as a “chosen corporate worker.” I had several glittering labels that made me proud: a PhD in economics from Cornell University, a former Amazon economist, a data scientist at Meta, an associate director of data science at Tencent, and a two-time recipient of the company’s five-star performance rating. By conventional standards, these labels carried real prestige. They satisfied my vanity and gave me a respectable, comfortable life. Yet I slowly realized that none of them made me genuinely happy from within.

The year I turned thirty-five, I began to ask myself: Is another promotion and raise really what I want? If respectability and comfort are purchased with freedom, are they truly respectable or comfortable? Some people seem to be charging ahead, but in reality they are like hamsters running in place on a wheel. They work hard by the rules of corporate life and chase promotions and raises, only to receive more work and greater pressure in return. Their own ideals and freedom remain unrealized; eventually, they can no longer even remember what those ideals were. I recall an event where the host asked me, “If money were no object, what would you most want to do?” I searched my mind and could not come up with an answer.

What finally made me decide to jump off the hamster wheel was a scene I still remember vividly.

On a business trip in the United States, I saw a huge hiring banner at McDonald’s offering $17.50 an hour. Through the meritocratic lens of mainstream values, the job had no career prospects and posed no technical challenge; surely it was not a respectable choice. But at that moment, I seriously placed “working at McDonald’s” on one side of a scale and my own job on the other. I instantly understood why the McDonald’s job carried so much weight: it offered a kind of freedom that a director at a major tech company did not have. During working hours, you did not have to exhaust your mind or worry about being laid off; after work, you did not have to constantly check the company messaging app or endlessly push yourself to learn more. I realized that no salary or social status, however high, could outweigh freedom.

In that instant, I woke up. For more than thirty years, I seemed to have lived by other people’s judgments. It was time to find the life I wanted and begin living for myself.

The Way Out Is to Combine Your Day Job with a Side Business

In today’s world, quitting cold to pursue freedom would have been a gamble with terrible odds. And although the position I had worked so hard to earn constrained me, it was also my greatest asset. I therefore realized that the most efficient way out was not to tear everything down and start over. It was to keep improving at my job, while shifting my main goal away from promotions and raises toward building a business of my own—gradually developing the ability and channels to earn money without depending on my day job. Those are the real skills that can earn you freedom.

At first, I worried that wanting both would be wishful thinking and that I would end up with neither. After practicing this approach for a while, however, I discovered that it was actually the most sensible and efficient path. A day job and a side business draw on the same underlying capabilities and can even reinforce each other. Skills learned at work can be applied to a side business; insights gained from a side business can, in turn, improve your performance at work.

Others had already demonstrated this point. Both *Low-Risk Entrepreneurship* and *The 4-Hour Workweek* point to the same central idea: results are determined not by how many hours you put in, but by the level of your understanding and the efficiency of your methods. But how do we deepen that understanding and find those methods? This is precisely where many of us have a blind spot. We are accustomed to searching textbooks for standard answers and measuring progress with test scores, but that approach simply does not work in the real world. Fortunately, I stumbled onto an effective path for learning.

At the time, I was using social media to seek advice, with an apprentice’s mindset, from more than a hundred accomplished people across many industries. After my awakening, the subjects of my interviews quietly changed. My early focus on working smarter and winning promotions and raises gradually gave way to the underlying logic of business, effective ways to make money, and the search for meaning in life.

From my own experience, I distilled a growth flywheel:

(1) Expand your awareness: break out of the prison of old thinking and see new possibilities.

(2) Learn from those ahead of you: seek advice from people who have already achieved the results you want and absorb their experience.

(3) Get in the arena: practice deliberately until what you have learned becomes instinctive.

Following this path, I gradually found and developed real skills of my own.

Six months later, I left big tech, returned to the United States, and joined Statsig, a startup I had chosen with care. I moved out of management and into a role combining technology and marketing: developer evangelist. A year later, I launched a paid-membership channel and an AI course, and my side income surpassed my salary. Six months after that, I built a membership community and an AI community. Today, those communities have more than ten thousand members.

After I had worked at Statsig for two years, OpenAI acquired the company for $1.1 billion. By then, I had also accumulated enough real skills to leave Statsig with confidence and devote myself fully to my own business. Today, that business earns me far more than I ever made as an employee. More importantly, it has restored a sense of freedom and meaning that I had not felt in a long time.

Later, another friend asked me, “If money were no object, what would you most want to do?” After thinking carefully, I answered, “Exactly what I am doing now.” At that moment, I knew I had found my own infinite game in life. I felt extraordinarily fortunate—and deeply happy.

What Keeps Us from Getting Results?

Looking back after achieving a measure of success, I see three main reasons why so many people struggle to escape the hamster wheel.

First, deeply rooted limits in how we think. From childhood onward, too many mistaken patterns of thought are planted in us. They distort our understanding of work, the workplace, and the rules of business. When we misunderstand those rules, we pour enormous amounts of time and energy into things that bring no return and remain stuck in place. Worse still, flawed thinking strips us of judgment and, at the root, destroys our ability to iterate and improve.

Second, a lack of effective guidance. In an age of information overload, there is no shortage of prominent experts and “mentors.” Yet the experience of highly successful people often does not apply to ordinary people. Their methods may be hard to put into practice, or they may be too advanced for others to understand and use right away. As for many of the “mentors” in the marketplace, their principal source of income may be mentoring itself. Their advice can therefore remain largely theoretical: everything sounds right, but none of it can be put to work.

Finally, an inability to identify what matters most. This problem is less visible than the first two and harder to overcome. Our time and energy are limited. To break through, we must focus our resources on the most important thing right now. Otherwise, the more we learn, the more confused we become. So what is the most important thing at this moment? Often, only someone who has been in circumstances similar to yours can tell you clearly. Many apparently helpful answers from other people merely consume and scatter your precious time and energy.

How Will This Book Help You Develop Real Skills?

Two conditions are necessary for the growth flywheel to turn in a virtuous cycle: break through your limitations and build sound mental models; then keep accumulating effective methods and applying them deeply in practice.

I, too, took many detours, paid plenty of tuition to life, and stepped into more than a few traps. Yet I believe that if someone had clearly explained the right thinking and methods at the outset, all those detours could have been avoided. Drawing on my experience, I systematically distilled and organized those insights and methods, then turned them into an online course. The positive feedback and tangible results from my students persuaded me to develop the material into a book, so that more people could acquire the real skills needed to win their freedom at lower cost and with greater convenience.

This book has two parts.

Part One is “Cognitive Awakening.” It examines three of the most common and damaging patterns of thought and offers sound alternatives to each, helping you lay a solid cognitive foundation and begin iterating on your thinking. With a stable foundation, we can grow faster and farther without taking unnecessary detours.

I then build and unpack the “personal value formula” around the ultimate goal of financial freedom, clarifying the true relationship among wealth, capital, and personal value. Through the theory in Part One, we can:

· See through the fog and identify what is truly worth pursuing;

· Work efficiently, freeing time and energy for more meaningful exploration;

· Go from zero to one by beginning to monetize our personal value in the business world.

Part Two is “Uniting Knowledge with Action.” It approaches practical methods from two levels—working and making money—and shares cases I have personally tested. It will help you use employment to accumulate human capital in your current environment, then try to monetize that capital. Why “work first, make money second”? Because a day job can give us a strong starting point and a platform for growth, while a side business forces us to confront the rules of business more directly and can gradually develop into an enterprise of our own. This is a more comfortable path with better odds of success.

Knowing is easy; doing is hard. It is not easy to do excellent work or actually make money. The methods summarized in Part Two come from my extensive reading, from what I learned by consulting more than a hundred accomplished people, and above all from my own practice. They helped me launch a business while advancing my career, and their effectiveness has also been confirmed by many members of my communities and viewers of my channel, saving them numerous detours and painful mistakes.

A Note on My Original Intention

How do I introduce myself today? My answer is: “Whatever fits.” It depends on the occasion. Deep down, the three labels I identify with most are good husband, good father, and good person. I cherish these labels because they mean that the right to define my life has moved from the outside world back to me. I have gained a precious freedom.

Life is an infinite game. “Real skills” do not mean suddenly turning your life around with one spectacular stroke of luck. They mean thinking one step further and doing a little better than others in every small detail. When those seemingly insignificant improvements meet time, compounding delivers an unexpected reward. I hope the frameworks in this book help you keep evolving until you find and complete your own path to freedom.

In the end, you need only focus on accumulation. If this book feels right for you, join me now and let us explore these ideas in depth.

Sun Yuzheng

# Part One: Cognitive Awakening

## Chapter 1: Wake Up from the Dream of “Good Student → Good Job → Big Money”

### Wake Up—It Is Only a Dream

I want to begin this chapter by puncturing a beautiful dream you have heard since childhood and may still believe today: study hard and you will find a good job; find a good job and you will make a lot of money. This is the route prescribed by meritocracy.

Waking you from this dream and showing you the world as it really is would not be difficult. The hard question is what comes next. There are two potential dangers, so we must proceed step by step and make the foundation of our thinking secure.

#### Danger One: Deeply Rooted Conflicts of Values

Meritocracy has not merely laid out a predetermined road through life for us. Over more than a decade, it has quietly shaped our core values. Think back to childhood. If one classmate studied hard, we praised them for “applying themselves to what matters.” If another neglected school and spent all day thinking about how to make money, we considered them “irresponsible,” whether or not they actually made any. Once we depart from the mainstream path, therefore, we face not only new challenges and uncertainty but also conflicts within our own values. Subconsciously, we worry that the outside world will see us as misfits—or failures.

#### Danger Two: Confusion and Cynicism After Waking

If I simply wake you up by force but offer no better alternative or more effective solution, I may leave you confused or even cynical. At best, you might chase quick returns and get cheated out of money and time. At worst, you might take a more dangerous wrong turn. That is why the foundation of our thinking truly must be solid.

This chapter will do two things. First, it will wake you from the old dream. I will reason from both directions to show why “good student → good job → big money” is, in all likelihood, an illusion. Second, it will ask you to open your eyes and look at the world. I will show you its reality—or, more precisely, the actual rules of the business world.

### The Forward Case: Why Is Meritocracy Probably a Dead End?

Let us begin with the forward case. I believe that most readers of this book, myself included, have either traveled or are still traveling the road of meritocracy with all their strength.

Take me as an example. I worked hard to enter university, then went from an undergraduate at an ordinary school to a doctoral program at a prestigious one. I worked hard again to find a good job, and then to win promotions and raises at the company. I followed the rules at every step. What was the result? I discovered that I was not living the life I truly wanted at all. Instead, I was lost. In my experience, this road is unlikely to take us to the life we genuinely want.

My story may not fully resonate with you, so let me guide you through a simple investigation. It will help you see more directly where the “ceiling” on this road probably lies.

Why conduct this investigation? Because human beings are born with many cognitive biases. One common bias is that we overimagine the likelihood of rare events while failing to assess objectively just how unlikely they are.

Consider the lottery. We cannot help fantasizing about how we would spend a jackpot, but few people truly grasp, in a rational way, how vanishingly small their odds of winning are. To our brains, a 0.1 percent chance and a 0.001 percent chance do not feel very different; both are simply “unlikely.” Assets worth hundreds of millions and assets worth tens of billions likewise feel similar; both mean “very rich.” In reality, a gap of twofold is already enormous, tenfold is a different world, and a hundredfold scarcely bears comparison.

Apply this bias to salaried work and we tend to notice the tiny handful of extraordinarily successful people, then subconsciously conclude: “I can become like them too.”

To remove this bias, conduct the following investigation. Observe and analyze the senior colleagues around you who share a background similar to yours—the same level of education, the same company, perhaps even the same personality type—but entered the field several years or even a decade before you. Why these people? Because your backgrounds were similar and your initial choices much the same. They have also survived an additional round of selection. They are the “survivors”: people who spent years or decades working “successfully” at this company or in this industry and remained there.

You may not think that staying with one company for more than ten years counts as success. But remember: you have not yet passed through the same selection. After a few years, you may well find that you cannot continue—either the company decides you are not a fit, or you can no longer stand it and choose to leave. Whether actively or passively, those people passed the survivor test; you have not. Moreover, when they entered the workforce, they probably faced far better macroeconomic and industry conditions than you do. Their present circumstances are therefore a reasonable approximation of your ceiling.

Now look carefully and think: What is their annual income? Where does most of their time go? Is the life they live now the life you want?

Analyze these questions, consider how long they have been working, and weigh the answer in your own mind. My guess is that this “ceiling” is unlikely to bring you genuine financial freedom. It may not enable you to care properly for your parents or fulfill your responsibilities to your children. It may not even give you an adequate sense of security.

This means that the “good student → good job” path you selected by default probably does not end in the riches and good life you imagined. It is more likely to end with you drained by endless overtime, or ruthlessly laid off at some point, with too little time and too few resources left to do what you truly want.

The conclusion of the forward case is clear: the familiar path will, in all likelihood, lead us into a dead end.

### The Reverse Case: Meritocracy May Not Even Conform to the Rules of Business

We have made the forward case. Now let us reason backward. Allow me one assumption: your ultimate ideal is financial freedom and the realization of your personal value.

Chapter 7 will examine financial freedom in detail. For now, a necessary simplification will suffice. Realizing your personal value usually requires enough wealth to support it. Here, “wealth” does not mean money alone. It includes your resources, health, and time; money is merely the universal token for exchanging among them.

In short, the purpose of working is not to work forever. We work to earn money, to gain freedom, and to realize our personal value. So here is the question: Does a company have an obligation to help you realize your personal value?

No.

Think carefully about what a company wants from its own point of view. A company’s objective is to generate profit and maximize commercial value. What is the most rational way to achieve that objective? It is to obtain as much value from you as possible—your time and results—at the lowest possible cost, your salary. And when your value declines over time, it will replace you with someone more cost-effective.

At this point, you may feel an urge to curse your boss. I suggest that you resist it for now. On the level of reality, this is simply a basic operating law of the business world. Complaining that the rules are unfair and calling your boss heartless can worsen your mood, but cannot change your circumstances. At root, much of that dissatisfaction is a way of avoiding the responsibility you should assume yourself.

Only we can truly change our own circumstances.

This raises another question. If the company’s goals and yours are in natural conflict, why do most of us still consider “studying hard and working hard” self-evidently right?

For the answer, we must return to school. A school’s central purpose is to maximize social value, just as a company seeks to maximize commercial value. A stable, developing society needs a large population of steady people who form its foundation through work—production—and consumption. Education unquestionably improves your skills and personal value. At the same time, however, it intentionally or unintentionally plants values aligned with society’s overall interests deep in your mind. It teaches you that “finding a stable job is good,” while those who choose another path are “neglecting what they ought to be doing.”

Schools and the value system they transmit may be optimal for society as a whole, but they are not necessarily the best path for each individual. That is why we must “open our eyes and look at the world”: while contributing to society, we must also fight for what we ourselves want. What does it mean to open our eyes? It means understanding the rules of business and acquiring the real skills needed to make money.

### The Rules of Business

What, then, are the rules of business? They are quite simple—almost brutally so.

(1) Wealth will not come to your door. You have to go out and claim it.

(2) The best and most sustainable way to claim wealth is to exchange equivalent personal value for it.

(3) Personal value is determined by market demand.

Personal value is not innate. We can cultivate and gradually establish it as we work and learn to make money; we can also increase it through leverage. But the most important point is that personal value is formed in the process of satisfying market demand.

Remember this above all: **Personal value is determined by market demand.**

This point is critical. From childhood onward, we become far too accustomed to other people telling us what has value. We grow used to examination mode. In that mode, there are explicit questions and correct answers; the answers are all in the textbook; and the harder the question, the more valuable it must be. In the real world, explicit questions, correct answers, textbooks, and the presumed value of difficult problems all disappear.

Anyone with work experience can readily verify this from personal experience. A difficult problem is not necessarily valuable; it may simply be hard to understand at first. Many companies, especially high-tech companies, place great value on an employee’s “technical ability” to solve difficult problems. I therefore designed a thought experiment to help you understand that value and difficulty are two different concepts.

Imagine that one hundred different examination papers lie before you. Together, they contain ten thousand questions. You possess the knowledge needed to answer any of them. Beside every question is its “price,” written in Arabic numerals: the reward you will receive for answering it correctly.

What should you do first? You should not immediately search for the question you are best at, nor should you seek out the one that looks hardest. Your first move should be to learn Arabic—that is, to determine what each question is actually worth.

In this thought experiment, learning Arabic is the prerequisite for judging value. In reality, Arabic corresponds to “the language of the market”: your understanding of market demand. Only after you understand how the market prices different skills and solutions can you identify the examination paper offering the highest return for the effort and then focus on solving it.

As you work through the paper, you will find questions you are good at and others you are not. But do not mistake “what you are good at” for “what has value,” or “what is hard” for “what has value.” Above all, do not bury your head in the paper and solve questions blindly. Solving questions is not the goal; making money is. Working is not the goal; gaining freedom and realizing your personal value are.

I hope this thought experiment helps you feel just how many deep imprints and constraints our education has left in our minds.

### Learning Method: Your Companion in a Parallel World

To show more clearly how our old mental patterns influence us, I will introduce a character in this book named Little S. Imagine Little S as a person exactly like you today. Little S has precisely the same background, resources, and starting point. There is only one difference: Little S has already read and understood the effective patterns of thought presented in this book, while you—for now—have not.

In later chapters, I will repeatedly use Little S as an example.

· Faced with the same situation, how would Little S think under a different mental model? What different choice would Little S make?

· What different result would that choice produce?

By comparing yourself with Little S, you will see clearly that even with identical starting conditions, different mental models allow Little S to continually build, accumulate, and amplify personal value through work and practice. Confronted with the very same opportunities, meanwhile, you may fail to seize them because of limits in your thinking—or fail even to recognize them as opportunities.

The greatest value a good book can offer is to change your future one choice at a time. No book can instantly change your present or immediately make you rich and well connected. But it can change how you understand the world and how you think, thereby changing your “acceleration.” Given time, this raises your ceiling, increases your rate of growth, and enables you to realize more personal value. This is why I emphasized in the preface that real skills cannot be mastered all at once. They require accumulated time. They are built day after day.

We have now established that the greatest difference between you and Little S lies in cognition.

In the next chapter, then, we will begin with cognition in earnest. In this age of information overload, how should we find, filter, and absorb genuinely useful information? How do we turn it into understanding that helps us make effective judgments about value?

## Chapter 2: The Right Way to Learn—Leave the Classroom Mindset Behind and Embrace the Real World

### Relearning “How to Learn”

In the previous chapter, we discussed why our thinking must change and what that change is meant to achieve: learning to recognize value and using the right methods to accumulate personal value, so that we can follow the logic of business and make money. This chapter focuses on how effective learning can change the way we understand the world.

You may say, “I spent more than a decade in school. Do I really need someone to teach me how to learn?” In fact, it is precisely because of our past experience in education that we need to relearn how to learn. The methods used in school were tailor-made for examinations and academic subjects. They do not fully apply to the two real arenas of society: working and making money.

### The Traps of the “Good Student” Mindset

One guest on my channel, Mike Develin, is a leading data scientist who previously headed analytics at Instagram and Shopify. In one of our conversations, he observed that many technical professionals—even some of Silicon Valley’s best data scientists—are highly intelligent yet severely limited in their career development. He traced the problem to mistaken habits of thought that good students carry with them from school into the workplace.

He identified five beliefs that hold true in school but not at work. I added a sixth. When I have discussed these six mistaken beliefs with outstanding employees at other major Silicon Valley companies, they have strongly agreed.

#### Belief One: The Real World Should Have Standard Answers

At school, an examination always has standard answers. In the real world, especially in work and money-making situations, there is almost never one uniquely correct answer.

#### Belief Two: Everything Can Be Proven with Certainty

School teaches us to prove whether things are right or wrong. In reality, determining causation is extraordinarily difficult. Even with something as simple as cooking, we wonder, “Why does someone else’s food taste good while mine does not?” Without attentive learning and experimentation, many people may spend a lifetime without finding the real reason. More importantly, only a tiny fraction of the world’s knowledge can be rigorously verified or falsified by science. Most knowledge—especially knowledge about business decisions, strategic choices, and investment judgments—cannot be proved absolutely. Yet the classroom mindset conditions us to seek ironclad proof for every choice, and this becomes an obstacle on the road to success.

#### Belief Three: Textbook Knowledge Is All I Need

School examinations always define their scope, and what they test never strays beyond the textbook. When we solve concrete problems in reality, who defines such a scope? We must draw on every piece of knowledge available. Solving the problem and creating value matter; the degree to which we have formally mastered a particular body of knowledge matters far less.

#### Belief Four: The Harder Something Is, the More Valuable It Must Be

This is an enormous misconception. From childhood, we are trained to tackle hard problems. We assume that solving a hard problem proves that we are impressive, and that being impressive means being valuable. In reality, simpler things are often more valuable. Why? At the technical level, a simple solution is usually more robust and less prone to failure; more scalable and easier to amplify; cheaper; faster to iterate; and easier for others to understand and accept. These qualities form the foundation for magnifying value. We need a sophisticated, cutting-edge technical solution only after every simple one has been tried and failed.

This belief that “difficult means good” is not merely held by individuals. It has seeped into the management and promotion systems of many companies—for example, at a large technology company, earning a promotion may require proving that you can solve complex, difficult problems. This is an unhealthy tendency. You may have to play the game while you are inside it, but you should remain clear about one thing: difficulty does not equal value. Lose that judgment and you may acquire a pile of impressive-looking “fake skills” with no real value. Many employees who appear outstanding and formidable inside big technology companies have no ability to make money once they leave that environment. This mistaken belief is a major reason why.

#### Belief Five: I Only Need to Communicate with People Who Understand My Field

At school, to earn a high score, you need only prove to your teacher and the exam setter that you know how to answer the questions. In reality, if you want your work to have an impact, you must deal with all kinds of people: customers, bosses, colleagues in other departments, and especially people in sales and marketing. You must learn to “sell” your ideas so that others understand and recognize their value. Many people communicate only with those who share their background, then reassure themselves that “these are the only people I need to talk to.” This imposes a severe limit on them. It is also an obstacle to acquiring real skills, understanding the market, and learning how to make money.

#### Belief Six: Someone Will Always Grade Me—and Be Accountable for My Grade

This is the point I added. At school, teachers grade your examination, a system records the score, and rankings may even be publicly posted. In the workplace, no one is assigned to grade you, much less to guarantee the “fairness” of your score. A manager does not study employees as if reading report cards and say, “This one earned 85 points this year and that one 92, so the one with 92 gets promoted.” You are responsible for actively demonstrating your contribution and explaining why you are worth what you receive—or more. You need to influence your boss’s “grade.” The workplace is not an examination hall. No one sets the questions for you, and no one guarantees that their difficulty will fall neatly within your capabilities. You must set your own questions.

### Three Mental Models for Understanding the Real World

If you accept that classroom methods of learning do not apply to the real world, you can move to the next question: How should we understand this complex world? I have summarized three mental models that can help you see it from a different angle.

#### Model One: Making Money Is Fundamentally a Craft

What do I mean by a craft?

First, craft differs from science. Science pursues falsifiability and can provide definite answers under specified conditions. A craft has no standard answer and cannot be absolutely falsified. A great deal of wisdom in life takes the form of “This feels right to me” or “I tried this, and it worked.” Craft cannot be proved in the same way as science. If you insist on pursuing proof to the bitter end, you may miss its real value. In daily work, we can judge whether a piece of programming, a data analysis, or a proposal is good, but such judgments are difficult to falsify. We can develop a feel for quality, but we cannot learn it from a textbook. These are crafts in essence.

Second, craft differs from art. Art pursues subjective expression and does not need a uniform standard. Craft, by contrast, is results-oriented, and its quality can be tested. Good craft produces good results; poor craft produces poor results. For this reason, we can use reality as a mirror and continually refine our craft, because practice gives us a constant stream of feedback.

Finally, mastery of a craft has no endpoint. An outsider watches the spectacle and thinks, “Isn’t that all there is to it? I could learn it in a few days.” An insider sees the technique, and the more deeply they study it, the more problems and depth they discover. Consider boxing. An outsider discusses flashy combinations; an expert may work on nothing more than a simple jab and conclude, “I could practice this my whole life and never make it perfect.” Only when you understand the muscular coordination a simple jab requires and the time it takes to practice do you appreciate how much room you have to improve. That is the charm of a craft: the deeper you study, the stronger you become and the more problems you discover. Mastery is endless, which is why a craft can bring both a sense of achievement and a living.

How, then, do we learn a craft? We need two complementary methods.

One is apprenticeship. A craft depends on experience and feel. It lives in the craftsperson and cannot be fully captured on the page, so guidance from a master matters. A master can tell you where they stumbled and which direction may be better. I recommend *The Soul of a Tree*, which presents a Japanese temple carpenter’s understanding of craft and shows the value of transmission from master to apprentice. The right attitude is essential: you must approach a craft as an apprentice, not immediately ask how to monetize it as quickly as possible. That impatience prevents you from learning anything real.

The other method is continual practice. Through practice, you experience the endless nature of mastery and gain an inexhaustible motivation to keep training. Practice alone advances a craft. As your skill improves, it naturally attracts more resources and opportunities. You then assume greater responsibilities, gain more practice, and form a virtuous cycle.

If you want to know how to make money, therefore, you need a craftsperson’s mindset. When you encounter a problem, your first response should not be, “Where can I find a paper to read?” It should be, “Which master should I ask, and how can I understand this through practice?”

The idea that true knowledge comes from practice is elementary. We all know that no one learns to swim without entering the water. Yet when it comes to learning how to work and make money, many people still fantasize that books alone will teach them.

Combining the craftsperson’s mindset with our critique of classroom learning requires us to shift the center of gravity in how we learn. Learning consists of three activities: action, reflection, and acquiring knowledge. At school, we place excessive weight on acquiring knowledge and often do too little thinking—memorizing problem-solving routines by rote instead. In the real world, I believe the ratio of action to reflection to acquiring knowledge should be at least 6:3:1. Let action lead reflection, and reflection lead the search for knowledge. Only when action and reflection meet a bottleneck should we study specific knowledge to overcome it. The sea of learning has no shore, and knowledge is endless. Much knowledge also depends on context: what is right here may be wrong there. Act first and reflect afterward; only then can you develop judgment about what to learn and how to use it.

#### Model Two: The Real Treasure Is Hidden in the Fog

*Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned* reveals a counterintuitive truth: the overwhelming majority of technological breakthroughs and product innovations were not produced by following a step-by-step plan. They emerged unexpectedly through continual exploration.

One example in the book is especially persuasive. Before the Wright brothers invented the airplane, everyone tried to build one by imitating birds. They studied the structure of feathers and the motion of flapping wings, hoping to copy flight in nature, and every attempt failed. The Wright brothers had repaired bicycles. Inspiration from the bicycle’s transmission mechanism helped them invent the propulsion system of the modern airplane.

Imagine a map on which the territory you can see clearly is only a small fraction of the whole. The real treasure is usually concealed in the unexplored fog, because other people have already mined the known territory clean. Your task is to explore bravely. Exploration does not mean charging around blindly, however. You need at least two abilities:

(1) Perception. When you discover the faintest clue, you can recognize, “Oh? There may be something here.”

(2) The ability to learn and review. You can use the clue to adjust your direction: “I should head east, not north.”

For someone who possesses both abilities and adds the initiative to explore, finding the treasure in their own life is only a matter of time. Who you are today was shaped by countless choices made by your past self. The future contains infinite possibilities, but you must define them through repeated exploration and choice.

Remember Little S from Chapter 1? Today, you and Little S stand at the same starting line. If, whenever a choice arises, stronger powers of understanding lead Little S to make a better decision than yours, then given time Little S may reach heights far beyond you. We study these mental models so that at every decision point, we can choose what serves us best and shape a more valuable future through our own understanding and effort. This is a dynamic process. Today’s choice determines tomorrow’s starting point. Do not remain entangled in the past; cherish every opportunity to choose in the present.

#### Model Three: The World Is Complex and Things Are Multidimensional

At school, we tend to simplify many questions and view them along a single dimension. To decide whether someone is outstanding, for example, we look only at their scores and ranking. Back in the real world, however, most things have many dimensions, and the world is complex. The statement sounds ordinary, but most people do not appreciate how complex the world really is. As a result, they repeatedly try to plan major matters, such as their own careers or their children’s lives.

After leaving school and entering the real world, we readily simplify problems with one-dimensional or otherwise reductive patterns of thought. This is a serious mistake. The markets and working environments we face are inherently complex, noisy, and uncertain. Only when we sincerely respect the complexity and multidimensional nature of things will we stop fantasizing that we can “prove with certainty” that a choice must be right or wrong. In such a complex world, everyone interprets things through personal biases and an individual point of view. Anyone’s answer may be wrong.

Only by assuming that our own answer may be wrong do we create room to improve.

### How Can You Develop a Discerning Eye amid a Flood of Information?

You now know the mistaken beliefs of the past and have mental models for understanding the real world. This brings us to the next crucial question: in an age of information overload, how do we identify genuinely valuable information and use it to shape our mental models and deepen our understanding of the world?

The heart of the problem is judgment. There is no shortcut to better judgment; it must be tempered through experience. Later chapters on work and making money will explain this through concrete cases. For now, I will give you a formula for distinguishing good information from bad and knowing what kind of information to pursue.

Finding valuable information requires at least three layers of filtering.

First, separate signal from noise. In physics, a signal carries useful information that can build models, guide action, and predict trends. Noise is interference containing no useful information. How many times have you heard phrases such as “the future is already here” on social media? Supposedly “disruptive” innovations are announced every day. Which are genuine signals, and which are merely noise? Why is social media so fond of manufacturing noise? Because most people lack the ability to distinguish signal from noise. Noise therefore works: it can attract traffic and make money.

Second, separate important signals from signals in general. A signal may be genuine but irrelevant to your present objective and circumstances.

Finally, among important signals, identify those with a high probability of mattering. Finding an important signal is not enough; you must also consider its likelihood. Be wary of the popular online phrase, “Is there a possibility that…?” If you ask whether something is possible, the answer is always yes. Could the universe explode tomorrow? Yes. Is it likely? The probability is infinitesimal. Deliberately evaluating the probability of an event makes our thinking far more rigorous. Suppose you ask me, “Teacher, is it possible that I will be promoted and get a raise next year?” I can only say, “It is possible.” But if you ask, “How likely is it?” our conversation becomes meaningful. We can analyze your specific circumstances, assess the likelihood, and perhaps identify the key factors affecting it so that you can direct your effort accordingly.

Truly valuable information is a signal—and an important signal with a sufficiently high probability. Thus:

**Valuable information = signal + importance + high probability**

### Shortcuts and the Proper Path to Learning

How can we find valuable information more quickly and thereby sharpen our judgment? I offer two shortcuts and one difficult but correct path.

#### Shortcut One: Learn from People Who Have Produced Results

Why do I dare write this book? Because I learned from many people who had produced results, put their lessons into practice, and produced results myself. With only four years of experience, I joined Tencent, one of China’s leading internet companies, directly as an associate director. I grew a team from six people to thirty and received the rare distinction of two consecutive five-star performance ratings. I attribute much of this to my emphasis, while building my media platform, on learning from people with concrete achievements in many fields. I continually upgraded my thinking, applied what I learned, and achieved results. Had I tried to discover all their lessons on my own, several additional years might still not have been enough.

In everyday life, a shortcut for deciding whom to learn from is to use “specific achievements” as a filter. Someone may speak magnificently and reason with perfect internal logic, yet their entire system of language may be no more than a stack of textbook knowledge. Until your own judgment is sufficiently developed, it is hard to distinguish truth from falsehood within it.

Importantly, “results” does not vaguely mean “made money.” It means achieving something in the particular field you care about. I earned promotions and raises at major technology companies, so I can share workplace experience. I made money through a side business, so I can share what I learned about side businesses. But if I said, “I was a director at a major technology company and made money, so I can teach you how to dress,” that would plainly be unreliable.

Nor is every accomplished person impressive, or every person without visible achievements unimpressive. We cannot judge a human being solely by results. And to evaluate another person well, your own level of understanding must exceed theirs.

#### Shortcut Two: Learn from Your Boss

This is an extremely practical shortcut—almost a magical one. There are two reasons to learn from your boss.

First, your boss is probably more capable than you in the workplace. Whether or not you accept it, in the overwhelming majority of cases, anyone who occupies that position has strengths you do not and something worth learning. You may see many of your boss’s shortcomings and, for a time, none of their strengths. Be especially careful at that point: the strength you cannot see may be precisely their core capability.

Second, the act of learning from your boss is itself a major positive. When you sincerely seek your boss’s advice and learn from them, the subtext is, “I recognize and respect you.” Everyone likes recognition and respect. When your boss receives that signal, they will naturally appreciate you more and be more willing to offer opportunities. You could call it an expressway to resources. A ready-made role model stands before you, and that role model also happens to influence your “fate”—your promotions and raises. Why not take advantage of it?

#### The Difficult but Correct Path

The difficult but correct path is to build a model of the world and apply the scientific method.

This is the most fundamental and reliable route, but also the hardest and longest. You must construct your own model of the world: a framework for understanding how the world operates. You must also master and apply scientific methods to continually iterate and improve that model.

This route includes not only scientific knowledge, but scientific patterns of thought and methods of verification. I recommend *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance*, which explores the scientific method deeply. We will return to it in detail in later chapters.

This chapter has been somewhat long and information-dense. That is because how we learn matters enormously, and we carry too many deeply rooted misconceptions that must first be recognized and then changed. I hope the chapter has helped you establish this foundation. Its central ideas can be summarized as follows:

(1) Beware the traps of the classroom mindset.

(2) Use three mental models—a craftsperson’s mindset, treasure hunting in the fog, and a multidimensional perspective—to understand the real world.

(3) Learn to filter valuable information from the flood and temper your judgment.

(4) Use shortcuts wisely, but also walk the proper path.

After finishing the book, I suggest returning to this chapter and reviewing it several times. Its ideas form the foundation for the cognitive upgrades that follow. The next three chapters focus on correcting three specific mistaken beliefs that affect how we work and make money.

## Chapter 3: Beware the Object Mindset and Learn to Take Responsibility for Yourself

### From “It Is All the Outside World’s Fault” to “I Can Take Control”

Welcome to the beginning of our journey to break the ice around our thinking. Over the next three chapters, we will smash three mental boulders that limit the ceiling on our lives.

The first is the object mindset. You may wonder whether something as abstract as a mindset can really limit the ceiling on your life. Let me begin with a few revealing examples.

Figure 3-1 is a drawing of my seven-month-old baby crawling on the floor. The world is fresh and unfamiliar to him. He is used to moving freely across the carpet in my room, but at its edge, faced with the cold floor beyond, he hesitates. He looks from side to side and cannot bring himself to cross. He mastered crawling some time ago and is perfectly capable of moving across the floor. But a thought forms in his mind: carpet and floor are different; the floor may not be safe. That thought brings him to a halt. This is a classic example of an idea constraining action.

![img](./assets/CB_3300200542_5WjANM6TL3HC6XV3oG6bV6TQ30j3LI6bd3jx3LH30c3YT3k73Tc3ow6XY3LI0Ub5PZb595c.jpg)

Figure 3-1: A baby crawling on the carpet

Now think of the older people around us. There are always uncles, aunts, and grandparents who simply cannot learn to use a smartphone. Is a smartphone really so difficult? Obviously not. If you actually try to teach them, you discover that the obstacle is never the complexity of the operations, but their inner resistance to something new.

“Oh, this is too hard. I’ll never understand it. Forget it—I don’t want to learn.”

Once the mind concludes, “I cannot learn this,” every minor difficulty becomes a perfect excuse to give up.

The problem seems obvious when we look at children or older people. When it comes to ourselves, however, we often have a blind spot. Have the people around us—and have we ourselves—not also been bound hand and foot by our own thoughts?

· “Should I start creating content?”—We can find ten thousand reasons not to.

· “Should I learn English?”—We can list ten thousand difficulties, even though hundreds of millions of people around the world are learning it, proving that anyone can do so with enough time.

· “Should I work out?”—Our body is fully capable, but we simply do not want to go.

Often, the true obstacle to accomplishing something is not an iron wall in the outside world, but our own pattern of thought.

The external environment certainly matters: the macroeconomy, industry trends, earlier choices, family background, and much more all affect us. The crucial point is that we cannot control the external environment. The only thing we can change is our own thinking. Changing that thinking, acquiring real skills, changing our actions, and thereby producing different results in the future is the only way to change our fate.

Recognizing this invisible ceiling and developing the drive to break it marks the dividing line between the object mindset and the agent mindset that I will discuss next.

### Who Are You—an Object or an Agent?

We can understand the two mindsets as follows:

· Object mindset: the outside world should be responsible for me.

· Agent mindset: I must be responsible for myself.

The table may appear abstract at first, so let me explain it through specific situations.

If the object mindset dominates you, your response to an event may be: “What did they do to me?” “What should they have done for me?” or “Why did they treat me this way?” You place yourself in the position of a passive recipient. Your focus is on other people and what happened to you.

If the agent mindset dominates you, your response may be: “What choices do I have?” “What should I do?” or “What can I learn from this?” You always see yourself as the protagonist who acts.

Eight or nine times out of ten, life does not go as we wish. Everyone has periods when things go badly.

· Are you satisfied with your career prospects?

“It is all because the economy is terrible.”

“The company’s system is the problem.”

“My boss is unfair and plays favorites.”

“I am not one of the boss’s insiders.”

“That colleague got promoted quickly only because they know how to flatter people.”

…

If any of these thoughts has crossed your mind, you may be trapped in the object mindset without realizing it.

Why is the object mindset wrong? Because it is both ineffective and contrary to a fundamental fact of life.

That fact is this: you are the sole and ultimate bearer of responsibility for your life. Once you enter this world, other people may offer advice or share the pressure, but no one can bear the consequences of your choices for you. Your success belongs to you, and so does your failure. In the final accounting, only you can settle the bill.

How, then, do we acquire the object mindset as a habit?

First, the family becomes a greenhouse around our development. In parts of East Asia especially, parents often take over everything. From childhood onward, they arrange their children’s lives, make choices for them, and even absorb some of the consequences. In such an environment, children neither bear the consequences of their actions nor possess the right to choose—from what to eat at a meal to how to spend time outside school. Whenever parents make a choice “for your own good” while disregarding a child’s boundaries and agency, they cultivate the object mindset.

Second, school becomes a closed simulator. School is a relatively enclosed and fair environment. It arranges your daily schedule, gives you a fixed and explicit system of evaluation, and carries an implicit promise: “Follow the rules and you will be treated fairly.” We come to assume that the world ought to be equally orderly, transparent, and fair, and that all we need to do is follow the rules.

But everyone eventually leaves home and school. The real world contains far fewer “shoulds,” and its rules are more complex and changeable. You must make your own choices and bear one hundred percent of the results.

Wisdom from ancient and modern traditions, in both East and West, confirms this principle. Wang Yangming’s School of Mind in the Ming dynasty taught that “the mind is principle,” urged us to “realize innate knowledge,” and advocated the unity of knowledge and action. Ancient Greek Stoicism emphasized mastery of the inner self. Sartre’s existentialism was more direct still: human beings are condemned to be free. Even if you wish to abandon your freedom, you have “chosen” to abandon it.

The conclusion is harsh, but also liberating: you cannot escape this responsibility. Even if you choose to “lie flat” and push responsibility onto others, you remain the person who must live with the final result. Rather than complain and wait, grow up sooner. Take back the initiative in your life and shoulder the responsibility that was yours all along.

Imagine a catastrophic flood. A child is stranded alone on a roof, eyes full of helplessness, waiting for a rescue that may or may not come.

Something of that child lives inside each of us. In certain areas, we feel powerless and long to be rescued. In reality, the child on the roof may be found by a rescue team; the child inside us often will not. Perhaps we never truly send a distress signal. Or perhaps, earlier, when the water reached only our ankles, we still had many options for saving ourselves or evacuating. Trapped in the object mindset of waiting, depending, and demanding, we missed the opportunity. Our choices narrowed until we forced ourselves into that pitiful and helpless position.

If you do not want to discover only in extremity that no rescue team is coming, then at the first sign of challenge or difficulty, separate the environment—your external constraints—from yourself, the agent who can act. Acknowledge the environmental limits, focus on what is controllable, and ask: “Under these conditions, what can I do?”

### Practical Exercise: Reexamine Your Difficulties through the Agent Mindset

Let us return to reality and use sharper questions to see how someone guided by an agent mindset would answer. This time, Little S—who has internalized that mindset—will respond. I suggest pausing first to consider your own answer before reading Little S’s.

#### Who Determines Whether You Get a Promotion and Raise?

· Someone guided by the object mindset says: “My boss, the company’s system, and Human Resources decide. If I perform well, they *should* promote me and give me a raise.”

· Little S, guided by the agent mindset, says: “I do. Promotions and raises are tools companies use to motivate employees and achieve objectives; they are instruments of management for the boss. They appear fair because fairness serves the interests of the company and the boss. In this ‘game,’ I should treat the company and the boss as resources.”

In Little S’s view, the responsibility is to choose valuable work proactively; state the desire for promotion and higher pay clearly; communicate with the boss to understand the gap between current performance and the goal; close the gap as both sides understand it; and go beyond extracting a verbal promise by convincing the boss at a deep level that granting the promotion will greatly benefit the boss and the team.

You may now think that Little S is neglecting the proper job—focusing on promotion and pay rather than on completing the work. That, too, is the object mindset at work. Your purpose in taking a job is to advance and earn more. Completing work and receiving favorable evaluations are the means by which you obtain those results. If you still believe, “Just do good work and whatever I deserve will come,” reread this chapter.

When the object mindset has dominated us for a long time, the “muscles” responsible for action atrophy. You may look at Little S’s actions and think, “I cannot do that.” That is all right. We do not demand that someone with atrophied muscles run a marathon the next day. They can begin with simple rehabilitation exercises.

#### Who Should Give Your Work a Complete and Fair Evaluation?

· Someone guided by the object mindset says: “My boss, company, and organization. They are obligated to evaluate me fairly.”

· Little S says: “I and the market should. My boss’s evaluation matters, but it serves management objectives. The fullest assessment comes from my own continuing reflection and from the value and feedback my work ultimately produces in the ‘market,’ whether inside or outside the company. I need to manage my boss’s expectations proactively and actively demonstrate my value.”

#### Why Is There a Midcareer Crisis at Thirty-Five?

· Someone guided by the object mindset says: “Society is at fault. It is age discrimination. Bosses are terrible!”

· Little S says: “The crisis at thirty-five—or, more precisely, the crisis in one’s value for money as a professional—is an objective reality and part of the laws of business. If, by a certain age, your experience, capabilities, and output offer no advantage over younger workers, and you may even deliver worse value for money, the likelihood of being laid off rises. A company would violate the laws of business if it deliberately chose less cost-effective employees. Once I understand this, I act. First, I actively choose and accumulate experience and skills that become more valuable with age, while avoiding simple repetitive labor. Second, while I still have choices, I plan early and change environments proactively rather than waiting until the flood reaches my neck and no path remains. Finally, I ask whether I have explored other skills, interests, or ways to earn a living outside my job. In short, if I sense that my career has an expiration date, the best time to act is today—not when I am almost thirty-five.”

#### Is It Fair When People Who Did Not Study Hard Become Rich?

· Someone guided by the object mindset says: “It is unfair! I work so hard and I am so intelligent. Why should someone inferior to me make a fortune?”

· Little S says: “It is fair, because the rule of the real world has never been ‘high test scores = great wealth.’ The world is more like a place filled with all kinds of opportunities. Whoever sees and captures them can profit. Being good at school is one route to certain opportunities, not the only route. I need to strengthen my ability to see and seize opportunities, rather than assume that opportunity is obligated to favor me.”

#### Does an “Inner-Circle Culture” Make Sense?

· Someone guided by the object mindset says: “No! It is nepotism and favoritism—disgusting. They were promoted quickly only because they belong to the boss’s inner circle. I do not, so nothing I do matters.”

· Little S says: “Being in the ‘inner circle’ is a result, not a cause.”

Inner-circle culture frustrates many people in the workplace, so let me say more. I personally went from an outside hire to someone others saw as “the boss’s trusted lieutenant.” At first, my boss recognized my ability, then my character, and finally the fit between us. That fit included my ability to create value for the boss consistently, to think and solve problems genuinely from his point of view, and both my capability and willingness to tackle difficult problems. As I gradually proved my value, the boss became willing to discuss more matters with me and give me more resources. My confidence grew, and the same words carried more weight when I spoke them. Through this process, I gradually became what others called an insider.

Two key ideas will help you understand this culture better.

First, learn to take another person’s perspective. Put yourself in the boss’s position: What kind of person would you choose to keep close? The answer is simple—someone you like or someone extremely useful to you, and ideally both. No sensible manager will rely heavily on a person merely because that person bears the label “insider.” If the person contributes no practical value and the manager does not like them, the supposed inner-circle status ceases to exist. This is why being an insider is a result, not a cause.

Second, beware the trap of self-deception. Many people habitually invoke “inner-circle culture” to excuse their workplace disappointments. At heart, this is an evasion of responsibility. When you say, “I have no opportunity because that person is an insider,” you are actually inventing a reason not to make an effort. More dangerously, your subconscious may generate a distorted feeling of superiority: “I have integrity and refuse to play those relationship games. That is the only reason things turned out this way.”

This is Ah Q’s method of psychological victory at work: outwardly claiming, “It is not that I cannot; I merely refuse,” while in reality avoiding the facts. If you investigate those supposed insiders carefully, you often find that their status was not innate. They earned it step by step by demonstrating value and building a manager’s trust.

### Beware Explanations Based on “Sides”: They Are Poisoned Chicken Soup from Social Media

Perhaps you accept the explanation above. Perhaps you remain unconvinced and think my “position” is flawed—that I am defending bad people. If you fall into the second group, I need to sound an alarm.

All of us spend large amounts of time consuming enormous quantities of information from social media. You must remain alert, because much of that content continually reinforces the object mindset.

Why? Because social media is fundamentally a business. It needs traffic and your approval; it wants you to become a loyal follower.

What is the shortcut to winning approval? Group identity. And what is the fastest way to establish that identity? Give you excuses. Attribute every disappointment in your life to external forces.

You have surely encountered claims like these: “You are unsuccessful because your boss is heartless / the system is unfair / someone is plotting behind your back / mysterious forces control everything.” This is the object mindset in its classic form. A content creator who speaks this way is subtly completing three moves.

First, they relieve your anxiety, at least temporarily. Hearing “It is not your fault” feels light, as though a burden has been lifted. Second, they create a shared identity: “We are all victims, but we refuse to become like them. We are the ones who see clearly.” Such language makes you feel that you have found your people. Third, they cultivate dependence. After prolonged immersion in this content, you develop a psychological habit. You begin to need the creator to keep interpreting the “truth,” confirming things for you, and supplying emotional comfort. Gradually, you cannot do without it.

Once you accept the creator’s excuse, you resonate deeply with them. From that point on, you are more likely to accept every opinion wholesale, because questioning the creator would mean questioning your own judgment and identity.

But see the situation clearly: the creator may be doing this for business—to win your approval and loyalty, and to turn your attention into traffic and revenue.

Whenever you encounter an interpretation that blames your difficulties entirely on outside forces, place a large question mark beside it: “Is that really the whole story? Why are they saying this? What is their incentive?”

### Action Guide: Take the Helm of Your Life

Once we understand these ideas and see the truth, we must put them into action promptly. I offer two maxims.

#### Take Responsibility for Yourself

Remember the word *for*. The external environment is a constraint you cannot change, but under any constraints, you always retain the right to choose and something remains possible. Make choices actively and accept responsibility for their consequences. Only you can take responsibility for your life.

#### Look for Causes in Yourself, Not Faults

This is a “patch” for the first maxim, preventing you from overcorrecting and attacking yourself. If you look for a cause, you say: “I made that choice because my understanding was limited at the time.” This is objective analysis intended to improve your understanding so that you do better next time. If you look for fault, you say: “Why was my understanding so poor? I am such a failure! If only I had been smarter then!” This is self-denial and self-attack. It does nothing for your future development and may make you even more afraid to choose next time.

## Chapter 4: Beware the Fixed Mindset—Unlock the Underlying Code of Success and Release Your Potential

### A Growth Mindset Is the Underlying Code of Success

The previous chapter discussed the shift from an object mindset to an agent mindset. I hope it awakened your sense of agency and gave you motivation to change. This chapter turns to the method of change. The most important step is moving from a fixed mindset to a growth mindset.

What is a growth mindset?

Put simply, it is the belief that ability can continually improve through effort and learning. It is the opposite of a fixed mindset, which treats intelligence and talent as innate and unchangeable. Someone with a growth mindset believes that with the right methods and sustained effort, anyone can become stronger in any field. Its core is a shift of attention from “Can I do it?” to “How can I learn to do it?”

The term first appeared in *Mindset* by Stanford psychology professor Carol Dweck. Many leaders in Silicon Valley—including former Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella—as well as prominent scholars strongly advocate it. Why does it matter so much? In my view, there are two main reasons.

First, it has extraordinary explanatory power.

Many supposed ingredients of success turn out to be optional. Say diligence is essential, and someone powerful seems to succeed while barely lifting a finger. Say talent matters most, and still more successful people prove to have fought their way forward through acquired effort. The growth mindset is different. You can find it in almost every successful person. Conversely, it is difficult to identify a leading figure who succeeded because of a fixed mindset. A growth mindset is therefore a common and necessary condition for success.

Second, it is simple to learn and produces immediate effects.

Unlike building muscle, it does not require years of daily accumulation. Ten minutes can be enough. Once you finish this chapter and understand the growth mindset, you can apply it anywhere in the life that follows.

In reality, each of us has a mixed mindset: growth-oriented in some areas, fixed in others, and usually an interweaving of both. But our answer to “Can intelligence change?” largely determines the underlying character of our thinking.

· If you choose B and believe intelligence can be developed, the foundation of your thinking is growth-oriented.  
· If you choose A and believe intelligence is determined at birth, its foundation is fixed.

The first step toward a growth mindset, then, is to believe at a fundamental level that intelligence and other abilities can change.

### Why Can Abilities Be Developed?

Chapter 2 explained that making money is fundamentally a craft. In fact, most abilities we need in life are crafts at heart. In *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise*, the authors describe an educational experiment in which every child in a class developed absolute pitch. An ability that appeared to be a gift could, in fact, be cultivated.

Mastering a craft depends on three key factors:

· Interest: Are you sufficiently interested in it?

· Desire: Do you genuinely want to master it?

· Effort: How much time and energy will you invest, and how many difficulties will you overcome?

When all three are present, sustained effort can develop the overwhelming majority of your abilities. The belief that effort can cultivate ability is the core conviction of a growth mindset.

Why, then, does most people’s thinking display fixed characteristics? At school, we grow accustomed to examinations and grading. We readily connect ability with static measures such as scores and rankings, then assume that “good students” and “poor students” are fixed categories.

More fundamentally, examinations use a subtractive logic: you begin with full marks and lose points for mistakes. Work and making money in real life use an additive logic: you begin at zero and gain points whenever you do something right.

When you believe stress can improve performance and use it to strengthen rather than crush you, life forms a virtuous cycle: a challenge produces pressure; you respond constructively and harness it; your performance and ability improve; and you become more eager for the next challenge. Through this cycle, stress supplies continuing motivation instead of defeat.

### Practical Exercise: A New Project Arrives—What Do You and Little S Choose?

Let us move from theory to a case that reveals the advantages of a growth mindset.

Suppose your colleague Little S has a growth mindset, while you are a textbook example of a fixed mindset. Your team receives a new project. It is challenging and risky, with many unresolved problems. Senior leadership is watching closely, and several veteran colleagues sit on the project team.

Faced with it, you may fall into anxiety. The challenge and risk are too great; with so many hard problems, the project cannot possibly succeed. What you cannot do now, you will never learn. Leadership’s attention creates enormous pressure. Failure would be humiliating, and leaders this invested surely will not tolerate it. With senior colleagues present, they will take the credit if things go well and leave you holding the blame if they do not. You cannot win.

Your conclusion: this project is a trap. Do not touch it.

Little S thinks very differently. A large challenge means many opportunities to learn. These hard problems will force Little S to master things not yet known and upgrade existing skills. Veteran colleagues are precious resources who can offer advice and teach a great deal. Leadership’s attention proves that the project matters and resources will be available; if difficulties arise, leaders will provide support. Risk is normal in a new project. If success were guaranteed and anyone could do it, where would be the meaning? Overcoming the difficulty and succeeding will create a true sense of achievement.

Little S’s conclusion: this project is wonderful. I must seize the opportunity.

Who is right? In fact, both of you are. That is the remarkable thing about mindsets: fixed and growth mindsets can each become self-fulfilling prophecies. A fixed-minded person who receives the project will probably ruin it; a growth-minded person will probably do it well. Different mindsets influence everything—what you notice, what you do, how you communicate, and how you report progress. Those different actions ultimately produce different results. If you doubt this, let us continue.

Suppose you reluctantly accept the project after all. When problems arise, panic sets in. Your first reaction is to conceal them or shift responsibility. When reporting to leadership, you share good news and hide bad news, or work to distance yourself from blame.

Little S remains steady. Problems are perfectly normal in a new project. Little S first considers whether they can solve the problem alone or ask colleagues to solve it together. If neither works and additional resources or direction are needed, Little S reports the problem and progress honestly to leadership and discusses a response.

Think from the leader’s perspective. On this critical project, whose conduct would you rather see: someone who conceals problems, passes the buck, and fails to move proactively, or someone who faces problems, actively seeks solutions, and communicates candidly? The answer is obvious. Whether competing for opportunity or earning trust and responsibility, the growth-minded person wins decisively.

### Three “Incantations” for Cultivating a Growth Mindset

We can now see clearly that a fixed mindset is not merely useless; it is a major source of resistance to growth. A growth mindset, whatever the environment, is the underlying capability most worth relying on.

Let us summarize the changes it can bring:

· Continuous growth and stronger abilities: you continually refine and surpass yourself, as if lighting up branches on a skill tree.

· More choices and better opportunities: as your abilities strengthen, your world expands. Opportunities appear naturally, whether for an internal promotion or a move to a better platform.

· Greater resilience and psychological flexibility: you no longer fear challenges, but see each problem as a stairway upward. Your mind is steadier and your capacity for pressure stronger.

· Better relationships: you accept feedback from others with greater ease and feel sincere happiness at their success. Other people can sense this open, positive presence and enjoy being near it.

· A stronger inner sense of control and enduring happiness: external evaluations no longer pull you around. You find satisfaction in growth itself. This also expresses the agent mindset from the previous chapter: you focus your energy on what you can genuinely control—effort, learning, and growth—and courageously take responsibility for your choices. Growth happens to be one of the things most firmly within our control.

Finally, here are three sentences to write down, place somewhere visible, or repeat inwardly to help internalize the growth mindset.

(1) Focus on what you put in, not how you performed—especially when you performed well.

When you achieve something—mastering a new skill or winning a promotion, for example—review the effort you invested. This reinforces the brain’s recognition that effort leads to good results. It teaches the brain to reward effort itself and makes you subconsciously more inclined to invest it. This is essential to shaping a growth mindset over the long term.

(2) When you face a challenge, ask: “What can I learn from this?”

Meritocracy is the dominant value system guiding our growth and learning. Under its influence, setbacks easily send us into endless inner conflict and catastrophic chains of thought. One poor examination may lead to weak scores on entrance tests, or a low GPA at graduation, which may mean no good job—and perhaps, we imagine, an entire life ruined. If this describes you, become aware of the pattern. Whenever a setback or challenge appears, ask, “What can I learn from this?” Bring your attention back to your own growth.

(3) Remind yourself: science shows that moderate stress can improve performance.

Remember that stress itself can become an ally. This sentence can adjust your state of mind and convert pressure into drive. Of course, if stress becomes excessive and exceeds your capacity, seek help promptly; never simply grit your teeth and endure. Under moderate pressure, however, this sentence is a powerful tonic.

## Chapter 5: Beware the Scoring Mindset—Embrace the World’s Diversity and Complexity

### The Harm Caused by the Scoring Mindset

This chapter addresses the third pattern of thought that demands caution: the scoring mindset.

Those of us raised in an examination-driven education system know scores intimately. They become almost the sole ruler for measuring a person’s worth. In high school, I often heard, “One extra point puts you ahead of an entire playground full of people.” I once believed that my score on the national university entrance examination determined which university I could attend, which in turn determined my career prospects and even my prospects in life. From an early age, many schools instill the idea that a high score equals high human value. Reinforced by a cultural tradition proclaiming that every pursuit is inferior to scholarship, this logic sinks into the bones of many people.

After leaving school, we learn that scores themselves are limited. Yet the scoring mindset remains quietly embedded in our minds. It leads us to understand the world partially and incorrectly: we stare only at what can be quantified and reduce a complex world to something deceptively simple. It drives an excessive pursuit of certainty, making us reach for what does not change while forgetting that change itself is the only constant. And in a world where scores alone matter, genuine happiness becomes difficult to feel.

More specifically, the scoring mindset causes three kinds of harm.

#### Harm One: The World Becomes Nothing but Numbers and Labels

The scoring mindset directs attention only to things that are visible, tangible, and measurable, dramatically oversimplifying reality. Let me make this concrete through mathematical modeling.

Suppose you want to score a choice—a job, a city, or a romantic relationship. Let *f*(*x*) represent the total score. The choice is certainly not one-dimensional; many factors affect it, which we can denote as *x*1, *x*2, *x*3, and so on.

A mathematical model of the scoring mechanism might look simple:

*f*(*x*) = *A*1*x*1 + *A*2*x*2 + *A*3*x*3 + …

Here, *A*1, *A*2, *A*3, and so on are weights, while *x*1, *x*2, *x*3, and so on are component scores. The expression is a simple weighted sum.

In choosing a partner, for example, you might divide your requirements into height, weight, income, education, personality, values, and more. You might think: good education, plus one point; tall enough, plus one point—and add everything together with the same crude simplicity.

The real world is nothing like this. In life, the “formula” for evaluating a partner is extraordinarily complex. *f*(*x*) may look more like:

*A*1 × (*x*1)² + *A*2 × *x*2 + *A*3 × (*x*1 × *x*3) + …

The factors may have squared relationships, where one strength stands out dramatically; exponential relationships, where one weakness is devastating; relationships that rise and then fall, where too much of a quality becomes harmful; and interactions among multiple factors. A person who is highly educated and wealthy may possess appeal far beyond the simple sum of the two. Humor combined with beauty may increase attraction geometrically.

Your brain cannot process this real and complicated “formula.” In the end, all you can remember and use is the primitive additive model.

Even if you could describe every relationship among all factors, a fatal problem would remain: you would focus on quantifiable “hard indicators” while overlooking “soft strengths” that cannot be measured but may matter more.

Consider romance again. Your scorecard may eventually contain only questions such as: How tall are they? How many digits are in their income? How many homes do they own? What is their education? Yet in a long-term relationship, the “soft” factors are often what truly matters:

· Are your personalities compatible?

· Does communication flow?

· Do they possess “relationship intelligence”?

Relationship intelligence is the ability to understand, manage, and maintain a relationship while creating a good experience within it. It includes knowing how to balance suggestion with directness and sincerity with a touch of mystery. How do you score these things? Because they cannot be scored, they are readily ignored.

Work is the same. To judge whether someone is impressive, we first look at education, certificates, and past performance—the hard capabilities. Yet the “soft strengths” of communication, emotional intelligence, solving complex problems, and tolerating pressure often determine how far and how quickly a person advances.

Under the influence of the scoring mindset, you may not see other people’s soft strengths at all. Even when you do, you may dismiss them because they cannot be quantified. Once you realize that the things around you should not be viewed through scores and begin attending to what cannot be measured, however, an entirely different world appears.

In romance, you may discover that someone with ordinary “hard qualifications” is exceptionally attractive. Curiosity leads you to observe where that attraction comes from: perhaps their way of communicating puts others at ease, or they resolve conflict with wisdom. You see and learn more.

At work, you may notice people with neither the strongest technical skills nor the best education who nevertheless get things done and navigate relationships with ease. Once you discard the prejudice of scores, you can learn what is genuinely valuable from them.

Working, making money, and building relationships are often much like learning a craft. The prerequisite for mastering a craft is seeing where you are weak and where others are strong. Only after breaking the scoring mindset can you truly see.

#### Harm Two: Grasping for “Certainty” and Ignoring That Change Is the Only Truth

The rules at school are brutally simple. The examination scope is fixed, question types follow patterns, answers are usually unique, scores determine rankings, and rankings determine destinations. Everything appears certain. Proceed step by step and place first in your grade or city, and an elite university will welcome you. Leave school for the real world, however, and everything changes. You do not know whom or what you will encounter when you leave home today. You do not know who on your team will be promoted or depart this year. You cannot predict the movement of any individual stock, and you may not even guess the mood of the person beside you one second from now.

The world is too complex and chaotic. Uncertainty is its normal condition and essence. Yet long training in the scoring mindset makes us continually grasp for certainty, which sends us down many unnecessary detours.

The pursuit of certainty causes overanalysis and decision paralysis. Faced with an important choice, you try to enumerate every advantage and disadvantage and locate the optimal answer. You may plan the next decade of life while still in high school, only to graduate from university into a world already transformed. Repeated weighing often causes us to miss the opportunity. Look back at the decisions that truly mattered in your life—which city to enter, whom to be with, what work to do, which opportunities to refuse and which to seize. Many were not products of rational analysis. They were driven by intuition or the subconscious, or carried forward by a larger trend.

Resisting change often means missing opportunity. In an article I published in early 2023, “The Five Most Important Questions About ChatGPT,” I accurately predicted many trends in generative AI and offered several views that ran against the consensus at the time but have since been borne out. Even so, I would never claim to see clearly what the ecosystem, applications, and opportunities will look like three years from now. Yet how many parents still plan a supposedly stable route for their children stretching ten or even twenty years? Such is the enormous inertia behind the pursuit of false certainty.

This pursuit not only creates anxiety and internal friction; more importantly, it prevents us from developing the thinking and behavior needed to adapt to an uncertain world. The earlier model that “the real treasure is hidden in the fog” is one effective response to uncertainty.

In short, uncertainty is the nature of the world. We must learn to embrace it instead of fighting it in vain. Certainty is often an illusion. When we stop pursuing it desperately, we can move through life with greater composure.

#### Harm Three: “Scores” Erase the Individual and Put Happiness Out of Reach

Why does the scoring mindset keep us perpetually unhappy? The answer involves the distributions found in the world. Most phenomena in life fall into two broad categories: normal distributions and long-tail distributions (Figure 5-1).

Most physical traits, including height, follow a normal distribution. The majority of people cluster around the average; exceptionally tall and exceptionally short people are rare. I am 1.83 meters tall, which places me fairly high in the distribution, so I feel good about it. But more consequential things in life—wealth, influence, work output—often follow a long-tail distribution.

Figure 5-1: Normal and long-tail distributions

· Wealth: a small number of people may possess thousands, tens of thousands, or even millions of times the wealth of an ordinary person.  
· Influence: the overwhelming majority of content creators remain unknown, while a handful of leading influencers capture most of a platform’s attention.  
· Work output: according to Price’s law—the square-root law of core contributors—the square root of the number of people in an organization (ten out of one hundred, or one hundred out of ten thousand) completes roughly half the work.

Now ask: What happens if you live by a scoring mindset in a world governed by long tails?

Endless comparison and endless defeat.

In a normal distribution, effort can move you from a score of 60 to 80, producing a visible and meaningful shift in relative position. In a long-tail distribution, great effort may still leave you somewhere along the “long, long tail.” Worse, the achievements of people at the head may exceed yours by thousands of times, making any score you earn seem trivial.

Even if you work your way into the top one percent, your friends may also belong to that top one percent. Look upward and the resources possessed by the top one-tenth or one-hundredth of one percent make you feel “desperately poor.”

More brutally still, even if you fight your way into the top one hundred-thousandth, the people entering your field of vision may be billionaires and people worth tens of billions. Beside them, your achievements instantly appear insignificant.

That is the terrible feature of a long-tail distribution: the farther forward you move, the larger the gap you observe between yourself and others. When you see, “They have 14,500 points and I have 145,” losing your psychological balance becomes almost inevitable.

You have bound personal value tightly to scores awarded by the outside world. When someone else’s score is one hundred times yours, you begin to doubt your worth no matter how much you have achieved or how comfortably you live.

If you build happiness entirely on comparisons with others, you will necessarily possess both an “inappropriate sense of superiority” when comparing downward and an “inappropriate sense of inferiority” when comparing upward. Without a stable inner core, your happiness becomes a reed in the wind, forever swaying.

### Think Precisely; Do Not Generalize Labels

As this chapter closes, I want to emphasize the importance of precision in thought. Many people online teach others to “abandon the student mindset.” In my view, this is imprecise and harmful.

For the formative decade or more of our values, “student” was our principal identity. We certainly learned valuable things during those years, and we also acquired habits requiring caution, including the object, fixed, and scoring mindsets discussed in these chapters. Those habits are harmful and should be replaced with better patterns of thought. But simply declaring “the student mindset is bad” is far too broad. Were those entire years wrong? Was every piece of knowledge and every habit learned at school mistaken? Such a critic does not tell you exactly what is wrong or how to change it. Is the problem the method of learning, the examination system, or something else?

Vague criticism of this kind is unacceptable. I have identified problems caused by examination culture and meritocracy, but I am not opposing examinations or education themselves. I have invested so much effort in isolating each problematic pattern—the object mindset, fixed mindset, and scoring mindset—analyzing its causes and harms, and then offering an antidote because I want to secure the foundation. Only then can improvement work well.

I hope you can generalize from this example. Whenever you encounter a concept or label, think one step further. Do not settle for a broad summary; dig down until you find the actual root of the problem.

## Chapter 6: Reshape Your Thinking and Begin the Road to Financial Freedom

### Integrate the Ideas and See Reality Clearly

We have now completed the first section of the book, covering four central subjects:

(1) The right way to approach learning, working, and making money;

(2) Breaking the object mindset and embracing the agent mindset;

(3) Leaving the fixed mindset for the growth mindset;

(4) Abandoning the scoring mindset and embracing multidimensional thinking.

Let us connect these concepts and revisit the three models for observing the world from Chapter 2.

#### Model One: Making Money Is Fundamentally a Craft

I have said that making money is fundamentally a craft. Let us now examine it from the perspectives of the three flawed mindsets we have just studied: object, fixed, and scoring.

First, object mindset versus agent mindset.

Someone with an agent mindset believes that a craft is something you *seek*, not something you wait to receive. Think about it: what craft can you learn merely by sitting still while someone guides your hands? Almost none. Anything genuinely useful must be pursued and explored on your own initiative.

I previously recommended *The Soul of a Tree*, which tells the story of Tsunekazu Nishioka, a master temple carpenter. The section titled “Earth” recounts how his apprentice Mitsuo Ogawa learned the craft and demonstrates initiative even more vividly. Ogawa decided in high school to become a temple carpenter, but his path was difficult. At first, he did odd jobs in a Buddhist-altar shop. His classmates earned 80,000 yen a month at Nissan while he received only 3,000. He did not lose heart. He searched tirelessly for teachers and opportunities. If an opportunity would not refine his craft or teach him real skills, he abandoned it decisively and kept searching for the right one. Every ability he acquired was sought out, piece by piece, through an active spirit of inquiry. To master a craft, you first need an active heart. Someone dominated by the object mindset waits in place and therefore remains separated from true craft.

Second, fixed mindset versus growth mindset.

To a growth-minded person, a craft is continually refined through making mistakes and overcoming them. No one avoids errors and dead ends. At the beginning, you will inevitably be clumsy and encounter things you cannot understand. When a growth-minded person makes a mistake, they say, “Excellent—another place where I can improve!” They accept failure calmly, perhaps even with a little excitement, and treat challenge as an opportunity to grow. Setbacks make them braver, and their craft naturally improves. A fixed-minded person, by contrast, easily turns one failure into a total rejection of the self: “I knew I was no good.” To protect a fragile residue of self-esteem, they subconsciously avoid failure and challenge, so their craft stops developing.

Third, scoring mindset versus multidimensional thinking.

To a multidimensional thinker, how could a score possibly measure whether a craft is good? People with a scoring mindset love certainty and quantifiable indicators. But how do you quantify a craft? Nishioka and Ogawa both emphasize that true craft lives in a human being; it does not lie dead in a book. Reading cannot teach you to build a house. Learning from a master carpenter, however, reveals countless techniques and pieces of wisdom. If you do not understand the craft yourself, how can you score a craftsperson? Excessive faith in scores blinds you to things that are genuinely valuable but difficult to quantify: experience, feel, and devotion to workmanship.

#### Model Two: The Real Treasure Is Hidden in the Fog

Someone with an object mindset will not enter the fog to search for treasure. They remain where they are, waiting for someone else to deliver it.

Someone with a fixed mindset circles only within the safe territory already illuminated on the map and dares not enter the unknown fog. Yet the most valuable treasure is usually hidden deep within it. This is why a growth mindset enables greater development: it gives you the courage to explore the unknown, which accords with the real world’s tendency to reward the brave.

Someone with a scoring mindset finds an unknown fog painful. Scoring requires a system, and systems rest on what is known. How can the unknown be scored? Obsess over the question and you will never take the first step into exploration, and therefore never find the true treasure.

#### Model Three: The World Is Complex and Things Are Multidimensional

*The Three-Body Problem* contains a warning worth remembering: weakness and ignorance are not obstacles to survival, but arrogance is.

People who lack experience and depth of thought often share two traits. First, excessive confidence. They assume they have already grasped most of the world’s truths and laws, and habitually use one mental model to explain everything. Second, limited cognitive dimensions. Their understanding may contain three or six dimensions, making it difficult to accept that a matter may actually contain dozens or hundreds. Anyone who cannot even see a thing’s complexity naturally cannot identify the most important and essential dimension among the many.

In this condition, someone with an object mindset may attribute another person’s success and their own failure to a dimension they cannot control, such as family background or talent. They fail to see factors that matter more: strength of will, adversity quotient, initiative, a drive for excellence, daily effort, and the courage to bear risk.

A fixed-minded person fears being wrong and therefore refuses to see other dimensions that might change the conclusion. A growth-minded person remains humble and continually searches for more important dimensions from which to view the same question, deepening their understanding of how the world works.

The scoring mindset compresses a multidimensional world into a one-dimensional number. Most people can use mathematical tools only for problems involving an *x*-axis and a *y*-axis. Add a *z*-axis and a little more complexity, and the equation exceeds their ability to compute. Anyone who insists on assigning a score to everything is therefore refusing to confront the world’s complexity.

I want to emphasize one point: when you truly understand the complexity of the world, and understand that what we call “right” and “wrong” are results of choices made in different coordinate systems, you become sincerely humble. When you imagine yourself entirely correct and cannot see another possibility or angle, your understanding ceases to grow. You cannot grasp the full picture, much less see the key dimension that may truly matter to you.

The agent mindset, growth mindset, and multidimensional thinking ultimately converge on the most precious condition of all: humble, open, and always moving forward.

## Chapter 7: The Road to Financial Freedom—Reshape Your View of Wealth

The first six chapters examined cognition. From this chapter onward, we focus on a practical subject: making money.

Why do so many people find money difficult to earn, or difficult to keep even after they earn it? Because the greatest obstacle is often our entrenched view of money—an unsound understanding of wealth. We grow up with little financial education while absorbing many mistaken ideas. Without a sound view of wealth, earning and preserving money become exceptionally difficult. This chapter explores how to build one.

### What Exactly Is Financial Freedom?

The concept that may concern us most in discussions of wealth is “financial freedom.” A concise formula defines it:

**Passive income > expenses**

In other words, even if you stop working, passive income is sufficient to cover your daily costs.

The key is “passive income”: income that does not require your continuing active labor. Income from employment, however high, is active income because it stops when you stop working. Many highly paid professionals, such as doctors and lawyers, may look rich without being wealthy in the fullest sense. They have not reached financial freedom; once their active labor ends, their cash flow dries up.

Passive income generally comes from two sources. The first is interest or other returns produced by assets: rent, company dividends, bank interest, returns on financial products, and so forth. The second is continuing income produced by a one-time creation: royalties from a book, ongoing sales of a product you developed, or licensing fees from a patent.

The second route seems difficult for most people, so let us begin with the more common first route, again expressed as a formula:

**Passive income ≈ assets × rate of return**

How are assets accumulated?

**Assets ≈ Σ savings**

Savings in this formula accumulate over time. The money a person sets aside each month and year builds up and may appreciate through compounding until it forms a pool of assets. This is a simplified model that temporarily ignores more complicated financial behavior such as using leverage to buy a home.

The source of savings is simpler still:

**Savings = income − expenses**

Subtract daily spending from personal income and save what remains.

Connect these stages and we obtain the complete logical chain leading to financial freedom:

**Σ (income − expenses) × rate of return > expenses**

Note: income minus expenses also accumulates over time.

### Unlocking Financial Freedom: The Key Variables You Can Control

The formula reveals four variables involved in financial freedom: time, expenses, income, and rate of return. Personal agency influences each to a different degree, and they vary in how difficult they are to control.

· Time: relatively easy to control. The earlier you begin saving and investing, the stronger the effects of compounding.

· Expenses: controlling spending is usually more direct and feasible than raising income. Cutting unnecessary costs is essential.

· Income: raising income is comparatively the greatest challenge. It usually requires more working time, stronger professional skills that lead to promotion and higher pay, or additional sources of income.

· Rate of return: the hardest variable to increase, largely because investing is filled with noise and traps. Promotions promising high returns often conceal enormous risks, including peer-to-peer lending failures and illegal fundraising schemes. As the saying goes, “You have your eye on their interest; they may have their eye on your principal.” Finding sound learning channels and investment methods is highly challenging. You must guard against an endless supply of fraud while developing the ability both to recognize and bear risk.

In today’s world, people who can produce higher returns on larger amounts of capital—whether Warren Buffett through investing or Elon Musk through entrepreneurship—stand at the summit of capital. We must also understand clearly that any offer or promise combining low risk, high returns, and no effort is almost certainly a fraud. There are many legitimate roads to take. Let me summarize them.

### Guard Your Wallet: Avoid Irrational Spending

We begin with the relatively accessible task of controlling expenses. Thrift is a traditional Chinese virtue, and China’s national savings rate does rank among the world’s highest. Even so, each of us still wastes a great deal of money in everyday life.

One report, for example, described a university student who accumulated hundreds of thousands of yuan in online debt while at school. The bills contained no major purchase, only repeated small expenses of a dozen or several dozen yuan. In the United States, people speak of the “latte factor”: one latte a day seems inexpensive, but year after year the total becomes astonishing, and compounding makes its effect on long-term wealth still greater.

There is nothing inherently wrong with consumption. We save not only to gain freedom, but also so that we can spend and please ourselves. Yet two subtle psychological forces—status games and consumerism—lead us to spend large amounts without noticing and may provoke impulse purchases that bring emptiness rather than satisfaction. Recognizing both forces matters.

#### Status Games

Many people economize in daily life, then make a sudden impulse purchase to demonstrate a certain “status.” They buy cars or homes beyond their means, middle-class families stretch to send children to extremely expensive private schools, and people purchase designer bags and costly clothing. Often, such consumption is intended to buy an illusory feeling of status. Human beings have no absolute hierarchy of status, but our minds readily construct its illusion.

We mistakenly assume that expensive consumption raises our social position, while the human instinct to feel superior drives us toward that false satisfaction.

#### Consumerism

Consumerism affects us even more deeply. It binds consumption tightly to social status and even to needs such as relieving boredom. Most of the time, when you eat a late-night snack or drink milk tea, are you physically hungry or thirsty—or inwardly empty and bored? When bored, we look for something to fill the time, perhaps playing a game and buying a cosmetic item along the way. Many unnecessary expenses slip quietly into life in this manner.

How can we avoid this unthinking consumption? The most important response is greater awareness.

Consumerism plants desire far more subtly than we imagine. Product promotion for a specific brand is relatively easy to notice, but films and literature often promote entire lifestyles and systems of value. When we imagine prosperity, for example, we picture urban skyscrapers, countless illuminated windows, elegant restaurants, and wineglasses—and long to possess the scene. That longing is not innate; it formed quietly under repeated influence.

In ordinary life, reflect often on the motives behind your purchases. Which seek a hollow sense of face and status? Which were shaped by consumerist ideas? This does not require becoming an ascetic without desire. Everyone has desires, and there is nothing wrong with participating moderately in status games or enjoying material pleasure. But greater awareness helps distinguish genuine inner needs from artificial ones imposed by culture, advertising, and social pressure. Reducing impulse purchases produced by misleading ideas can save substantial money and carry us a large step toward financial freedom.

### Spend Where It Counts—Investing in Yourself Matters Most

Avoiding wasteful spending is relatively easy because most of us have been told since childhood not to squander money. The next mistake is more subtle: refusing to spend where we should. The principal place where we *should* spend is investment in ourselves.

A simple numerical simulation can show the enormous difference such investment produces. Suppose two young people, Little A and Little S, begin from exactly the same position:

· Both earn 10,000 yuan a month.

· Both initially earn a 3 percent return on savings, perhaps through basic bank investment products.

· Both spend 5,000 yuan a month on living expenses.

Their principal difference is as follows.

Little A saves the entire remaining 5,000 yuan and makes no additional investment in personal development. We therefore assume annual income growth of 5 percent, reflecting normal inflation or ordinary salary adjustments.

Little S spends 2,000 yuan each month on self-investment—learning new skills, expanding the boundaries of understanding, building a network—and saves 3,000 yuan. Because of this continuing self-investment, assume Little S’s income grows 15 percent annually. In the process, Little S also learns about finance and investing, gradually raising the investment return until it reaches 7 percent at age forty, a relatively prudent and achievable level.

Both begin at twenty-five, perhaps upon entering work after graduate school. Figure 7-1 shows how their wealth may look at forty, fifteen years later.

Initially, Little A does have slightly more savings because of saving 2,000 yuan more each month. Over time, however, Little S’s income grows faster and the investment return gradually improves. That wealth begins growing exponentially and quickly overtakes Little A’s.

At age forty:

· Little A has accumulated approximately 2 million yuan;

· Little S has accumulated nearly 6 million yuan.

Figure 7-1: Wealth accumulation—conservative saving versus investing in yourself

The difference approaches a factor of three. The number may sound exaggerated, but it is entirely plausible. An ordinary salaried worker who labors diligently for fifteen years may accumulate about 2 million yuan. Another who invests in personal development and advances rapidly may accumulate about 6 million. Neither path is unusual in real life.

More important is the gap in passive income.

Little A: approximately 2 million yuan in wealth at a 3 percent return produces about 60,000 yuan a year in passive income. This is unlikely to cover future living expenses, which generally rise with age. Financial freedom remains distant.

Little S: nearly 6 million yuan at a 7 percent return produces more than 400,000 yuan a year in passive income. This can support a relatively comfortable, secure retirement. By our definition, Little S has already achieved financial freedom at forty.

From the same starting point, different choices can create entirely different paths after fifteen years. If both continue working, Little S’s faster growth in income and wealth only widens the gap.

You may object that the assumptions are too idealized. Let us examine them.

For income growth, Little A’s annual increase of 5 percent takes monthly pay from 10,000 to about 20,000 yuan after fifteen years. Little S’s 15 percent growth takes monthly pay from 10,000 to about 80,000 yuan, or nearly 1 million a year.

There are many people with fifteen years of experience who earn 20,000 yuan a month; those who remain in place may well follow this path. Continued effort can also take an ordinary employee to director level or make them a senior expert in a popular field—an algorithm engineer during the boom years, for example—where annual pay close to 1 million yuan is a real career outcome. The income assumptions are therefore reasonable.

For investment returns, Little A remains at 3 percent, choosing only low-risk products because of limited tolerance for risk. Little S studies and raises the return to 7 percent.

Is 7 percent annually too high? Over the past ten or even twenty years, major market indexes such as the Nasdaq have delivered annualized returns well above that figure. This does not mean random investments can earn 7 percent. Little S achieves it by investing time and energy in financial knowledge, developing a deep awareness of risk, perhaps building relevant relationships, and acquiring a distinctive eye. Investing requires continual study and practice. For a serious learner, however, 7 percent is not beyond reach. The return assumption is also broadly reasonable.

If the simulation shows that spending an additional 2,000 yuan each month on yourself can produce financial freedom fifteen years later, why do most people fail to do it?

The crucial point is that Little S spends money on personal development, which raises income and then improves investment returns.

Two questions deserve careful thought.

Question one: If you merely spend money—buying many courses and books every month, for example—without thinking deeply or acting, can you genuinely learn to work and make money effectively? Clearly not.

Question two: If you think deeply and take practical action without spending money, or spending very little, can you learn to work and make money effectively? Very possibly yes.

Much of the world’s valuable knowledge and many of its resources are free or extremely inexpensive: classic books and advice from experienced people, for example. Many of the insights in this book came from free sources, practice, and my own reflection. Paid learning is comparatively useful for filtering information, saving time, and providing a structured framework.

What truly matters is not how much money you spend on knowledge, but how you learn and what you do.

Recall the book’s earlier points. Working and making money are crafts. We improve them through learning and practice, and the effective order is action first, reflection second, knowledge third.

Learning is a dynamic process of personal practice, deep thought, review, and extracting lessons from experience. Investing a sum of money does not automatically grant the ability to work or make money. There is no one-click button for self-improvement. The only reliable method is to embrace uncertainty, enter practice actively, and keep learning. This requires:

· Thinking deeply;

· Acting bravely;

· Avoiding traps;

· Improving discernment so that you can distinguish reliable information from noise.

Only then can personal income and investment returns improve.

### Beyond the Numbers: Above Financial Freedom Lies True Wealth

I hope the concept of financial freedom is now basically clear. Beginning from a reasonably modest point, setting a realistic goal, and continually investing in yourself—including stronger professional ability and financial knowledge—can make financial freedom possible within a foreseeable period, perhaps by around age forty.

This is only a simplified model, but not an excessively optimistic fantasy. In reality, faster growth or better opportunities may let an individual exceed the model’s expectations and reach the goal still earlier.

Wealth, however, is not the same as money. That is why I call the first level “financial freedom,” not “freedom through wealth.”

What is money? Warren Buffett once offered a wonderful analogy: money is the gasoline in a car. You go to a gas station to fill the tank so that you can drive where you want to go. The gas station is never the destination. What is life’s destination? What constitutes true wealth?

· Freedom of time: the ability to decide how your time is spent.

· Health: a healthy body and mind with which to experience life energetically.

· Relationships: deep and meaningful emotional connections with family, friends, and a partner.

· Experiences: a rich and varied life filled with memorable moments.

· Possibility: the right to choose and the opportunity to explore the unknown.

· Inner peace: when we stop pursuing external approval and find our own intrinsic drive and satisfaction, peace follows. We still work and live seriously, but the motive has changed. We no longer chase a success defined by convention; we genuinely experience and enjoy the present.

· Meaning: when what we do accords with our role, identity, and personal narrative, a sense of meaning naturally arises. Meaning is a powerful force. It helps us endure temporary suffering, gives us something to live toward, and drives many important achievements at the deepest level.

These are the forms of wealth we should truly pursue. Money is a powerful tool and effective lever for obtaining them more efficiently. It can buy time by paying someone else to handle housework and create richer experiences through travel and learning. But if we focus only on money and forget to exchange it for true wealth, money loses its proper meaning.

We must also beware several common psychological traps.

· The illusion of immortality: we know life is finite and that we can carry no material wealth away at the end. Subconsciously, however, we behave as though we will live forever. We accumulate money desperately and forget to experience life, perhaps leaving behind nothing but a number we never had time to use.

· The money game and the status game: many of us mistake making money itself for life’s ultimate objective. We become addicted to the growth of an account balance and forget that earning is only a means, and money only a tool for obtaining true wealth. This is as absurd as treating the gas station as the destination of a journey.

· The comparison trap: however much wealth you possess, someone will always have more. Enter a higher social circle and new objects of comparison appear. If happiness rests on being richer than others, genuine happiness will remain forever impossible.

Money can resolve many troubles, especially those caused by its absence, but it cannot buy genuine and lasting happiness. True well-being comes more from within and from the forms of “true wealth” listed above.

I know that while you are focused on making money, this discussion may sound abstract or like comfortable advice from someone spared your hardship. But these are the questions that run through an entire life and truly matter. I repeat the reminder for you and for myself: while working hard to make money, remember to look up from time to time and ask what life is ultimately for. Otherwise, we may lose ourselves in a game of pursuing numbers.

## Chapter 8: The Investment Trap—a Bottomless Pit for Money, Time, and Life

The previous chapter examined common spending traps, including status games and consumerism. Compared with the damage caused by poor investment decisions, however, those losses often look trivial. For an ordinary person trying to build wealth, bad investments are the greatest potential risk.

Why?

The education and social beliefs we absorb from childhood usually contain a built-in warning system against excessive consumption. When we waste money, we feel at least some guilt and unease. Few ordinary people therefore enter a severe financial crisis through consumption alone.

Investing is entirely different.

Poor investment decisions are extremely common, and their losses can be catastrophic: blindly buying a home at the peak of the market; becoming trapped in stocks bought at a high; or entering a supposed “wealth-management project” and losing every cent. A decision made after a few minutes—or even longer—can cost years or decades of hard work. This chapter therefore examines the most common investment traps.

### The Destructive Power of Loss

A simple arithmetic problem exposes the frightening consequences of losing principal. Suppose there are two two-year investment scenarios.

· Scenario one: in the first year, your investment performs brilliantly and earns an astonishing 90 percent, almost doubling your assets. In the second, the market reverses and you lose 30 percent.

· Scenario two: you follow a steady strategy and earn 20 percent in each year.

Which produces the higher total return after two years? Scenario two:

Scenario one:

Initial capital × (1 + 90%) × (1 − 30%) = initial capital × 1.9 × 0.7 = initial capital × 1.33

Scenario two:

Initial capital × (1 + 20%) × (1 + 20%) = initial capital × 1.2 × 1.2 = initial capital × 1.44

A seemingly modest 30 percent loss is enormously destructive and easily cancels an earlier windfall. Protecting principal and avoiding major losses matter far more than pursuing a short-term fortune. Veteran investors including Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger, and Duan Yongping treat making fewer mistakes—and never repeating the same one—as the first principle of investing.

### How Do We Fall into Investment Traps?

Why do we repeatedly make bad investment decisions? I see three common causes.

#### Cognitive Mismatch

Blindly investing in a field you do not understand and have not researched is gambling in substance, and it usually ends in failure. Trading stocks on tips without understanding the market; buying property on an agent’s recommendation without knowing real estate; or putting money into an opaque project because it promises high interest are all attempts to profit beyond the boundary of your understanding. Most end with money earned by luck being lost through lack of skill.

#### Fear of Missing Out

Fear of missing out, or FOMO, is especially common in investing. “I cannot miss this bull market!” “If I do not buy a home now, I will never afford one!” The anxiety that “it will be too late if I do not get aboard” makes people decide in a rush without calm analysis. They buy at the top or chase extremely risky fashions.

#### Emotional Impulses

Fear of missing an opportunity is not the only enemy. Other emotions also demand caution:

· Competitiveness. You enter a luxury store without intending to buy, but a salesperson’s dismissive words provoke your pride and you purchase impulsively. Emotion has produced an irrational decision.

· Envy and comparison. Someone around you boasts of an investment return. You think, “If they can do it, so can I,” and follow blindly without considering your circumstances or tolerance for risk.

· Self-esteem. When others question or compare you—“Why aren’t you rich yet?”—you may take risks beyond your capacity merely to prove a point or preserve face.

Like fog, these emotions obscure rational judgment and produce investment mistakes.

### Treat the Cause: How Can We Avoid the Traps?

Once the roots are visible, treatment becomes possible. The method is simple: keep learning. Here is my prescription.

#### For Cognitive Mismatch: Invest in Your Mind Before Investing Money

I do not mean that you should never invest in what you do not yet understand. I mean that you should recognize the importance of understanding and approach it with a growth mindset. Before putting down real money, do the work and deepen your knowledge of the field.

If you are considering a home, study the history and present condition of the real-estate market. Determine what truly drives prices. Understand taxes and holding costs, the difference between an investment property and a primary residence, and which neighborhoods and cities offer greater potential.

The more we learn and the deeper our research, the higher the probability of success. If we still fall into a trap after serious study, the loss is not entirely wasted; it is tuition. It raises our understanding and creates future opportunities. The worst outcome is investing in confusion, losing money, learning nothing, and repeating the same mistake. That truly is giving money away.

#### For Fear of Missing Out: Think Long Term and Do Less

Ninety-nine percent of “opportunities” that seem capable of making someone rich quickly are high-risk gambles. What we can do well is make effective investment decisions over five- and ten-year horizons. Before a decision, ask: What result will this choice produce in ten years?

Buffett has a “twenty-slot punch card” idea: imagine that you have only twenty major investment opportunities in your lifetime and must punch one slot for each decision. You would become extraordinarily cautious, force yourself to research deeply, and invest only in fields you truly understand.

Good investment opportunities are always scarce. They deserve patient waiting and careful selection. A handful can create substantial wealth. Yet many people react to one tip today and become anxious about a new trend tomorrow. Frequent trading wastes precious opportunities and produces a pile of mistakes.

#### For Emotional Impulses: Strengthen the Inner Self and Block the Noise

Managing our own emotions is hard enough. When other people’s evaluations influence us as well, we are even more likely to become overheated. Once emotion loses control, reason steps aside and bad decisions follow. A strong inner core that is not governed by others’ judgments is essential to avoiding emotional investing.

### A Trap Worse Than a Failed Investment—the Black Hole of Time

We now turn to a form of lost wealth more damaging than a bad investment: lost time. Its long-term cost can be even greater than lost money.

In his 2010 essay “How to Lose Time and Money,” Paul Graham argued that the worst way to lose time is often not play, but work that looks like work while producing nothing.

Consider a common example. Many newcomers receive an assignment and immediately bury themselves in it according to their own understanding, without first clarifying the background, objective, or other key information. They work late for a long time, only to discover that the result is entirely different from what the boss actually wanted.

Curiously, spending the same time playing would make you uneasy. Because you are “working,” however, diligence in action conceals laziness in decision-making and provides self-comfort. From the perspective of effective output, unproductive work and play both waste time.

Ask yourself: Do I still remember the substance of what I worked overtime to produce last week? Did overtime feel unavoidable then? Looking back, did the effort have long-term meaning? Would using that time to strengthen a core capability have made a promotion or raise more likely? Would exploring a side business have been more likely to add an income source?

Many people know these principles, but habitually use diligence in action to hide laziness in thought, inventing countless reasons why overtime is unavoidable. They continually invest life’s most precious resource in things with no long-term value and no capacity to accumulate an advantage. This is the more hidden and expensive form of wealth loss.

I hope these words provoke reflection. An old saying tells us that a teacher does not enlighten someone who is not eager, nor explain to someone who is not struggling to express an idea. Without a jolt, we find it difficult to change deeply rooted habits. We must recognize clearly that much of the time, we are making excuses for inertia and an unwillingness to change—not genuinely lacking time to learn.

### The Compounding of Time: Little SS’s Leap in Wealth

Return to Little A and Little S from the previous chapter, and add a third character: Little SS. Little SS begins with the same income and investment approach as Little S. The only difference is that Little SS uses spare time to build a side business seriously. Assume:

· First four years: the side business starts slowly, averaging 1,000 yuan a month.

· From year five: it suddenly takes off, reaching 100,000 yuan a month and growing 10 percent annually thereafter.

To some degree, Little SS reflects my own experience. My first four years of content creation produced almost no income. In the fifth year, I began monetizing, and revenue reached 1.5 million yuan in the first year. Believe that the pattern of “slow at first, then a leap” genuinely exists. Many achievements that appear impossible cannot be foreseen at the beginning. Only practice and reflection reveal how they work. Later, in the section on learning to make money, I will explain in detail how to draw insight from personal experience and understand theory more deeply after practice.

Under the same assumptions, the three people’s wealth after fifteen years is shown in Figure 8-1:

· Little A, conservative saver: approximately 2 million yuan.

· Little S, self-investor: approximately 6 million yuan.

· Little SS, self-investor plus side business: approximately 34.67 million yuan, excluding returns from reinvesting it.

The point is that investing your time poorly may cause you to miss an opportunity like Little SS’s to change your fate. Only by continually investing time in things with cumulative effects—strengthening core skills, building a personal brand, exploring a promising side business—can enormous returns become possible. Spend the time elsewhere and the opportunity passes you by.

One reality must be emphasized: sufficient conditions are rare in the real world. Unlike a school examination, investing *x* hours guarantees neither success nor riches. But putting time into cumulative activities is often a necessary condition for success. Without it, success is impossible; with it, success becomes possible. What we can do is satisfy the necessary conditions, then maximize our probability of success—and improve our “luck”—through continual choice, experimentation, learning, and iteration. If Little SS’s outcome appeals to you, you should at least give yourself the opportunity to try.

Besides matching my experience and observations of people around me, the income assumptions for Little SS explain a phenomenon I call the “side-business filter.” If side businesses can make money, why do most people fail to earn from them? In my view, inaction and dabbling without persistence—failing to dig the well deeply enough—are the main reasons.

### Leave the “Slowlane” and Enter the “Fastlane” to Wealth

Borrowing from *The Millionaire Fastlane*, let me restate the significance of exploring a side business. The book argues that there are two routes to wealth.

· The Slowlane: employment + saving + gradual financial investing.

This is the “correct method” most people are taught. In reality, even if you save 20 percent of monthly income and earn an 8 percent annual return, genuine financial freedom may take decades. Along the way, economic crises, industry changes, health problems, and other uncertainties may intervene. Most people may never break through the bottleneck of exchanging labor time for income.

· The Fastlane: create value, build systems, and own an asset or business that produces continuing income.

The key is the “system”: an income source that can operate without depending entirely on your personal time. It may be a scalable online business, a portfolio of rental assets, or digital products and courses you develop. The idea is not to quit your job and gamble. It is to begin asking what value you can create beyond selling time for income, and how that value can continue producing returns.

Three common misconceptions require clarification.

#### “Fast” Does Not Mean “Immediately”

The Fastlane is fast relative to spending decades in employment. It does not mean weeks or months, but perhaps ten years. Accumulating tens of millions of yuan in roughly a decade is already extraordinarily fast. Do not fantasize about becoming rich overnight; it is unrealistic. Unless deliberately trained, however, our minds continually produce that fantasy. Remind yourself that an enterprise capable of generating sustained profit often requires a decade spent sharpening one sword.

#### “Fast” Does Not Mean “Easy”

Ali Abdaal distinguishes “get rich quick” from “get rich easy.” Getting rich quickly over a ten-year horizon may be logically possible; getting rich easily almost never is.

Our minds notice only the event—“Their side business earns millions a year!”—and assume it happened in a single stroke. We must train our attention on the accumulation behind the event: study, practice, trial and error, adjustment, improvement after rejection, and persistence when progress seems absent. Many “sudden breakthroughs” rest on three, five, or more years of deep work.

Valuable growth is never easy. Persisting in work with compounding effects, even when the immediate result looks slow, is often more meaningful than mindless overtime. Accumulation grows exponentially; overtime usually grows only linearly. Over the long term, the outcomes are worlds apart.

#### Do Not Follow the Crowd

If everyone around you works and works overtime in the same routine, following the same pattern will probably produce the same result. A different result requires different choices and actions.

### Travel with Companions and Make Change Happen

Building wealth is undeniably challenging and may be hard. One effective way to improve your odds of persistence is to find people who share your aspirations. You can learn, explore, and practice together; exchange lessons, stimulate ideas, encourage each other, and even collaborate. With companions, the road feels less lonely and difficult.

Try sharing the ideas in this book with friends. If they agree after reading it, you gain fellow travelers. You can support each other and grow together.

## Chapter 9: The Personal Value Formula—the Core of Moving from Working Well to Making Money

This chapter presents one of the book’s central ideas: the personal value formula. It is not a shortcut to instant success, but a framework for choosing, allocating effort, and evaluating returns. Whether you want a promotion and raise, a better platform through a job change, or a sustainable source of profit from a side business, the framework makes your actions clearer and more repeatable.

If that sounds too good to be true, your instinct is understandable. The personal value formula is powerful, but not because it produces immediate results. Its value appears when you stand at a crossroads and must judge what deserves accumulation and what merely drains you; what is a direction and what is a wrong turn; what should come first and what can wait.

Specifically, it means:

· Using the market’s perspective to distinguish real demand from false;

· Building a foundation for compounding as you would build a product;

· Identifying leverage to accelerate success.

The previous chapter concerned accumulation. This one answers the more important question: what should you accumulate so that your effort is not wasted?

A map can point the way, but you must still take the steps.

### What Is Your Value, Really?

Before discussing the personal value formula, we must answer a basic question: What is personal value? Frankly, many people mistake “value” for a skill label or volume of work. Very few can explain personal value at the level of first principles.

The marginal market value of certain once-popular skills—for example, some traditional e-commerce development patterns—has clearly declined. This reminds us that personal assets such as money, equity, and property may appreciate over time, but particular skills often depreciate as technology and markets change.

That introduces an apparent paradox: if concrete skills depreciate, should young people still learn them?

The key is to distinguish two points:

· The problem is not learning skills, but binding all your personal value to one particular skill.

· A more robust approach divides skills into two categories.

The first consists of core capabilities that resist depreciation: underlying, transferable abilities such as creating, selling, and seizing opportunities.

The second consists of specific skills that depreciate more readily because they depend on a technology stack or market cycle, such as operating a particular programming tool, process, or procedure.

Young people lack accumulated capital and are unlikely to earn serious money through financial investments. At this stage, our greatest asset really is ourselves—our human capital and ability to understand. Self-improvement and continued growth therefore matter, but priority should go to what is most transferable and compounds most readily.

### The Core Capabilities That Are Truly Valuable: Creating, Selling, and Seizing Opportunity

*The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* suggests that a person’s most important group of core capabilities can often be summarized as three (Figure 9-1):

(1) Creating: producing valuable products, services, or content.

(2) Selling: enabling value to be seen, understood, chosen, and ultimately paid for.

(3) Seizing opportunity: increasing the probability that you encounter, recognize, and capture opportunities.

Figure 9-1: The core capabilities that are truly valuable

You may ask, “Aren’t my skills programming, data analysis, and product design?”

Those are mostly specific skills. They certainly matter, but their market value fluctuates as tools and paradigms shift.

Consider algorithm engineers. A few years ago, theirs was a star role in technology. With the rise of large models, however, professionals whose abilities remain limited to applying one class of traditional algorithms may see their career space squeezed unless they complete a paradigm shift and enter a new field.

My own experience makes the three capabilities more concrete:

· I continually invest time in making content, explaining knowledge, and publishing ideas. That is creating.

· Without a plan for distribution and conversion, even excellent content may remain unknown. Helping more people see and understand it and become willing to pay is selling.

· As for what Naval calls “luck,” it is not mere accident. By producing continually, entering dense networks, and exposing your work to the market, you increase the probability of opportunity. In my case, readers discovering me, collaborations arising, and learning from exceptional people all become more likely in an environment where I am visible and connected.

Turn these capabilities into an action checklist for both your principal job and side business:

· Have I created real, meaningful, verifiable results? (Creating)

· Can I help the target audience understand and recognize the result, and pay for it when appropriate? (Selling)

· Am I continually increasing my probability of being seen and selected? (Seizing opportunity)

The framework applies broadly.

### Warning: Do Not Let a Job Title Define Your Value

Many people—especially conventional “good students” and “good employees”—bind their value to a job title: data analyst, data engineer, data scientist, and so forth, as though a different label changes their worth.

In essence, these labels matter far less than we imagine.

Chamath Palihapitiya, an early head of growth at Facebook, now Meta, once said in an interview that the company invented the title “data scientist” to recruit a data analyst from Google who held a PhD. The occupation corresponding to that invented title was later called “the sexiest job of the 21st century” by *The New York Times*. As a data scientist myself, I have published videos explaining my definition of the role. Looking back, however, it is a label. Labels usually arise to solve practical problems such as recruiting difficulty or unclear organizational divisions. They possess no intrinsic substance.

A label can assist communication, but do not mistake it for value itself. The capabilities actually driving value remain creating, selling, and seizing opportunity.

Viewed another way, titles such as software engineer, programmer, and developer do not differ greatly. The people behind them all perform some form of creation. The rise of AI makes the point clearer. Your value does not lie in the physical act of typing code, because AI can write code too and will become increasingly capable.

Your value lies more in whether you can continually use tools and resources to produce results that matter to the market. An excellent data analyst’s core competitiveness rarely lies in the label. It lies in deep thinking, problem-solving, and value creation.

Why do we fall into the mistake of equating labels with value? This leads to the next key question: How should we measure the value of labor?

### Three Levels of Labor: Input, Output, and Outcome

The value of labor can be measured at three levels:

(1) Input: how much effort you expend, measured in hours, energy, lines of code, number of meetings, and so forth.

(2) Output: what you produce—a program, report, model, proposal, analytical conclusion, and so forth.

(3) Outcome: the impact produced by your output—higher revenue, lower cost, reduced risk, an improved user experience, and so on.

Many people remain focused on input. A smaller number attend to output, and fewer still to outcomes. This is not a question of who works harder, but of the coordinate system used for measurement. In my experience, although many people know they should be results-oriented, fewer than one in ten may genuinely measure the value of their work by outcomes rather than input.

The focus differs at each level:

· People focused on input repeatedly optimize *how* to do something;

· People focused on output clarify *what* to make;

· People focused on outcomes begin with demand and objectives, ask *why* it should be done, and seek the answer in the market.

Someone who continually displays weekly reports, overtime hours, and meeting counts in workplace communication tools and uses them to prove personal value is probably still operating at the input level. That should be a warning.

Consider content writing.

· Input: time spent researching, thinking, and writing.

· Output: the article, measured by quality and completion. Time invested does not equal output quality. High quality often requires extensive cutting, revision, or complete rewriting. What matters is not how long you spent, but what you wrote. Fixating on input makes you reluctant to delete hard-won paragraphs and can damage the output.

· Outcome: views, likes, shares, subscriptions, revenue, influence, and so forth.

The most important and most frequently neglected step is the leap from output to outcome—going to market.

Does higher output quality necessarily produce a better outcome? No. Many people believe their products have strong technology and high quality, yet receive no market interest. Often the problem is not quality in itself, but that the supposed quality is not the quality the market needs.

I have interacted with many founders. Especially among those from technical backgrounds, the most common realization after starting a company is how important and difficult going to market really is. Before launching, they assumed that making a good product would naturally win public recognition and payment. After building it, they discovered that even relatives and friends might have no interest in trying it, let alone consumers.

In one sentence: output is what *you* consider good; outcome is what the *market* recognizes as good.

Moving from input to output is difficult, but the standards are relatively clear. Moving from output to outcome is harder because it requires understanding and respecting the market and possessing the ability to enter it. Later chapters will return to this repeatedly.

### The Personal Value Formula and How to Apply It

Let us first summarize the chapter so far:

· Personal value is a person’s principal form of capital while young and deserves focused development;

· Skills fall into two categories: core capabilities that depreciate slowly and specific skills that depreciate readily;

· Labor value has three levels: input, output, and outcome;

· Core capabilities connect more directly to outcomes, while many specific skills remain at the input or output level;

· Going to market is the key step from output to outcome.

With this foundation, we can now introduce the chapter’s central formula. It emerges from three simple steps (Figure 9-2):

(1) Understand the market;

(2) Build the product;

(3) Use leverage.

**Personal value = understanding the market + building the product + using leverage**

Xiong Li, an engineering manager at Databricks, first shared the formula with me in a conversation as an explanation of how to build competitiveness in the employment market. I later discovered that it applies not only to job hunting, but to promotion, career change, side businesses, and entrepreneurship—in essence, every situation that involves going to market.

Most people begin career decisions with “me”: “I can do these things, so this is what I should receive”; “I did these things, so others should recognize me.”

The market never prices your personal narrative. A more robust path begins by asking what the market actually needs, how it judges value, and what kinds of outcomes people will pay for.

Even after you understand the market, you may fail to develop yourself as a product. When writing a résumé, for example, you may pile on experiences rather than emphasize what matters for the target role. When delivering a project, you may focus on making the thing without explaining what outcomes it produced.

This is especially common among recent graduates. A typical résumé mistake is listing every experience the writer considers “valuable” and refusing to delete any. The result runs several pages despite containing little substance. In the writer’s imagination, an interviewer will discover a unique spark buried in one of those experiences. In reality, the interviewer may scan the résumé for a few seconds and discard it.

Xiong Li explained the formula’s origin. During a demanding heli-skiing trip with friends, one participant was an exceptionally discreet billionaire: an elite Texas Hold’em player, entrepreneur, and philanthropist. After becoming acquainted, the billionaire saw promise in Xiong Li and the other young people and passed on what was said to be a formula for becoming a billionaire—the formula above.

I hope the story conveys the weight carried by such a simple-looking expression.

Later chapters will repeatedly unpack it through concrete cases and show how to apply it in working and making money.

The publication of this book itself provides one example. You can view publishing and marketing as an application of the personal value formula:

· Understand the market: identify the target reader’s needs and pain points. After studying the audience, I selected “from working well to making money” from among many possible subjects.

· Build the product: refine the content into a form and structure that match readers’ needs and the patterns by which platforms distribute material.

· Use leverage: use a platform’s distribution system and user base, team collaboration, and channel resources to increase reach and influence.

If the book ultimately performs well—through reach, reputation, or actual sales—the formula will be the underlying reason: align with the market, deliver the product, then amplify its influence with leverage.

### Take Action: Three Exercises in Going to Market

Theory should be followed by practice. Each of the following exercises is intended to make you take a first step toward the market. Here, “market” is broad and depends on your objective.

#### Exercise One: Have You Met the Promotion Standard, and Will You Align with Your Manager?

· Market: your direct manager, who often has enormous influence on your career development.

· Task: Do you believe you have met the standard for promotion? Are you willing to discuss it proactively and hear your manager’s assessment? Do your self-evaluation and your manager’s agree? Do you genuinely understand the manager’s expectations and criteria?

#### Exercise Two: Does Anyone Value the Knowledge You Share?

· Market: your social circle or the audience on a public platform.

· Task: publish three pieces of content you consider valuable, preferably not pure records of daily life, and observe the interaction—likes, views, and so forth.

· Reflection: Do you dare to share? Do you know what to share? Does anyone pay attention, respond, or repost? If you are afraid, do not know, or receive little response, you probably have substantial room to improve at product building and going to market.

#### Exercise Three: What Is the Current Market Value of Your “Professional Calling Card”—Your Résumé or Portfolio?

· Market: the employment market, industry, or role you want to enter.

· Task: Do you know which companies your current résumé and capabilities could target, and approximately what level and salary range they correspond to?

· Reflection: If you lack a reasonably clear answer, you probably have neither studied the market seriously nor refined your professional calling card sufficiently.

I hope these exercises remind you that going to market is something you should do, but very likely have not begun and do not yet do well.

Mark your present situation honestly—for example, “I am afraid to ask in Exercise One,” “I tried Exercise Two but received a weak response,” or “I have no idea about Exercise Three”—and record your thoughts. Understanding the present is the beginning of improvement.

## Chapter 10: From Ideals to Action—a Concrete Path to Realizing Personal Value

Many people identify with the statement, “I understand many principles, yet still cannot live this life well.” The hidden problem is this chapter’s central question: How do we convert understanding into concrete action, maximize our progress toward financial freedom, and live well?

### A Broad Highway That Appears Correct

Begin with the path widely believed to offer the best odds. For a professional, success seems to mean doing the following things well:

· Find the “wind”: enter fast-growing industries on the upswing.

· Choose the right major: while still in school, select a field that will place you in a future boom.

· Prefer industry over company: choosing a promising industry matters more than entering a particular big-name company.

· Invest early: after receiving your salary, invest a fixed share—say 10 percent—in an index fund such as a Nasdaq fund; hold for the long term and ignore short-term movement.

· Learn and accumulate early: human capital is your principal asset at the start of a career, so invest time in learning the business and polishing foundational skills.

· Acquire marketable skills: in spare time, learn abilities useful across companies and earn relevant certifications as hard currency on your résumé and capital for changing jobs.

· Plan a job-change cycle, perhaps every three to six years: practice interview problems, improve your résumé, prepare for interviews, and move to industries and companies with better prospects in search of higher pay, greater responsibility, or a new wave of growth.

· Monetize experience: turn your work experience and industry insight into articles or content, share them through social media, and perhaps earn additional income or build a personal brand.

· …

The list can continue indefinitely.

Do you agree with this apparently perfect route? If you nodded frequently while reading it, be careful: you may not yet have fully internalized the mental models emphasized earlier. These perfect-looking routes are one deep reason why people “understand many principles, yet still cannot live this life well.” Let us uncover the misconceptions hidden inside them.

### Three Misconceptions Behind the “Perfect Path”

#### Misconception One: The Object Mindset

You are waiting for a path designed by someone else. That places you in the position of being arranged, just as you followed examinations and promotions from grade to grade throughout childhood. You appear to choose, but actually outsource your life to a template: industry, company, certificate, job-change cycle, investment percentage. You follow the “correct answer” rather than developing judgment and creating.

#### Misconception Two: The Fixed Mindset

The path assumes that your objectives, preferences, abilities, and living conditions will remain stable for years. Reality is the opposite: industries change, opportunities change, and you change. A better condition is not a precise five- or ten-year plan, but the continuing ability to recalibrate—to change strategy as information changes, change tactics as ability grows, and redefine success as objectives evolve.

#### Misconception Three: The Scoring Mindset

The path looks workable because it compresses a complex life into a collection of indicators measured with single scores. But as I have repeatedly emphasized, life is a complex, multidimensional system. Fixating on one score may make you more “successful” in the short term, but over time it is more likely to create imbalance and backlash.

You may object: “Even if the mental model is imperfect, I will accept it if it brings wealth and status.”

Abandon that fantasy. The “perfect path” is not reliable either. If it were universally applicable and offered high odds, most people could follow the map and succeed. Reality says otherwise. The reason is no mystery: many returns on the path arise from gaps in information and ability or from differences in competitive intensity. Once it becomes a universal template, competition dilutes the return. Any route guaranteed to make people successful, however reasonable it appears for a moment, eventually traps them in an arms race.

### Another Cognitive Trap—Naming the Right Direction for Effort

If a perfect path is impossible, surely we can at least identify the correct direction. Consider three popular routes to making money.

· Embrace content creation: in an age of information overload, the barrier to distribution has fallen. Individuals can become visible through content, connect with others, and monetize what they create.

· Develop or create independently: with mature technical infrastructure such as cloud computing, an individual or small team can now build products or services once possible only for large companies and reach users at scale.

· Become a super-individual: AI enables one person to perform work that once required a team. It can assist design, programming, market analysis, and more, greatly expanding the boundary of individual capability.

All sound promising, but each can become a mental trap. Almost every direction has an opposite supported by equally valid reasons and logic.

Take content creation. We can also argue the reverse: in an age of information overload and scarce attention, deep reading, deep thinking, and deep writing are rarer. People with these abilities can not only create content, but also build influence inside organizations through high-quality documents, proposals, and reports. They penetrate noise and form distinctive insights, making it easier to stand out. In this age, might deliberately reducing distraction and preserving intellectual depth create an asymmetric competitive advantage? Paul Graham has suggested that people in the future may divide into two groups: those who can write—meaning think and express themselves deeply—and those who cannot.

So which is correct: connecting outward by embracing media, or cultivating depth inward through serious writing?

The answer is that direction alone usually does not determine whether someone succeeds.

### Two Reasons the “Right Direction” Becomes a Trap

#### Reason One: The Future Is Dynamic

A direction that appears correct today may change or be disproved a month from now. Without underlying logic and a mechanism for recalibration, you can only keep depending on others for the next direction, leaving you forever a follower.

#### Reason Two: Markets Move Toward Equilibrium

If a path genuinely offers stable excess returns, more people eventually discover and enter it. Competition dilutes the return until it approaches the average. Long-term winners are often not those who first learned the direction, but those who can continue widening their lead within it.

### The Essence of Success: How Far You Travel Matters More Than Where You Start

There is more than one path to success, and how far you can travel on one often matters more.

Two people may both create content: one flashes briefly and disappears, while the other persists for a decade and becomes a leading voice. Two people may both write: one remains preoccupied with technique, while the other turns expression into a cognitive advantage. The key difference is whether you develop an ability within a field that can accumulate, transfer, and receive a market price, then invest over the long term so that it compounds—not only in money, but in knowledge, skill, influence, and trust. How far you can travel ultimately depends on inner motivation: your willingness to endure long, monotonous periods, bear the pain of delayed gratification, and continually correct your work.

### Secure the Foundation Before Discussing Methods

This is why the first half of the book devoted so much space to cognitive biases, mental models, and underlying frameworks.

Had I begun with methods and tactics, you might remember the conclusions while reproducing the old mindset: still searching for templates, shortcuts, and directions, and ultimately returning to the cycle of “knowing much but being unable to act.”

The rest of the book assumes that we have reached a cognitive agreement: you no longer search for standard answers, but learn how to judge, test, and iterate. If you still strongly expect someone else to choose for you, return to the preceding chapters with one question: Why does this expectation keep making me ineffective?

The formula for escaping the arms race and moving toward financial freedom can be simple (Figure 10-1).

“Simple” does not mean “easy.” The simpler something is in logic, the harder it often is to execute. Persist for one year and you meet opportunities suited to one year of ability. ### Stay Alert in Safety: Preserve Your “Jungle Skills”

No matter how stable your present job, periodically place yourself back in the real market and test your value.

A classic story compares two tigers, one placed in a zoo and the other in the jungle. Their habits become entirely different. The zoo tiger grows accustomed to being fed and exchanges daily performances for food. Released into the jungle, it may be unable to survive because its hunting ability has atrophied. Work often resembles life in the zoo. We complete assigned tasks and receive corresponding rewards—salary and bonuses.

An organization is a relatively closed environment like a zoo: it determines the rules, evaluation, and allocation of resources. Becoming accustomed to it is normal. But you must also ask whether you retain the independent ability to survive and create value if the external environment or organization changes.

Go too long without testing yourself in the market and you lose your hunting ability. The more you depend on stability, the more you fear instability. The more you fear it, the more you need to reassure yourself: “My job must be secure.” Your mind becomes increasingly fragile. Even if your goal is simply promotion and higher pay, preserve your jungle skills. They also make you calmer inside the zoo, freeing you from excessive anxiety and hardship driven by fear of losing your job.

I therefore recommend beginning three things early.

First, establish interactions with the real world that do not depend on your current company.

Start small: explore a side business of any size; volunteer your expertise to help a small shop improve operations or conversion; actively observe and understand how genuine commercial activity works—how customers decide, how cash flow forms, and how competition occurs.

Second, identify your distinctive strengths, interests, and passions. Answer these questions concretely:

· What are my natural gifts? What do I find easier to do correctly?

· What do others find tedious that I can continue doing, perhaps even enjoy?

· What is relatively easy for me but difficult for others?

Third, accumulate and build personal value in response to real market demand. Shift your perspective from “doing my job well” to “accumulating value the market recognizes.” Different objectives require different expressions of value.

· If your goal is advancement and higher pay inside the company, you need to be seen as high-potential talent, earn a manager’s trust and crucial support, and develop a stable reputation and influence within the organization.

· If your goal is changing jobs or careers, you need verifiable performance and cases, abilities closely matching the new role, and strong learning ability and adaptability.

· If your goal is a side business or startup, you need the ability to build what the market needs and the ability to make people know and trust you and become willing to buy your product or service.

Whatever the objective, the central point is the same: value is defined by the market. Here, the “market” may mean the internal promotion market, the external talent market, or the consumer market.

### The Foundation Is Complete; Prepare to Build

This chapter contains a great deal of information, but it has one purpose: turning the cognitive framework into a system for action.

Later chapters present more concrete methods. Remember, however, that understanding and mental models are fundamental. They determine whether a method becomes useful assistance or another template for self-deception.

After finishing the book, reinforce the foundation in three ways:

· Internalize more: reflect repeatedly and test the ideas against your own experience.

· Act more: test and correct your understanding through action, turning theory into usable experience.

· Communicate more: exchange ideas with people who share these principles and improve together.

# Part Two: Uniting Knowledge with Action

## Chapter 11: The Wisdom of Choice—Use “The Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, and Method” to Plan Your Career

This chapter explores the wisdom of choice: how to use the framework of “the Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, and Method” to plan your career. The framework comes from *The Art of War*. I borrow it for modern career decisions for two reasons. First, it is a cultural anchor familiar to many Chinese readers. These five terms are deeply embedded in our language and experience, making the analysis easier to understand and remember. Second, the structure has strong explanatory power. Once each term receives a modern meaning, it maps closely onto reality.

· The Way: understanding and mental models.

· Heaven: macro trends and the dividends of an era.

· Earth: the company platform and business unit.

· Command: support from leaders and key colleagues.

· Method: practical skills and execution.

These five levels map almost completely onto most people’s path of growth. Early in a career, we usually fall directly into Method, focusing on concrete skills and execution. With experience, we discover that much of our performance and room for development comes from Command—how leaders and the team view us and whether they offer resources and opportunities. Later still, we see more clearly that the speed and ceiling of each lower layer are often determined by the one above it.

If we possess the full perspective of all five layers from the beginning, many choices become clearer and detours fewer. Their hierarchy also explains the saying “choice matters more than effort.” If the direction is wrong at the levels of the Way, Heaven, Earth, or Command, however refined our Method, the return may be half the result for twice the effort—or nothing at all. If the industry and era, company and business, or leaders and team are wrong, individual diligence often receives no equivalent reward.

### Borrowing from *The Art of War* to Build a Framework for Choice

If choice matters so much, how do we work at choosing well? We cannot invest only at the level of Method by improving skills and techniques. We must devote sufficient energy to the directional levels of the Way, Heaven, Earth, and Command.

My own career lasted only eight years, but had one distinctive feature: I actively created many opportunities to choose and made several difficult decisions at critical points.

#### 2017: Turning Down a High-Paying Consulting Job for a Technology Giant

In 2017, I declined an offer of 1.34 million yuan a year from Boston Consulting Group’s Beijing office and chose an Amazon position in Seattle paying $180,000.

#### 2019: Financial Freedom or Growth?

In 2019, I faced several highly tempting opportunities. One was an economist role at Kuaishou offering 3 million yuan annually, including pre-IPO options then valued at 1.74 million that might multiply after listing. Including appreciation, it may have been the highest compensation I have ever been offered and could have changed my life. I ultimately declined Kuaishou. I also gave up Amazon stock that had doubled in value but remained 80 percent unvested, moving from economist at Amazon to senior data scientist at Meta for substantially lower pay.

#### 2021: Giving Up a Promotion to Return to China for “Entrepreneurial” Growth

At Meta, I again gave up a promotion opportunity and returned to China as an associate director at Tencent. The internet industry was in a cycle of layoffs and cost reduction, yet my team expanded against the trend. I had earned deep trust from my manager and directly assumed control of a team the manager had personally developed. By the time I left Tencent, I managed thirty people across six teams and had received two consecutive five-star performance ratings. Without the industry-wide setback, my scope might have grown further.

#### 2023: Returning from Management to Technical Work

In 2023, I left Tencent and joined Statsig in the United States as an individual contributor. I shifted from a purely technical role into developer evangelism, combining sales, marketing, and technology.

I share these experiences not to prove that I chose correctly, but to emphasize that I thought comparatively deeply at every crucial moment. After each choice, I continued reviewing it: what might have happened with A, and what with B? This long-term reflection made my understanding of choice more complete.

Had I joined BCG, I might eventually have entered finance and investment. Had I chosen Kuaishou, perhaps I would already have achieved financial freedom and become a technology investor. Had I remained at Amazon, I might have followed a stable path of rapid advancement at a super-company. Looking back, no choice was absolutely right or wrong. What mattered was developing a reusable framework and knowing clearly why I chose as I did.

The same principle applies when reading biographies of successful people. Do not look only at the decision. Examine the historical background, the alternatives in front of the person—especially consensus choices that seemed self-evident—why they took a non-consensus path, what insight lay behind it, and how they persisted. Only by seeing the iceberg beneath the choice do we learn something real.

### Accumulate for Choice: First You Need the Right to Choose

We often discuss choosing among job opportunities, but many people overlook the beginning of the logical chain: choice requires options. Working backward, the process looks roughly like this:

(1) Receive a job offer;

(2) Pass the interview;

(3) Obtain an interview;

(4) Begin a job search with confidence and preparation;

(5) Possess the strength and reputation to compete—résumé, ability, and standing.

If you say, “I want to choose freely among high-paying opportunities, but I can barely obtain an interview,” ask: “At which step of this staircase am I stuck?”

For most people, the answer concerns whether everyday accumulation has been sufficient. Examine yourself honestly in four areas.

#### Accumulate Valuable Experience for Your Résumé

Ask whether you have remained too long in transactional, repetitive work without challenge. Such experience often adds nothing to a résumé and may even detract. Determine what abilities and traits the target role genuinely requires, then deliberately accumulate results and evidence demonstrating them in your present work.

At a résumé workshop in my community, for example, a participant aspired to become a game producer. His résumé listed a great deal of work, including many detailed execution tasks such as community operations. The volume looked impressive, but matched the target poorly. A game producer needs product planning, team coordination, commercial judgment, and related capabilities.

#### Maintain Sharp Market Insight

Do you genuinely understand the latest industry developments? Do you know which skills the market needs most, which industries are rising or declining, which companies are hiring, and what kinds of people they want?

Without clear answers, your résumé cannot be precise and your job-search strategy cannot be effective. You must understand market demand.

#### Emphasize Professional Exchange and Public Practice

If your work is highly specialized—data, development, product design—communicate beyond the company. If confidentiality prevents discussing actual work, find an equivalent public project and display the process openly.

A data analyst cannot publish company financial statements, but can analyze public data online: trends in a city’s home prices or the distribution of ratings on a food-delivery platform. Explain how you found the data, modeled it, and reached the final insight, then publish the analysis.

This builds a display window outside the company walls. Others can see your craft, raising your competitiveness in the job market and perhaps broadening how you approach your day job.

#### Build Long-Term Recruiter Relationships and Apply Precisely

Excellent recruiters can bring excellent opportunities, but they are hard to find and require filtering and long-term cultivation. Except in emergencies, experienced professionals should avoid low-quality mass applications. Treat every application seriously: tailor the résumé, write the cover letter carefully, and research the company’s business and industry position deeply.

This follows a basic principle: focus on actions with better odds rather than blindly multiplying attempts. If you often complain that good opportunities are absent, review these four areas. Most people have invested far too little effort.

### Applying “the Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, and Method” at Work

Let us examine the framework’s modern meaning in greater depth.

#### The Way: Understanding and Mental Models

The Way is the foundation of every choice. What you understand determines what you can see; your mental models determine how you judge. Together, they shape every important choice in life. Even when options exist at the other four levels, insufficient understanding may prevent you from seeing the truly good ones or seizing one encountered by chance.

The key is learning how to think, not memorizing conclusions. Genuine understanding requires time to chew, digest, and internalize.

#### Heaven: Macro Trends and the Dividends of an Era

Heaven means the macro environment and industry trends. When the macro environment rises, a rising tide lifts the market. When an industry trends upward, individual development finds leverage. The logic resembles investing: choosing the right track—the environment and industry—often matters more than selecting an individual stock—the company or role—within a bad one.

#### Earth: Company Platform and Business Unit

Earth includes the company platform and specific business unit, whose importance is often underestimated. Two Tencent employees, one in games and one in cloud services, may face entirely different cultures, objectives, rhythms, and capability requirements—almost two separate professional worlds.

Heaven sets Earth’s ceiling; Earth rarely changes Heaven. Choosing a better macro trend and more promising business unit is therefore crucial.

#### Command: Support from Leaders and Key Colleagues

Command refers mainly to your leaders and key colleagues, especially people who can become mentors or supporters. Leaders include not only your direct manager, but those higher in the organization. Together, they determine business direction, resource allocation, opportunities for development, and methods of evaluation. If you plan to remain at a company, much of your experience—performance standards, assignment of work, atmosphere, and frequency of overtime—is determined by leaders and the team.

Remember that the Way, Heaven, and Earth continue to operate above Command. Even an excellent leader cannot resist a bad industry and business direction forever.

#### Method: Practical Skills and Execution

Method covers concrete working practices, professional skills, and the ability to execute, including communication, project management, and technical ability. It is the easiest layer on which to focus, but its effectiveness depends heavily on the four above it. When the direction is wrong, more refined methods may merely amplify the error.

Method is best learned through active reading and problem-driven study, which usually outperform passive listening. Two people may read the same article: one changes behavior and receives a return, while the other merely “knows” its content. The difference lies not in the information obtained, but in the willingness to expend genuine effort digesting and applying it.

### A Method for Career Choice: From Company Life Cycle to Personal Positioning

The following perspectives form a reusable framework for judgment.

#### Perspective One: Read the Company Life Cycle

Corporate finance theory suggests that companies follow relatively stable life cycles. With time on the horizontal axis and money on the vertical, sales, cash flow, and profit trace their development (Figure 11-1).

![img](./assets/CB_3300200542_5WjANM3LG3cc6fi3k53XZ6fk30i6XU6gT3jx3TV31R6YH6U83HD3LH6bd3PS0Ub5PZ330fc.jpg)

Figure 11-1: A company’s development trajectory

The five principal stages are startup, growth, competition, maturity, and decline.

· Startup: sales are low, heavy investment is required, and cash flow and profit are usually negative.

· Growth: sales rise rapidly and profit may begin, but cash remains tight because expansion requires continuing investment. This stage often offers the highest return on investment.

· Competition: profit, cash flow, and sales accumulate steadily; scale effects form; competition intensifies; and consolidation may begin.

· Maturity: sales growth peaks or declines and profit comes under pressure, but reduced investment—and perhaps layoffs to cut cost—can push cash flow to its peak.

· Decline: sales, cash flow, and profit all fall. China’s traditional internal-combustion vehicle industry is a typical example.

Most career opportunities lie in companies at the growth, competition, and maturity stages. Startups carry high risk; companies in decline should generally be avoided.

How do you apply this?

First, identify the company’s stage using the descriptions above together with other factors.

Consider Baidu. Its search business entered maturity long ago, and its transition to mobile internet largely failed. Yet employees’ experience there over the past decade was not necessarily poor. Baidu remains stably profitable, can maintain operations, and can make strategic experiments such as ERNIE Bot. Technical work there also retains résumé value, depending on the project. Is Baidu necessarily worse than a company in an emerging industry? Not at all.

Second, match the company to your needs.

The appropriate stage depends on your career phase, central objective, tolerance for risk, and alternatives. There is no standard answer. What matters is whether the company genuinely fits you.

#### Perspective Two: Evaluate Career Opportunities Like an Investor

Choosing a job is one of the largest investments of time in your life. We research cars and homes extensively, yet often invest too little in a decision that will occupy at least eight hours a day and alter our future path.

Viewed as an investor, human-capital investment has several characteristics:

(1) You invest your human capital in a company, which is more particular and complex than investing money.

(2) Access is differentiated: ability may let you join a leading private company such as OpenAI even when you have no opportunity to invest in it.

(3) The bet is highly concentrated: unlike a stock portfolio, you cannot place 1 percent of your human capital into several companies at once. Choosing a company means wagering most of your time on it.

A concentrated bet is risky, but success may be transformative, even producing financial freedom. Deep work in one company also forces continual appreciation in your own value: understanding the industry, accumulating relationships and resources, strengthening skills, and developing commercial judgment. Work is not merely selling labor for wages; it is a key process for appreciating human capital. Done well, that capital becomes increasingly valuable, brings better “investment” opportunities, and compounds.

How should you manage the risk?

· Conduct thorough due diligence on the industry and company.

· Unless you possess exceptional confidence in an opportunity, make your first job change to a company at Series D or later, or to a more mature company. Early-stage companies are risky, and an unknown brand offers limited endorsement of your human capital.

· Take a longer view of multiple moves. Do not treat one job change as a final battle. Over ten years, you may have three to five important opportunities to move. Ideally, your human capital is more valuable and your judgment sharper each time.

Before more specific strategies, remember:

· This is your life choice. All advice is only a tool; the final decision is yours.

· Choice should enable better exploration of the unknown, not attempt a once-and-for-all verdict. The future changes dramatically; remain open and flexible.

· Prefer directions that enable continuing growth and longer travel over superficial trends.

Before any important choice, answer honestly:

· What stage of career development am I in?

· What is my central pursuit?

· What is my tolerance for risk?

Base the final decision on your own judgment. Other people’s advice is reference material only. Your judgment should carry at least 70 percent of the weight, and ideally all of it.

### More Concrete Strategies for Different Career Stages

#### Early Career

The strategy should center on establishing a strong starting point and accumulating momentum.

(1) Prefer mature industries with stronger foundations.

(2) Seek companies that expand your future options. This may mean a large famous company or a smaller one with an exceptional brand, such as Game Science, the Hangzhou developer of *Black Myth: Wukong*. The key is significant added value to your résumé.

(3) Ensure the role has clear standards of success. Outcomes, good or bad, should be measured as objectively as possible. Avoid marginal positions whose presence makes little difference.

(4) Compensation differences below 30 percent are relatively limited in effect. Gaps beyond 30 percent are usually difficult to overcome through rapid promotion, so investigate their cause.

#### Midcareer, Generally After Three to Five Years

At midcareer, paths diverge strategically.

· For stability: choose a leading company in a mature industry and enter a core business unit with stable income.

· For aggressive growth: choose a startup- or growth-stage company and prefer an organization with a fast operating cadence.

A simple way to judge cadence is to observe how quickly vague time commitments are honored. When someone says, “I’ll look into it and get back to you,” do they respond ten minutes later or one month later? This often reveals the organization’s true speed of decision and execution.

### Choosing within the Same Company: Order Matters

Within a company, rank the factors as follows:

**Business value > direct manager > specific project**

A strong business benefits the entire team with more opportunities and faster promotion. A strong leader develops people, protects subordinates, and secures resources. However interesting or technically advanced a project, poor business prospects or weak leadership make it difficult to sustain.

### On Compensation: Think Long Term

We should care about total income across an entire career, not the short-term salary of one job. Starting pay does affect the baseline for the next role, but excessive focus on immediate compensation may cost a more important opportunity to grow.

One warning line deserves attention. If a company believes you can create 50 million yuan in value but will pay only 1 million, become highly cautious.

· If 1 million is the maximum within its compensation authority and the standard for all candidates at the same level, the offer may still be sincere.

· If it is clearly below the market or internal level for peers, think twice.

Career choices have no standard answer, but they can rest on mature frameworks. Apply those frameworks to your situation and a wise decision becomes more likely. Preserve agency and judge through probability and strategy rather than obsessing over the gain or loss of one outcome.

### The Ultimate Compass for Career Choice: Know Yourself

After all these principles and methods, I believe the most important point is to know yourself. Only genuine self-knowledge lets you travel farther—and this is true of every choice in life. One person experiences a job as torment; another savors it. Those who savor it are more likely to persist, travel farther, accumulate more compounding effects, and receive more opportunities. The reason is that the work better matches their nature and strengths and awakens their passion.

Knowing what you want, what you do well, and what you enjoy is the prerequisite for every wise choice. Of course, “know yourself” is a lifelong subject, not something completed overnight. Nor must you wait for perfect self-knowledge before choosing a career. Choice itself can become a process of self-discovery. By trying different jobs, projects, and roles, you deepen your understanding through practice and gradually align work with life.

This chapter contained a great deal of information. Through the five-level framework and more concrete methods, I hope it helps you notice crucial layers previously overlooked, direct attention and effort toward what truly matters, and build your own system for choosing—so that future decisions are wiser and better suited to your life.

## Chapter 12: Capture the 10 Percent of Work That Creates Core Value

This chapter explores how to identify and focus on the 10 percent of tasks that create high core value, leaving inefficient busyness behind.

### Stop Working Blindly: Focus on the Vital 10 Percent

Begin with two common phenomena—two laws of work.

Law one: work automatically expands to fill all available time.

However much time you reserve, an endless stream of tasks will fill it.

Law two: work finds the people who are good at handling it.

Trivial tasks flow toward people who are good at them, or at least do not resist them. Difficult problems flow toward people willing and able to solve complexity.

Given these laws, what does working smart mean? Many people hear “smart” and think of cutting corners, then assume that working smart is the opposite of working hard. This is a common error. The opposite of working smart is working stupidly.

Smart work and hard work are fully compatible and often reinforce one another. Smart work frequently requires hard effort.

What, then, is stupid work? It has two typical characteristics:

(1) Investing too much time and energy in low-value work;

(2) Investing too little in high-value work.

These patterns are especially common among young people entering the workplace because they carry habits formed at school directly into work.

### Two School Habits Lead Us into the Trap of Spreading Effort Evenly

#### Habit One: Equal Effort Everywhere

School subjects—language, mathematics, English, and others—carry similar weights, so we naturally distribute time and energy fairly evenly. Even when one is more difficult, we rarely devote 90 percent of our resources to it and only 10 percent to all the rest.

Bring this egalitarian habit into work and problems arise. The difference in value among tasks is often not 20 percent or twofold, but tens or hundreds of times.

#### Habit Two: Accept Everything and Merely Finish It

School assignments and examinations have clear endpoints; generally, completion is enough. Work has no end. If we continue accepting everything like students, without filtering, refusing, or setting priorities, we invest too much in work of little value and far too little in work capable of immense returns.

The value of tasks in a real workplace varies enormously. One high-value output may be worth hundreds or thousands of low-value ones because much low-value work is worth almost nothing. Yet we may give both categories similar time and energy. This is stupid work: the input appears large while the return is diluted almost beyond visibility.

What does it mean to invest sufficiently in high-value work?

Think of preparing for an examination. If a crucial hard question in a subject you do well remains beyond you, but effort seems likely to conquer it, what do you do? You try repeatedly, use several approaches, consult teachers and classmates, search for materials, and understand it at the root. Once mastered, you practice repeatedly until fluent and able to generalize.

At school, that counts as investment. At work, it is usually only the beginning. The additional return on high-value work far exceeds the benefit of answering one more examination question.

First, high-value work settles into your core competitiveness.

Such work is usually difficult. When you solve problems others cannot and overcome barriers others cannot, the experience and ability become hard-to-copy human capital. Your personal value becomes easier to see.

Second, high-value work compounds opportunity.

Once you complete a high-value task with a striking result, opportunities move toward you. Demonstrate the ability to solve thorny problems, and future work that is valuable, challenging, beyond others, and within your capacity tends to seek you out. A virtuous cycle forms (Figure 12-1).

### Use High-Value Work to Crowd Out Low-Value Work

The two opening laws support two conclusions.

#### Conclusion One: Doing High-Value Work Is the Best Way to Refuse Low-Value Work

If available time will inevitably be filled, occupy it deliberately with high-value work rather than trivia. When you are fully invested in work widely recognized as important and valuable, others struggle to justify interrupting you with inconsequential errands because what you are doing matters more.

People who complain of constant busywork and errands may be doing work without clearly visible value, so others naturally assume that “my request deserves priority.” An effective way out is to begin taking on high-value work now and make its value visible.

#### Conclusion Two: High-Value Work Tends to Multiply

Work finds people who are good at it. Complete one high-value task and you gradually become defined as “someone who can handle important projects.” Similar opportunities then follow, forming a virtuous cycle.

To make the process vivid, bring back Little S and Little A. Little S represents a more proactive and capable person who invests deeply; Little A represents the typical employee.

### Little S and Little A: How Does the Gap Widen from the Same Opportunity?

#### Scene One: Recognizing the Opportunity and Starting

One day, both notice a potentially high-value opportunity. The work is difficult, has no ready-made method, and is filled with uncertainty. Initial attempts produce no result. After hearing their report, the manager says, “You may try, but only in your spare time.”

Little A searches online for an hour and asks three colleagues. Still without an idea, Little A tries during one evening of overtime, makes no progress, and sets the project aside.

Little S’s actions look similar—research and consultation—but the depth is entirely different. Little S may spend ten hours searching systematically, contact thirty colleagues or outside experts, and find a key lead, then work intensely for two weeks to produce an initial breakthrough.

Their starting points were similar, but their investments of time and energy were worlds apart, so the results naturally diverged. As Chapter 10 emphasized, which road you choose matters; how far you are willing to walk often matters more.

#### Scene Two: Setting Priorities During Execution

Suppose Little A somehow keeps pace and both enter execution. The new project now conflicts with existing responsibilities and time becomes tight.

Little A continues working overtime but completes old work first, squeezing the new project into what remains. The concerns are understandable: old work already runs with momentum, the team depends on it, and performance assessment rests largely upon it. Neglecting it may invite criticism or damage the evaluation.

Little S also works overtime but places the new project ahead of the old. The decision is based not on assessment, process, or short-term evaluation, but value. The potential of high-value work deserves full attention, even at the cost of disrupting the old order and bearing the risk of experimentation.

#### Scene Three: Responding to a Bottleneck

As the project deepens, both encounter bottlenecks.

After one or two failed attempts, Little A gives up, escalates for support, or changes direction. This reflects the conventional pattern: if it does not work, take another path.

Little S enters a mode of continual iteration: trying ten or more times, adjusting strategy each time, and reconstructing the path, method, and mobilization of resources until a breakthrough appears. Little S also communicates proactively, coordinates resources, persuades leaders and colleagues, and brings the team into collaboration along the high-value path.

#### Scene Four: Divergent Thinking After Completion

Suppose both ultimately finish the work.

Little A feels relief: “At last—it is one hundred percent complete.” The work ends and it is time to relax.

Little S responds: “This is only one percent.” The basic version exists, but the value has not yet appeared. Little S asks: How can the result grow from 1 percent to 10 and 100? How can it be reused, distributed, and scaled? How can this outcome become the starting point for the next opportunity?

### How Do You Find the 10 Percent of High-Value Work?

Before offering methods, I must emphasize that firsthand insight rarely lives only in books and articles.

Writing can stimulate thought, but high-value information and opportunities are often hidden in a few people’s practical experience, judgment, and tacit details. We do not ultimately need a standard answer, but the ability to turn input into action and results—to investigate, communicate, attempt, and verify.

Three methods are especially useful.

#### Method One: Develop Judgment—in Other Words, Taste

Steve Jobs and Allen Zhang both emphasized “taste.” Here, taste is not merely aesthetic ability but integrated judgment: can you recognize a good problem, solution, or crucial detail, and distinguish apparent effort from real effectiveness?

Develop it deliberately:

· Encounter many cases and practices at different levels.

· Label them as accurately as possible: what is good, what is poor, and where does each apply?

· Continually calibrate your judgment. Ask why, gather evidence first, then analyze causes and review in a structured way.

Over time, a more stable and explicit system of judgment forms.

#### Method Two: Maintain a Non-Consensus Pattern of Thought

Many important opportunities come from being correctly non-consensus. This does not mean contradicting others for sport. It means thinking independently from facts and logic, resisting the crowd, and finding a better path outside the mainstream.

When sufficient evidence leads you to a conclusion different from the majority’s, you possess a non-consensus view. Others may question or ridicule you. If correct, however, its value is usually greater than a mainstream choice because you saw an unnoticed opportunity earlier or found a method better than current best practice.

The prerequisite is independent thought and rigorous logic. Being different merely for the sake of difference is another form of following the crowd.

#### Method Three: Communicate Proactively and Build Information Channels

Communication is often essential to high-value information and opportunity. If you have not held deep conversations with enough people but easily conclude that the direction is unclear and no one will guide you, you probably have not invested enough.

Communication includes high-quality input from books, blogs, interviews, and podcasts, but it must ultimately return to your actual work and become usable understanding and action through digestion and practice.

Prioritize conversations with people “two levels up.” They need not be your manager’s manager; they may work across levels and departments but stand closer to the core objective and possess a broader view. They focus more on final outcomes, and high-value work connects directly to outcomes, so they are more likely to understand and support you.

Approach conversations with an altruistic mindset. Do not merely take; first ask what you can provide. Sincerity, curiosity, and willingness to help make trust and honest information easier to obtain.

Capture both “hard information”—industry developments, methods, experience, and lessons—and “soft information”: what the other person actually cares about, their standards of judgment, and unspoken concerns. Soft information often helps more in advancing collaboration, setting priorities, and anticipating resistance.

Finally, act immediately. Remember three recommendations.

First, combine quantitative and qualitative thinking.

Data matters, but crucial judgments about taste, experience, risk, trust, and collaboration cost cannot be fully quantified. High-value opportunities often hide in these ambiguous but essential spaces. Use data for optimization and qualitative thought for key decisions; neither can replace the other.

Second, think long term and set a reasonable time horizon.

Finding and completing high-value work never happens in one stroke. Developing judgment, accumulating relationships and resources, deepening understanding, securing support, and reaching the objective all require time. Across several companies, I usually needed about a year to enter a stable state of high-value output. Six to twelve months is a reasonable evaluation period.

Third, act first and optimize through action.

I repeat this point whenever training newcomers. The voice saying “I cannot,” “It is too hard,” or “What will others think?” is often not truthful. It is the brain’s protective mechanism when experience is thin. Learn to recognize and ignore it, then begin. You may fear that speaking with someone two levels above you will be difficult; after doing it, you may find it far simpler than imagined. True failure is not trying without succeeding, but never beginning.

Remember: when hesitation, fear, or laziness prevents you from pursuing high-value work that you should explore and master, you are working stupidly. You surrender your most precious time, energy, and attention to low-value work and miss the chance to change your professional trajectory.

If you are reading this book to change your circumstances and break through mediocrity, do not wait. There is no need. Begin now.

## Chapter 13: See the Essence of Business—the Core Laws of Growth

This chapter explores the underlying logic of markets and business. As the saying goes, “A true teaching fits in one sentence; a false teaching fills ten thousand books.” I have compressed the most fundamental lessons from years of practice into three sentences. They provide a foundation for understanding markets and business and for building your own commercial worldview.

### Revisit “Growth”: The Key to Career Development

Before revealing the laws, let me restate a practical premise: promotion and higher pay fundamentally depend on growth. This is why I repeatedly emphasize the framework of the Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, and Method. Whether the system around you grows often determines the ceiling on your own growth.

#### Method—Personal Ability—Is Constrained by Command—Leadership

Your ceiling for advancement and pay depends largely on how quickly your leader advances. It is difficult to move above your own leader. A leader’s promotion may carry you upward; their stagnation may block you. Within the leader’s own room to develop, their evaluation of your work and the opportunities and resources they provide determine perhaps 80 percent of your career development. Even someone with only a year or two of experience can see which weighs more by observing the relationship between Method and Command.

#### Command—Leadership—Is Constrained by Earth—the Company and Business

A leader’s ceiling is determined by the company’s business development. A stagnant business can offer little substantial upward space. When the business expands rapidly, a leader’s responsibilities naturally grow, followed by a higher level and organizational restructuring. The leader’s development is constrained by the company’s.

#### Earth—the Company and Business—Is Constrained by Heaven—the Industry and Macro Environment

The limit on company and business growth is strongly shaped by industry conditions and the macro environment. If you remain rooted in real estate today, even a hard-working team faces uncertain prospects. Ten years ago, by contrast, industry growth came almost naturally. Industry and macro conditions define the boundary of company development.

#### Heaven—the Industry and Macro Environment—Is Constrained by the Way—Understanding and Mental Models

In every era, some people succeed, make money, and realize personal value and ideals. Even during Japan’s “lost thirty years” from 1989 to 2019, companies such as Uniqlo rose. Some people see Heaven, Earth, Command, and Method clearly and put them to use. You cannot choose your era, but you can improve your understanding, see the era clearly, find the appropriate business and people within it, develop corresponding abilities, and seize your own opportunity.

The ceiling at each level is often set by the level above. Position, platform, and timing frequently influence the upper bound of a career more than effort alone. “Growth” usually has two meanings:

· Inner growth: stronger ability and judgment, and changes in mental models.

· External growth: richer outcomes, greater influence, and higher income.

They affect each other. Inner growth determines whether you can seize opportunities and deliver; external growth tests whether inner growth is effective. Personal development, team expansion, company growth, and industry evolution are not isolated events, but links in one continuous value chain. Focus only on self-improvement while ignoring the growth logic of platform and business, and you eventually hit a boundary.

### Understanding the Laws of Growth: The Underlying Logic of Markets and Business

If growth is crucial, how do companies, products, and businesses achieve it? Understanding the laws helps identify what is genuinely worth doing, whether for workplace advancement, entrepreneurship, or a side business.

When I moved from Amazon to Facebook, now Meta, I lacked a clear view of the underlying logic of markets and business. Amazon operated many diverse physical businesses, while Facebook seemed to be merely a website, so I assumed Amazon’s prospects must be better. Yet I sensed that the matter was not so simple and that I should join Facebook to discover why its workforce was one or two orders of magnitude smaller while its market capitalization was in the same order. Within a year, I understood that the answer centered on business model and profit margin.

I summarize the underlying logic in three laws:

· Product determines profit;

· Profit drives scale;

· Efficiency determines success or failure.

#### Law One: Product Determines Profit

Traditional economics often defines profit as price minus cost. In modern business, that is insufficient. A more realistic pair of variables is customer acquisition cost, or CAC, and lifetime value, or LTV.

For one user, the approximate profit space is LTV minus CAC, less the cost of delivery.

Modern markets overflow with products, and consumers no longer buy on value for money alone. Luxury prices cannot be explained merely by production cost plus reasonable profit. The CAC/LTV view reveals more:

· CAC tends to converge. In the same channel, traffic pool, and competitive environment, bidding and imitation pull competitors’ acquisition costs into a similar range.

· LTV creates the real difference. The total amount a user continues spending with you depends on purchase value and retention. If you acquire customers at roughly the same cost as competitors but produce a higher LTV through higher prices, more frequent repeat purchases, or longer use, you gain greater profit and more capacity to expand.

Three common factors produce high LTV and profit:

(1) Industry characteristics and business model. Some industries naturally possess better profit structures. Games, for example, often enjoy high retention and payment rates.

(2) Brand and position in the customer’s mind. A Starbucks premium pays not only for coffee, but for identity, predictable experience, and social context. Customers often buy not merely function, but self-positioning and others’ perception.

(3) Product quality and delivery. Beyond brand premium, reliable quality and a good experience remain hard strengths. Even at the high end, quality and stability determine whether customers stay.

The key to profit is therefore not cost control alone, but whether business-model design, brand building, and product strength create higher LTV.

#### Law Two: Profit Drives Scale

Profit is not only an outcome, but a prerequisite for expansion. Sustained expansion depends heavily on sufficient margin to cover acquisition, delivery, and iteration.

First, growth requires acquiring customers, and acquisition costs money at every stage: awareness, conversion, and retention.

Second, marginal CAC often rises. Early customers lie close to the core audience and convert easily. As scale grows, you must reach peripheral customers who are harder to persuade, raising CAC.

Third, high profit creates room for error. With a low margin, expansion stops as soon as marginal CAC crosses a threshold. A high margin means the company can afford experimentation and continue adjusting while it scales.

Two dynamic observations clarify the law.

First, concessions are also CAC. Using lower prices or higher subsidies to increase penetration converts potential profit into acquisition spending. If you could earn ten yuan from a user but choose to earn only three, the remaining seven can be viewed as long-term CAC purchasing reputation and retention.

Second, financing does not contradict the law. Outside capital can expand a company in the short term, but it advances money against future profit. Whether the model ultimately works still depends on unit economics and profit structure.

#### Law Three: Efficiency Determines Success or Failure

Many people understand a “moat” as a first-mover advantage. Apart from legally protected assets such as patents and a few irreproducible secrets, however, most business advantages are dynamic. Even giants can be challenged. What matters is who sustains greater efficiency.

Efficiency does not mean busyness or doing more. It means continually producing more effective value under the same resource constraints: lower cost, higher quality, faster delivery, and better experience. Greater efficiency produces higher margins; profit drives scale; scale attracts more resources—talent, data, and capital—which further improve efficiency. A virtuous cycle forms and the winner grows stronger.

Do not mistake movement for progress. Real efficiency contains at least four elements:

· Efficiency creates a flywheel. Behind it lies the evolution of a system. Once a product develops user stickiness and data feedback, a self-operating ecosystem forms. A later product may be better and still fail to match its speed or break established habits.

· Efficiency is the ability to allocate resources. It does not mean doing more, but betting resources on the critical path so that investment compounds.

· Efficiency requires exploration and iteration. Repeating something one hundred times without improvement is mechanical labor; correcting a little with each repetition is compounding growth.

· Efficiency ultimately lands in execution. Strategy that cannot be implemented equals zero; fierce execution in the wrong direction merely fails faster. Correct strategy multiplied by efficient execution becomes results.

The three laws support one another. Understanding them improves your judgment of direction and investment in a complex, uncertain age.

### Return to the Present: What Should You Do Now?

Once you understand that product determines profit, profit drives scale, and efficiency determines success, use the five-level framework to ask: What should I do now?

A realistic and effective answer is: find every way to help your direct manager—Command—succeed.

Only when achieving the team’s objectives genuinely becomes the center of your work will your actions and attitude naturally display cooperation and ownership. A leader can sense this. You become more likely to enter the core circle of collaboration and receive trust and important opportunities, a necessary condition for becoming a promotion candidate.

More importantly, when you play an indispensable role in helping a leader achieve key objectives and grow the business, that leader will often need capable partners like you to continue alongside them. Helping your leader succeed can raise your own ceiling. This is worthwhile and within reach now. For the long term, reflect on two questions:

· What single undertaking is most worthy of your long-term investment? Does it deserve a lifetime of time and effort?

· Have you begun preparing around that objective? If not, today is the starting point.

May this chapter help you see the logic behind personal development more clearly: less blind effort, more forceful breakthroughs.

## Chapter 14: Cross the Barriers and Understand the Real Market

At work, understanding the real market means understanding what the company, your boss, and the broader employment market want. This chapter provides a practical method.

### You Are the Greatest Barrier to Understanding the Real Market

Many people assume the largest barriers are information asymmetry, insufficient resources, or lack of money. My conclusion is more direct: the largest barrier is often you.

This appears in four forms:

· Wishful thinking: you prefer the market to be what it *should* be rather than learn what it actually is.

· Subconscious resistance: reality may make you uncomfortable because it requires action. Action creates anxiety, so you subconsciously avoid the truth.

· Limited perspective: you view the world only from your small circle and naturally receive a partial picture.

· Ineffective exploration and measurement: you are willing to explore but do not know how, and afterward do not know how to measure, review, and correct.

#### Barrier One: Wishful Thinking

First internalize an observation: perhaps 80 percent of our judgments about markets come from subjective assumptions. Test yourself with two questions.

· What kind of person do you think your boss or the market needs? Does the answer align closely with your own experience and strengths? Do you subconsciously assume the market “should” want someone like you?

· When judging whether a product will sell, do you begin with “What advantages do I have?” and “What can I make?” rather than “What does the market actually need?”

The problem is hard to see in imagination, but becomes fatal when you actually launch a business, change roles, or seek a job. Many projects fail not for lack of execution effort, but because the market was never understood at the outset. Success occurred only in the creator’s imagination.

Try an exercise.

Suppose you may place only three photographs on a dating app. You are highly educated, good-natured, financially secure, experienced, and funny—in short, you have many strengths. Which three photographs maximize the probability of attracting your target partner?

Write down an answer first. The exercise is not about dating technique. It forces you to confront the possibility that even in important and common situations, your intuition and the market’s real demand may differ enormously. Perhaps you have never seriously studied what the other person actually values.

The same applies to jobs and products. Do not begin with “What do I have?” Begin with “What signals does the other side use to decide whether I am worth choosing?” The three photographs for a job are your résumé, portfolio, and interview presentation; for making money, they are product benefits, page copy, and pricing package.

Wishful thinking arises because we begin with the self rather than the market. Avoiding it requires first-principles thinking and intellectual honesty.

(1) First-principles thinking: derive conclusions again from fundamentals.

First-principles thinking discards appearances, convention, and untested assumptions. It returns to the essence of a problem and derives conclusions from more basic facts. It emphasizes evidence and logic while reducing dependence on experience, authority, and traditional beliefs.

Its characteristics include:

· Rejecting untested consensus and convention;

· Not readily accepting authoritative advice, experience, or conclusions;

· Deriving conclusions from basic facts;

· Returning to principles and constraints;

· Exploring multiple paths before choosing.

Think of it as writing a proof: begin with limited premises and reason rigorously toward a conclusion, not from “someone said” or “everyone does it.” The following are habits, not first-principles reasoning:

· Copying by analogy: “Someone else succeeded this way, so I will copy it.”

· Dependence on experience: “This is how I have always done it.”

· Assumption-driven reasoning: “If the market is like this, then…”

· Appeals to authority: “An expert said…” or “The book says…”

· Applying best practice: “The industry’s standard method must be optimal.”

You can often tell whether someone is reasoning from first principles. If their language begins with what others say or how things were done before, it is probably habitual thinking. If they begin by breaking down the problem’s constraints and deriving a conclusion, it is closer to first principles.

To understand and practice this further, study discussions by Bridgewater founder Ray Dalio or Elon Musk and observe their paths and systems of logic.

(2) Intellectual honesty: do not distort yourself in the face of evidence.

Intellectual honesty is rarer than intelligence or ordinary personal honesty. It means minimizing the distortion of evidence and reality by bias and position, not avoiding conflict, and not binding self-esteem to an opinion.

Common signs include:

· Admitting the boundaries of your knowledge and errors in judgment;

· Treating evidence for and against your position equally;

· Recognizing that bias is universal and correcting for it actively;

· Pursuing clarity, rigor, and transparency in expression;

· Allowing yourself to say, “I am uncertain” and “I do not know.”

Many people can nominally accept criticism, but instinctively avoid, refute, or deny unfavorable evidence. They scrutinize contrary evidence mercilessly and accept supporting evidence easily, mistaking stubbornness for dignity.

Ask yourself:

· Am I deliberately avoiding unfavorable evidence or different views?

· Will I admit a gap in my knowledge or an error in judgment?

· Am I trapped in emotional or label-driven thinking and drawing conclusions from it?

· When facts conflict with my prior position, can I remain open?

No one achieves perfect intellectual honesty in every situation. What matters is the continuing reminder to correct ourselves.

#### Barrier Two: Subconscious Resistance

Even after recognizing wishful thinking, you may fail to avoid it because you subconsciously fear change and prefer comfort. People capable of genuine growth often share one trait: they seek feedback and use it for calibration.

“Feedback is a gift” appears in many corporate cultures. It is not romantic, but practical. Most people’s actual condition is different:

· They fear feedback;

· They dislike feedback;

· Their first response is defense: “You misunderstood,” “That is not what I meant,” or “It is not like that.”

Why?

They prefer the comfort zone to real market demand and fear exposing weaknesses. Admitting a weakness requires change, and they do not want to change.

Your first product will probably contain many problems. If you seek feedback, you sincerely ask what fails and how to improve, accept advice humbly, and make people willing to advise you again. If you resist it, your words say “Tell me anything,” while your tone says “Do not say anything unpleasant.” The other person offers valueless suggestions and you lose the real information. At the extreme, you immediately argue or become angry and treat advice as an insult.

This pattern traps people in wishful thinking. Worse, the subconscious justifies it in a subtler way: it depicts growth and risk as moral problems and packages inaction as principle. “Those insiders flatter the boss and surrender their dignity.” “Those people know only how to curry favor and steal credit; principled people like us cannot do that.”

Attaching a negative moral label to successful people lets you explain lack of growth as “having standards” and provides comfort. It neither improves ability nor moves you toward market truth.

From first principles and intellectual honesty, what is an insider? At most, someone able to form a community of interest with the boss around crucial objectives. This is not inherently a moral matter. Your more likely problem is that even if you wanted to become such a partner, insufficient ability and reliability would prevent it. That is the pain point.

Without puncturing this moralized self-protection, genuine growth remains difficult.

#### Barrier Three: Limited Perspective

After crossing the first two barriers, you become more willing to act. What limits you then is often not how, but not knowing what to do. The cause is a limited perspective.

Elsewhere I have described “32 Important, Invisible, Controllable Factors of Success.” They expand perspective, and training them produces substantial progress.

(1) Self-knowledge and mindset:

· Know, accept, and be yourself—build your career on real strengths and passion rather than playing a role that is not yours.

· Growth mindset—believe ability and intelligence improve through effort and learning rather than remain fixed at birth.

· Open-mindedness—welcome new ideas and knowledge even when they conflict with deeply held views.

· Intellectual honesty—pursue truth, admit error and ignorance, and refuse self-deception.

· Curiosity—maintain intense interest in how the world works and keep asking why; this is the engine of learning.

· Conviction—think deeply before deciding, then have the courage to maintain your position through difficulty.

· Responsibility for yourself—fully accept your choices and results without blaming the environment or shifting responsibility.

· Learn from mistakes—treat every failure as an opportunity to gain valuable experience, not a stain to hide.

· Equanimity amid uncertainty—remain calm and decide even with incomplete information and an unclear future.

· Long-term thinking—prefer enduring value and growth to immediate gains.

(2) Ways and principles of working:

· Work smart—focus on core tasks producing the greatest value, not on looking busy.

· Find the right direction—ensure effort is applied to the right thing; this matters far more than doubling effort on the wrong one.

· Care deeply—attend to the work, the details that determine quality, and refine it repeatedly.

· Act on your word—turn ideas into action quickly, because execution is the only bridge from idea to result.

· Truly want it—possess a deep desire for the goal that becomes your strongest force for overcoming difficulty.

· Be reliable—do what you say so others can trust your promises without reservation.

· Take initiative—find problems and opportunities and move matters forward without prompting.

· Be resourceful—adjust quickly to surprises and resource limits and find inventive solutions.

· Think from first principles—strip away appearances, find the essence, and discover breakthrough solutions.

· Know what you know and admit what you do not—honest boundaries earn trust and accelerate learning.

· Manage time and energy—treat them as your most precious assets and invest them consciously in what matters most.

(3) Human interaction and influence:

· Find supporters—build a network of mentors, colleagues, and friends who offer advice and help at critical moments.

· Influence without authority—use expertise, character, and communication to win support from people who do not report to you.

· Communicate effectively—convey information clearly, accurately, and empathetically so that the other person truly understands.

· Build relationships—invest in sincere, reciprocal connections, because opportunities often live between people.

· Be kind and generous, and share credit—genuinely wish for others’ success, help them, and attribute team achievements to the group; this earns enduring respect.

· Be altruistic—begin by creating value for others, the team, and customers; your own value will ultimately be expressed most fully.

· Be candid and genuine—offer honest feedback directly while showing care and respect, enabling shared growth.

· Be customer-centered—always begin with customer needs and experience, the ultimate source of commercial value.

(4) Creating and seizing opportunities:

· Learn the business—master not only your profession, but the operating model of the whole business and the company’s strategic direction.

· Learn for life—make continual learning a way of living that meets a changing world and profession.

· Create luck—increase the probability of good fortune through more action and attract opportunities through a strong reputation.

#### Barrier Four: Ineffective Exploration and Measurement

Once willing to broaden your view and act, learn a method. It can be summarized as a cycle:

Explore: enter the fog and collect real information.

Form a hypothesis: propose an explanation based on the information.

Measure: design a way to test it.

Learn: review the result, adjust the hypothesis and direction, and begin another cycle.

Measurement deserves emphasis. Many people explore without measuring and remain forever at the level of feeling. Measurement need not mean cold numbers from a ruler. Qualitative records and reflection also count. Restating what you learned in your own words and recording which ideas changed and why is effective measurement.

### Practical Exercise: What Kind of Subordinate Does a Boss Actually Need?

Apply the method to a concrete question: what kind of subordinate does a boss actually need?

The standard answer is unimportant. What matters is using the method to break down the question; otherwise the method remains unusable theory.

Many people immediately answer, “The boss cares only about insiders.” Beyond that, their image of what a boss wants usually falls into four types:

The obedient type: cautious, rule-bound, and unquestioning. Inner monologue: “If I obey, the boss will like me and I will be safe.”

The flattering type: agrees with everything. Inner monologue: “Flattery at least keeps me out of trouble.”

The omnipotent type: strives to be the most capable and brilliant. Inner monologue: “I must prove that I am the strongest.”

The visibly hard-working type: works desperately late and makes sure the boss sees it. Inner monologue: “It is enough that the boss thinks I am working overtime.”

Ask honestly whether one describes you.

From a manager’s perspective, what a boss actually needs is often the evolved form of each.

Obedient → independent-minded and able to execute. A boss does not need an echo. An intelligent employee without a position of their own creates insecurity. A boss wants someone who voices disagreement and holds a view during discussion, then executes firmly once the decision is made.

Flattering → business-focused. The flatterer attends to the boss as a person; the boss would prefer attention on the business. The object of your concern should not be the boss’s mood, but the business result.

Omnipotent → collaborative and growing. A boss rarely expects someone to do everything from the start. More important are teamwork, learning potential, and steadily increasing reliability. To demonstrate growth, let others see that you admit weaknesses and can iterate and improve.

Visibly hard-working → outcome-oriented. A boss ultimately looks not at how much an employee suffered, but at how much verifiable value they produced for customers, projects, and the team. What matters is not self-perceived effort, intelligence, or obedience, but the ability to deliver value, keep growing, and produce closed-loop outcomes. What bosses actually want often differs sharply from what employees imagine.

### How Do You Interact Effectively with Your Boss?

After understanding the general need, apply it to your situation and discover what your particular boss wants through continual exploration and awareness.

One of the most effective practices is regular, high-quality one-on-one communication. This is not a one-way status report, but a two-way flow of information that clarifies the cooperative relationship:

· What support do you want from the boss—resources, priority, feedback, or opportunity?

· For which outcomes does the boss expect you to be responsible—metrics, project success, or key deliverables?

· Do you share the same definition of “done well”?

Express your career objectives clearly as well. Let the boss know where you want to go, which opportunities you need, and which outcomes you will own. Treat the boss as a collaborator: the boss has objectives and needs your help; you have objectives and need resources and feedback. Only a win-win relationship supplies enough motivation on both sides.

Why does a boss sometimes withhold support? Often not from stinginess, but because you have not demonstrated a sufficient return on investment. Through proactive, reliable performance, show that one unit of resource invested in you produces several units of value.

Understand the boss’s situation and anxiety. To whom are they accountable—one level above, two levels above, or peers? Which result matters most? What do they fear and value? Some concerns may be irrational but remain real. The better you identify them, the more effectively you avoid creating trouble, fill gaps, and add value.

Three questions can begin an effective one-on-one. Use them flexibly rather than as ritual:

(1) What is the single result you most want me to deliver this week or during this phase?

(2) What is your standard for “done well”—quality, deadline, and priority?

(3) What kind of support can I receive from you to do it well—resources, feedback, coordination, or opportunity?

(2) How do the requirements of the employment market while job hunting differ from the boss’s requirements after you join?

(3) Why does this book appear relatively unconcerned with the distinction between market and company requirements? Hint: consider the book’s central objective.

The process of answering matters far more than the answers. Copying an answer is useless. What matters is whether you can break down the questions, derive answers yourself, and put them to use.

## Chapter 15: A Method for Building Products—Using the Résumé as an Example

This chapter addresses a key subject: how to build a product. A “product” is not only a physical good or service. It also includes you. In job hunting, the résumé is your most direct product form.

Using this familiar tool, we will unpack the logic that makes product building effective. Begin with three suggestions for improving a résumé:

· If your real ability is worth 80 points, the résumé should communicate at least 80. Many people’s résumés show only 50 or 60.

· To move from 80 to 90, editing the document is not enough; you must change the substance of your real work. What you can write depends on what you accumulate every day.

· Reaching above 90 depends not on templates or stacked certificates, but on a virtuous cycle of professional growth: continually upgrading experience, ability, and outcomes, with the résumé merely presenting them.

General advice and templates can help by repairing weaknesses in expression. But the question here is not how to write a résumé. It is why most résumés—the admission ticket to a career—fail even to pass.

### Why Résumés Fail

A résumé distills the core achievements of your career and opens the first door to an ideal role. It should receive exceptional care. Yet many read like diaries or private monologues, hastily written and without focus.

Why?

They expose the most common product-building error, including in personal brands: beginning with yourself instead of the market. We become absorbed in “what I did,” “how hard I worked,” and “how much I suffered,” but seldom ask:

· Does this information matter to the reader?

· What does the reader most want to confirm in a short time?

· Have I made the key point clear?

This is not only a résumé error. It often lies at the root of stalled career development. Without understanding the market, we fall into the illusion of “I think this is good” and “I assumed this was important.” Our perspective remains limited and iteration ineffective. The résumé is a classic specimen of this build-in-isolation bias.

If you have invested heavily in your résumé with mediocre results, the problem may not be poor expression. You may never have understood how the job market works or what companies and interviewers actually value.

Five misconceptions commonly explain why much effort produces no effect.

#### Misconception One: I Assume They Will Understand

You may assume the interviewer will infer value that you did not write. Because an experience matters to you, you expect the reader to see it the same way and complete your logic automatically.

In reality, they may spend only seconds on the résumé. Unless you first adopt the interviewer’s perspective—what can they see, and what do they want to see?—the clues and setup you imagine may not exist for them.

#### Misconception Two: I Wrote a Lot

This follows directly from the first. Seeking completeness, you stuff every experience into the document until it becomes dense and overloaded. You focus on whether everything is included, not on what the interviewer actually sees.

An interviewer may review dozens or hundreds of résumés a day. However much you write, if they scan only headings and two lines, the rest was wasted.

#### Misconception Three: Misunderstanding What Is Impressive

At a résumé workshop in my community, one participant was a technically strong chief data scientist. Her résumé said, “I built a machine-learning model that helped the company make an important decision and increased profit by X percent.” That sounds good. Yet for many mid- and senior-level roles, the interviewer may care more about whether you exercised judgment and made tradeoffs at critical points, drove the decision, accepted responsibility, and produced verifiable impact. The impressive element is not what the model improved, but the data scientist’s judgment and execution. The résumé should emphasize her, not the model.

If that still seems counterintuitive, apply the intellectual honesty from the previous chapter. Could someone else perform the items you listed? If simple training would enable another person to do them, you are not very irreplaceable—and therefore not so impressive.

#### Misconception Four: No Objective, Only Imitation

Have you seriously researched whom the target company and role are hiring? Did you begin from their real needs?

Many people copy an attractive template or edit a friend’s résumé. The result looks like everyone else’s and lacks a target. Worse, if you have not decided what role you want, even an exquisite résumé may represent effort in the wrong direction.

#### Misconception Five: No Everyday Accumulation, Then Panic When Writing

The classic case is trying to recall the past only when a résumé becomes necessary, discovering there is little to say, then assembling fragments and flat descriptions of responsibilities. Highlights are not written into existence; they are accumulated and built in everyday work.

Recognizing the problem is the first step. Now use product thinking to reconstruct your professional assets and solve the lack of highlights at the root.

### Two Life Scripts: The Résumé Stories of Little A and Little S

The two characters possess completely different approaches to product building.

#### Little A: The Head-Down Worker Who Begins with the Self

How does Little A build a résumé?

Step one, inventory possessions: education, internships, projects, responsibilities, certificates, and skills all go into a pile.

Step two, polish the package: find templates, learn tricks, adjust layout, and replace ordinary words with “high-level” ones so the résumé looks professional.

Step three, optimize internally: filter, choose, and rearrange the existing material into the most presentable story, listing every contribution to the business.

Little A appears diligent, but the résumé is only a refined account of existing experience. If the market threshold is 80 and the relevant abilities are worth 10, no amount of optimization may produce more than a résumé worth 20. The target remains distant.

The problem is direction, not packaging. Without knowing what the market wants, the likely next move is mass application. Without a target, Little A casts a net and hopes for luck. The résumé usually disappears without response. Even if luck produces a job, it may be unsuitable, unenjoyable, or misaligned with ability. Over time, Little A enters a cycle of greater confusion while working and greater anxiety with every move.

#### Little S: The Precise Builder Who Begins with the Market

Little S devotes substantial time to two key stages.

First: self-positioning plus market research. Little S clarifies three things:

· Where exactly do I want to go?

· Which transferable capabilities do I possess?

· What is the target role’s real threshold?

Then comes research around the target role and company:

Sample the market: examine the backgrounds of people previously hired for comparable roles and identify common traits.

Seek advice: approach industry practitioners, paying if necessary, conduct mock interviews, and obtain real feedback.

Clarify demand: determine the actual requirements for hard skills, soft skills, knowledge, and talent.

Second: build the “product” of the self to demand. Once demand is clear, effort becomes targeted.

The résumé is customized around the role rather than copied from a template.

Daily work deliberately accumulates material that can become value for the target role.

Projects, activities, and public output outside work all serve the target direction.

Little A may study skills and earn certificates blindly without learning whether they truly add value to the role. Every step Little S takes closes the distance to the target.

This targeting creates a large gap in intense competition. In 2024, Statsig advertised one new-graduate data scientist role and received more than 3,000 applications in two days. Only 2 percent were clearly targeted; the rest were generic. Naturally, we continued screening only within that 2 percent. At the initial stage alone, Little S’s probability of success would have been more than fifty times Little A’s.

Job hunting is not filtering your existing experiences; it is building a product fitted to demand. You do not sell your past self through a résumé. You construct the future you who fits the role.

### The Secret of Success: Do Work That Is Truly Valuable

The difference between Little S and Little A is not diligence, but whether key time and effort are invested correctly in more valuable places.

At school, everyone studies the same subjects, encouraging the belief that one unit of effort brings one unit of reward. From the beginning, this book has emphasized reading the language of the market and choosing carefully. Income and growth speed are determined not by intensity of effort but its effect—by where time, energy, and attention go.

Little S is more likely to succeed because every investment follows one line: invest around what the market actually needs.

Researching demand means understanding the market.

Editing the résumé means matching precisely.

Improving skills means closing the gap.

Much of Little A’s work is building in isolation. A résumé polished in the wrong direction is ineffective optimization; skills developed without a target are resource misallocation; untargeted mass applications waste opportunities. The market pays for none of them.

Connect this to earlier chapters:

Chapter 8: poor investment decisions are the greatest potential risk—Little A invests time and energy in the wrong places.

Chapter 9: personal value = understanding the market + building the product + using leverage.

Chapter 12: ineffective work overinvests in low-value tasks and underinvests in high-value ones.

Finding the main line in your work and shifting time and energy toward high value is the fundamental path to greater efficiency, income, and growth.

As Chapter 8 noted, every one of us makes bad investments continually. Keep a beginner’s mind and examine and adjust deliberately.

That is the deeper lesson behind Little S and Little A. Understand the market, then build the product. This is not only the logic of business, but the central logic of planning a career and managing a life.

### Practical Guide: Build the Product Around the Market

Whether creating a résumé or a commercial product, use the same method.

Step one: understand the market as fully as you reasonably can.

This does not mean spending forever. It means genuinely acting. Have you spoken with “customers”—hiring managers at target companies, employees in the role, and senior practitioners on the team?

Without conversations with a group of high-quality target users, it is difficult to claim real understanding. Go out, ask, and listen. This is the first and most important step.

Step two: quickly make a product that can be evaluated.

Based on your initial understanding, build something testable: a targeted résumé draft demonstrating core strengths, or a portfolio piece. Its purpose is to test whether your understanding of the market is correct.

Step three: release and test, gather feedback, and iterate quickly.

Your central hypothesis should be: “I believe the market needs A, and my product satisfies A.” Put the product into the market and let reality answer yes or no through action. The market is often poor at expressing clearly what it wants, but excellent at saying what it does not want. Once shown a concrete product, it communicates through negative signals: no interview, no next round, no response. Silence and rejection all say, “We do not want this product.”

You enter an evolutionary loop:

**Form hypothesis → make product → release and test → obtain feedback → adjust and iterate → test again**

More mature research frameworks for validating demand can help remove noise and obtain useful information.

Through the loop, you remove what the market does not need and approach a solution it will pay for—with an opportunity, an offer, or a purchase. Will revolving around the market make everyone identical?

No. The market tells you only that something is wrong; it does not teach you exactly what to do. The résumé or portfolio ultimately accepted is a distinctive solution refined through your own understanding, judgment, and creativity while receiving negative signals.

This explains a subtle but important fact. I cannot readily give you one universal path to making money, but I can give you a method for finding a path. It must be internalized through your own action, error, and iteration before it grows into your ability.

Again, no fixed path remains effective for everyone over the long term. The underlying logic of careers and earning can be learned. Do not fantasize about a ready-made route. The book’s greatest value lies in understanding and method; their power depends entirely on your action.

(2) Compared with the three steps above, what were the main weaknesses in how you prepared résumés before—for example, failing to study the market or never testing the finished résumé effectively?

## Chapter 16: Use Leverage to Amplify Influence and Accelerate Growth

This chapter concerns a crucial ability: using leverage. Many people encounter leverage without recognizing it, much less using it systematically. They invest heavily for a limited return.

### The Basic Principle of Leverage and Its Workplace Application

The familiar saying is, “Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.” The same force, placed within the right structure, is amplified. Complete leverage contains at least three elements:

(1) The force you apply: your effort, time, and investment.

(2) The object you want to move: the result you seek.

(3) The lever: tools or resources that amplify the input—people, platforms, brands, mechanisms, and so forth.

Why use leverage at work? Consider investing in a home. Suppose Little A and Little S each possess 1 million yuan.

Little A buys a 1-million-yuan home in cash. Its price rises 20 percent, producing a 200,000-yuan gain.

Little S uses 1 million as a down payment, borrows 3 million from the bank—fourfold leverage—and buys a 4-million-yuan home. The same 20 percent increase produces an 800,000-yuan gain.

You may immediately ask what happens if prices fall. At 20 percent, Little A loses 200,000 and retains 800,000; Little S loses 800,000 and retains only 200,000. Financial leverage amplifies risk and return together.

Workplace leverage often has an important difference: risk and return are not perfectly symmetrical. Success may produce enormous upside, while even failure often has limited consequences. A failed project does not necessarily remove you from the company—provided, of course, that you have not crossed a red line or used reckless leverage in a highly regulated, high-responsibility situation.

Counterintuitively, project failure can sometimes contribute to promotion. What organizations lack most is the drive to take ownership and venture forward. When you assume a high-leverage task, the organization pays a costly price for experimentation. Economically, the mistakes you encountered and experience you gained are assets embedded in you. Rather than waste that investment, the organization may invest further and let you realize the “dividend of failure” on a higher platform.

In many workplace situations, taking on high-leverage tasks is therefore not high-risk, but potentially a low-risk, high-return strategy. Once we see this, we can discuss how to add leverage.

### Finding Leverage: Anchor the Objective Before Deciding Whether to Apply It

The three elements are objective, lever, and force. The most important first step is defining your objective and aligning it with that of whoever provides the leverage. Workplace leverage is rarely an indifferent tool. It is a resource with intentions and preferences—your boss, colleagues, or other teams. Why should they become your lever?

The central condition is that what you want to do is also what they want. When your objective is precisely one your boss strongly desires, you use the boss as leverage while the boss uses you to reach the objective. Both win.

### High-Leverage Situations: Different Degrees of Care Produce Vastly Different Effects

When will a boss consider it worthwhile to give you leverage? Usually when the task lies in a high-leverage situation, where the degree of care you bring dramatically affects the result.

Suppose a product needs promotion and you must write a social-media article. This is a classic high-leverage situation because traffic multiplies quality. The potential audience is enormous—a good article can escape its initial circle—and platform algorithms send high-quality content to more people. Quality increases both conversion per user and the number reached.

Excellent work may require several times the time and care of merely checking the box, yet produce an impact several orders of magnitude greater.

A 60-point article makes no splash. An 80-point one may perform reasonably. Moving from 90 to 95 is extremely difficult, but may create orders-of-magnitude differences in distribution. Content returns are often superlinear. Most articles cluster near 80; a 90 may receive ten times the views, and a 95 ten times more again. Those last points are difficult and potentially immensely valuable.

A boss then becomes more willing to add resources. A little support might lift your article from 90 to 92—two points for you, but possibly an enormous incremental return for the boss.

Not all work deserves leverage. Ask which tasks need only pass and which become more valuable the better they are. The second group deserves your focus.

Two further points matter.

First, effective quality is judged by the market, not by you. A carefully produced video may fail while someone else’s casual clip goes viral. Feeling wronged means failing to see the matter’s multiple dimensions and most important one, and confusing input with result.

Second, care is essential. High-leverage work is often difficult precisely because quality is hard to measure and improve. There is no formula to copy mechanically. A thoughtful person must study and attempt it repeatedly before discovering the key. Whether you truly care determines whether quality can keep improving and earn a superlinear return. Not all work deserves leverage, but work whose result changes radically with care absolutely does.

### How Do You Make Leverage Work?

A boss can usually provide:

· Reward and recognition for contribution;

· Resource coordination across departments and removal of collaborative obstacles;

· Greater visibility—the most overlooked form—by communicating your contribution to senior leaders and key stakeholders at the right time and in the right way.

Not every boss is naturally good at increasing visibility. You may need to remind or even teach them. Make your work easy to explain: your key role, difficulties encountered, support needed next, and value to the objective.

One director I worked with was especially skilled at this. He helped me and other colleagues by distilling our work into stories and naturally telling them to his own leaders at meals and meetings. Once your manager’s manager pays attention, the space for coordinating resources and the leverage both expand greatly.

What must you do for the boss?

First, state the objective and request clearly. When objectives align, candor builds trust. Explain what return you seek—promotion, growth, or something else. A boss fears someone who claims to want nothing while privately calculating otherwise.

Second, define responsibilities and deliverables. Specify what you own and will produce rather than offer a slogan. In cross-team work, clearly divide your responsibilities from others’. Express a resource request as incremental return: the resources move the result from 80 to 90 or 95. Show the path from investment to payoff.

Typical high-leverage situations include valuable internal projects that are hard to move; opportunities that quickly raise market value, such as promotion or cross-disciplinary work; fast-growing fields such as large AI models; opportunities to build influence through public speaking or flagship projects; and tasks that rapidly build core skills with observable success or failure.

Business history repeatedly shows that a successful person’s core team rarely descended from heaven. They are often the people who fought the earliest hard battles and faced the most challenges together: the early teams at ByteDance and Sina Weibo, Didi’s original growth team, or the ministers and generals around Liu Bang, many of whom came from Pei County. Fighting together may bring financial rewards; more importantly, shared experience creates deep trust and durable connection. Those high-quality relationships become future leverage.

### Recognizing Leverage: The Value of the Asset

Return to the mortgage. Many people think borrowing capacity depends on willingness and ability to repay. Those are only basic conditions. The core determinant of leverage is what you can pledge—the value of the asset. A bank lends against a home because the home itself has value. If it does not, the bank scrutinizes your personal ability more strictly or refuses the loan.

The same applies at work. Choose the right objective and work on something inherently worthwhile, and trust and support become easier to obtain even before you are perfect.

Say, “Boss, give me a chance; if I fail, I’ll work overtime to repay you,” and the boss becomes nervous: “I cannot wait for you to compensate later.”

Say instead, “This project is worth doing. I lack experience, but I am committed to making it work. We can test one round first.” If the boss agrees on its value and sees you as the person most likely to succeed, approval becomes more likely.

Ability still matters. But when project value is high, direction correct, and organizational demand strong, the decision gives more weight to whether the opportunity deserves a bet than to whether you are flawless from the start.

High leverage also requires two attitudes.

First, do not fear rejection.

You need only one success in the search for leverage. Good opportunities are scarce because they cannot be given easily to everyone. Fear of rejection removes most candidates.

Second, dare to take responsibility.

Resources flow toward people willing to own the result. The more responsibility you accept, the easier it is to earn trust and leverage.

Other common levers include the company brand, personal brand, content assets such as articles, videos, and code, high-quality relationships, and industry trends.

“High-quality relationships” do not mean the number of prominent contacts in your messaging app or celebrities in a group. They mean people willing to endorse you, offer opportunities, and entrust resources to you. Why are they willing? Because of your outcomes, value, and contribution.

I know leading AI experts and investors who recognize me because I have carefully operated a high-quality community. Three practices made it work:

First, I strictly filter topics and maintain discussion quality so valuable content continues to appear.

Second, I continually contribute content myself.

Third, I keep inviting experts from many fields, persisting even after rejection.

### Applying Leverage: Direction and Force

One element of leverage is force. Here, force has at least two components: its direction and its strength.

#### Direction: Judgment Arising from Conviction

Judgment creates direction; only with correct direction can leverage work. Stand before a lever without knowing what to move—or push in the wrong direction—and effort is wasted.

Judgment arises fundamentally from understanding the objective and inner certainty: conviction. Without conviction, every difficulty triggers doubt: “Is it worth it?” “Did I misjudge?” What you doubt is your own judgment.

Experience and learning can create clear understanding and firm conviction. Once the direction is settled, difficulty no longer asks whether to continue, only how to solve it.

Judgment appears in three areas:

Timing: the same proposal is accepted from one person and ignored from another; timing matters.

Priority: with ten possible project directions, decide what comes first, when to ask for help, and how to divide work.

Strategy: the final result you seek determines resource allocation and the narrative.

All require deep knowledge of the work and conviction supporting your choices. Understanding the objective comes first.

#### Strength: Inner Power Determines Whether You Act and Persist

Inner power determines how far you travel. It includes action and endurance.

· Action: do not merely think; keep moving.

· Endurance: can you act today and tomorrow, and persist for one year or ten?

Real inner power appears as resilience, persistence, and decisiveness. People with strong execution, tolerance for pressure, and decisive judgment usually possess it; its soil remains conviction.

Recall the high-value task completed by Little S and Little A. Little A said, “Done—one hundred percent.” Little S said, “This is only one percent; ninety-nine remains to explore.” Little A saw the immediate task; Little S saw its long-term value. People willing to invest for the long term, keep refining, and bear greater responsibility more readily earn trust, resources, and larger leverage.

### The Foundation of Leverage: Enduring Aspiration

Beyond the three elements lies something deeper: enduring aspiration. It is neither fleeting interest nor impulse, but a stable inner drive. You continue when progress is smooth and still persist without results or through setbacks.

Wang Yangming described it as “holding one’s aspiration as though the heart were in pain”—an inner force impossible to ignore. Aspiration begins action and anchors judgment and choice. Decisions about what to do, what not to do, when, and how much ultimately rest on what you genuinely want. If the aspiration is false, vague, or weak, inner power wavers and judgment distorts. The clearer it is, the steadier you remain through difficulty.

Suppose someone is poor at public speaking but truly wants to help more people through professional knowledge. Setbacks hurt but do not make them quit. They practice, study, and try again because weak expression is only a technical problem to solve. Another person whose motive is recognition through speaking may abandon the effort as soon as delivery is poor and response weak. Different origins produce entirely different responses to difficulty.

Consider Lei Jun’s move into automobiles. Observers notice commercial sensitivity and judgment, but underneath was confirmation of aspiration. Before deciding, he researched extensively and examined his motives repeatedly until willing to make this his final startup and a long-term undertaking. Once aspiration was established, doubt and difficulty ceased to be reasons to withdraw and became practical questions of execution.

Aspiration shapes intention, determines resilience, and supports the removal of apparently insurmountable obstacles. The clearer the reason why, the more likely you are to act firmly and continually until the work succeeds.

### Beware and Use Wisely: The Two Sides of Leverage

Leverage used correctly accelerates; used wrongly it amplifies error. If the objective is wrong, leverage merely carries you faster in the wrong direction. If force is applied poorly—pushing before preparation, depending excessively on outside resources, or placing yourself beyond control before ability is ready—the lever may crush rather than help. Standing briefly in a position mismatched to your ability may look glorious, but the gap eventually appears and the cost grows. Reality contains many cases of position exceeding merit.

### Treat Your Personal Brand as Portable, Long-Term Leverage

Using people and tools to move a project is valuable, but more important is accumulating a portable long-term asset: your personal brand.

This is not simply becoming a social-media persona or seeking constant exposure. At its core are the methods, outcomes, and abilities accumulated in concrete projects, and the stable way others understand you. Reviewing proactively, organizing material, and summarizing experience develop ability. Turning that accumulation into visible artifacts—a clear report, persuasive presentation, or effective working model—creates visible value.

When you continually produce and communicate results appropriately until people associate you with a particular kind of work, you own a label: strategically perceptive, strong in cross-functional collaboration, able to tackle hard problems, insightful about users, or skilled at process design. The label itself matters less than its clarity, specificity, and scarcity. Inside an organization, labeled people more readily receive resources, trust, and fitting assignments.

Some people rush to display themselves before developing substance. In personal branding, “accumulate first, express second” is nearly an iron rule. Workplace output often arises from team collaboration. Distinguish company and project property from personal lessons you may retain and share. Publicizing blindly before trust exists may provoke doubts from a boss or partner and cost you the leverage already granted.

First ask how collaborators and stakeholders can continue benefiting. Only their benefit sustains your leverage. Then consider what you can accumulate, what you can say publicly, and how.

After all methods, strategies, and techniques, return to the Way. Judgment, inner power, and aspiration rest on deep self-knowledge.

Without understanding what you truly desire, aspiration lacks sincerity, inner power cannot last, and judgment drifts. You may sprint toward another person’s definition of success only to discover that the entire road was wrong. Many say, “I just want to make money,” then find after earning it that anxiety has not decreased and happiness has not increased. Money was not the true desire, but a projection of security or freedom. By the time they realize this, they may have run too far along the wrong track to turn easily.

#### Exercise One: Identify and Design Your Leverage Strategy

## Chapter 17: Communication—the “Meta-Skill” of the Workplace

Earlier chapters examined how to understand the market, turn yourself into a product by converting ability into deliverable outcomes, and use leverage to amplify influence. One thread connects them: communication.

Communication tests your understanding of the market, makes the product of your work visible and usable, and lets you combine other people’s time, resources, and trust into leverage.

### The Value of Communication and Mistakes in Learning It

Communication is a classic meta-skill. More than an independent ability, it connects market understanding, delivered outcomes, and mobilized resources and lets them compound. Influence, project execution, trust, resources, and opportunity ultimately depend on it.

To leave a routine track and create something genuinely valuable—to make work produce visible outcomes rather than merely finish tasks—you must communicate effectively. The mechanism is straightforward: it makes your judgment understandable, your proposal supportable, collaboration smoother, and value easier to see.

Yet most people receive almost no systematic communication training. We confuse communication with slick talk or social maneuvering and attach negative labels such as flattery or “all talk.” Some conscientious people with real ideas therefore avoid it and believe silence is golden. At crucial moments, they cannot explain their thoughts or obtain deserved support. They are misunderstood or ignored, and over time their value remains unrecognized.

Effective communication is not rhetoric. It is expressing your genuine self, clear ideas, and logical judgment in a form others understand and want to hear—so that problems are solved, relationships built, and work advanced.

We will examine three questions:

· Why are many people poor communicators?

· What is communication actually for?

· How do its levels—and the levels of listening—work?

Treat communication as a craft and keep refining it through practice.

### Why Are So Many People Poor at Communication?

I see four common reasons.

First, the influence of a single evaluation system.

We grow accustomed to scores determining everything. A score on paper looks objective and clear, so communication seems unimportant: good grades appear to need no explanation, advocacy, or coordination. We let report cards speak for us and ignore a crucial reality. In society, even an excellent idea loses value if it cannot be communicated clearly. Scores show command of knowledge, not the ability to use it to influence others and solve real problems. Neither people nor tasks carry visible scores. Value depends on others’ judgment, and that judgment depends heavily on communication. School mainly rewards solvers of individual problems; workplaces reward many solvers of collaborative problems.

Second, homogeneous environments make us underestimate the difficulty.

Many people share similar backgrounds, education, language, and values. In a highly homogeneous environment, we understand “roughly enough” without explicit speech. Enter cross-functional, cross-cultural, cross-city, or cross-class settings and “roughly enough” immediately fails. Communication’s value becomes visible.

Third, we understand the purpose too shallowly.

Many assume communication means clarity: better structure, sharper focus, more persuasion. These are important but basic. Focus only on technique and deeper problems remain: why does a clear explanation fail to move the work? Why does the same content win support when someone else presents it?

Fourth, we learn in the wrong way.

Communication is not mastered by reading. It requires repeated training in real situations. Online tips do little when an actual high-stakes interview, negotiation, or conflict forces the brain to handle the problem, read reactions, and manage distractions at once. There is no spare capacity to recall a checklist.

A better route is practice—real communication or realistic simulation—repeated until techniques become natural responses.

### The Real Purpose of Communication: A Deepening Structure

Clear expression alone is insufficient. Workplace communication has descending layers; the deeper layers are more vital and determine performance above them.

· Level one: express clearly. Others at least understand the words. This accomplishes only information transfer.

· Level two: solve the problem. Advance the project, coordinate responsibilities, secure resources, or resolve disagreement. Once the problem is solved, elegance matters less.

· Level three: clarify value. Do others know what you solved, why you solved it, and what the solution is worth? If not, you may still have worked for nothing.

· Level four: signal position and intent. People use communication to decide whether you are ally or opponent, collaborating or taking credit. Once they know you stand on the same side, work becomes easier.

· Level five: build trust. Without trust, all earlier effort may be wasted.

· Level six: gain identity acceptance. Beyond trust, the other person mentally categorizes you as “one of us,” “reliable,” or “worth a long-term partnership.” This creates sustained influence.

Focus only on clarity and confusion follows: Why does clear speech fail to move things? Why does the same content win support from someone else? Why did I do the most but someone else receive recognition? The deeper goals were not achieved.

Different situations emphasize different levels: reporting focuses more on levels two and three, negotiation on four and five, and long-term cooperation on six.

### Common Communication Hazards

(1) Speaking without listening. Beginning with output keeps you at level one. Deeper aims such as trust and identity acceptance are achieved through listening.

(2) Rushing to express yourself. A mind full of “I think,” “I believe,” and “Why don’t you understand me?” signals indifference to the other person’s goals and circumstances, so they lose interest in understanding you.

(3) Lack of confidence. Opening with “I may explain this poorly,” “I do not understand much,” or “I have not thought it through” weakens your position before communication begins.

(4) Failure to focus. Moving from A to B and around to C loses the main line and the listener’s patience.

(5) Relying on scripts. Mechanical “high-EQ phrases” may sound smooth while making the atmosphere wrong.

(6) Ignoring context. The same words produce radically different effects with different settings, relationships, and timing.

(7) Fear of conflict. Avoiding what must be said may prevent short-term awkwardness while accumulating a larger problem. Conflict handled well can deepen a relationship.

(8) Excessive defense or emotion. Treating advice as criticism, disagreement as a personal attack, imagining insinuation, or fearing stolen credit makes communication impossible.

### Deepening the Communication Pyramid—from Solving Problems to Clarifying Value

#### Level Two: Solve the Problem

In teams, people often complain, “I did all the work and others took the credit,” or “My teammates are useless.” These may be true, but ask a more valuable question: did you find a way to make others willing to participate, accept responsibility, and achieve the result?

Often you possess ability but cannot mobilize resources or allocate work through communication. If you bury your head and do everything, others naturally stand aside. One key to motivating participation is making success meaningful for them too.

When seeking resources, most people say, “I need your help,” “This is urgent,” or “Do me a favor,” perhaps adding, “You are the best and most reliable; I cannot manage without you.” This may work briefly. A more effective communicator reframes it: helping me also helps you accomplish something valuable. Ask: What is your objective? How does my work move you closer to it? What standard must I meet before you are willing to help?

Reporting also matters. A report reveals whether someone truly controls the situation. A good report is not a diary, but gives listeners clear judgment and expectations. It should be:

· Candid and clear: state what is moving, what is blocked, current progress, and the next step—without painting everything rosy, reporting only good news, or merely complaining.

· Fair with credit: explain collaborators’ contributions so people feel treated justly.

· In control of risk: identify risks and response paths early so stakeholders know what to expect and can mobilize resources quickly.

· Clear about meaning: explain why the work exists and why this approach was chosen.

· As a bonus, educational: listeners leave understanding the key point and perhaps learning a method.

#### Level Three: Clarify Value

After improving at solving problems, ask whether others felt your value and whether you explained it clearly.

Two mistakes are common.

(1) Saying nothing. You believe that doing good work is enough and that gold inevitably shines, expecting others to understand and appreciate you automatically. You cannot assume everyone possesses the discernment to discover quiet talent. Entrusting your career to waiting for someone who understands has poor odds and reflects the object mindset.

(2) Trying too hard. Turning small matters into great achievements and claiming credit constantly provokes dislike and distrust.

Do not seek a midpoint between the two. “How hard to push” is itself the wrong frame. Take the listener’s perspective: what do they care about and seek? Describe your contribution and value from their objective.

Suppose Little A and Little S are data analysts who spent a long time cleaning a dataset for a partner.

Little A says: “I worked overtime for days and finally cleaned the data. Here!” The message emphasizes personal hardship and requests emotional recognition. The partner may accept the data without valuing Little A more.

Little S says: “You mentioned problem X. I investigated and found the root cause. This cleaned dataset should help you solve it quickly.” The partner hears that Little S remembered the pain point and produced work that solves it. Little S could create this feeling because of listening and remembering. As the personal value formula says, first understand the market—the other person’s need—then build the product—your work and communication. Understanding the market requires listening.

Little S did not mention overtime. Does that hide the extra effort? Results usually matter more than input. Often it is more effective for a colleague or boss to add at the right moment, “Yes, it looks simple, but Little S worked late for several days and found many hidden problems.”

There are situations when you should explain input yourself: aligning expectations, explaining risk, seeking resources, or correcting a misunderstanding. Judgment determines when.

### Listening: the Beginning and Core of Advanced Communication

Listening has four levels:

Understanding: I grasp and can restate your view, and you confirm that I understood correctly.

Appreciation: I also understand your mental models and values and can identify what is worth learning.

Empathy: I can feel your emotion and am willing to care for your feelings.

Evaluation: only after the first three do I offer judgment or advice.

The most common and destructive error is skipping the first three and moving directly to evaluation.

Understanding appears simple but is difficult. We often grasp only surface meaning or mistake intention. A real case, shared with permission, illustrates this.

#### Case: A Conversation with a Community Moderator

A user posted in a content-sharing section to organize an offline gathering; the rules required such posts in the discussion section. The moderator wrote: “If everyone is just organizing a meetup, it would be best to post in the discussion area and leave the resources of the content-sharing area to users with concrete content to share, or you can post a summary here afterward.” Note the tentative particles and the word “resources.”

I gave the moderator three points of feedback:

(1) The direction is correct, but the tone and perspective do not sound like a moderator and may make the reader uncomfortable.

(2) “Resources of the content-sharing area” sounds like “you are unworthy of using this” or “you are wasting it,” which feels aggressive.

(3) I offered an alternative: “The content-sharing area is mainly for sharing content. For organizing a meetup, please post in the discussion area [rules link]. You are also welcome to return after the event and share a summary here; that fits the purpose of this section and readers enjoy it.”

The moderator’s initial understanding was, “Understood—the word choice was careless.” This captured only “resources.”

I explained that wording was not the only issue. Tentative particles made the message sound unsure and the reminder exploratory. More importantly, the moderator had not established the identity of moderator.

I asked for a restatement. The response was: “I have not truly placed myself in the moderator role. I still passively resist taking the initiative to guide and serve, so I phrased it as a suggestion.”

I corrected again: not adopting the moderator position was close, but “passive resistance” was personal projection. My real point was that the moderator seemed embarrassed to remind someone, and did not need to be. The key was to state the role, provide the basis, and speak clearly—for the poster, the community, and the moderator.

I entered our conversation into AI, which produced a summary. With its help, the moderator gained a new insight: deep listening means not only absorbing information, but distinguishing personal judgments. Interpreting “you need not feel embarrassed” as “your motives are being questioned” was the moderator’s own judgment. Recognizing that deepened the understanding of communication.

The case shows that understanding has levels and full understanding is difficult.

We usually treat listening as the beginning: understand the other person, then express and act better. From another angle, it is also a ceiling, because it determines whether you can build trust and achieve identity acceptance.

Consider the classic sales challenge, “Sell me this pen.” The wrong approach immediately praises the pen’s quality, prestige, and price. The right approach uses questions and listening to discover the real need. Does the person even need a pen? If not, do not force the sale. Genuine listening does more than collect information; it helps reach communication’s ultimate goal.

### The Ultimate Goal of Communication: Identity Acceptance

If someone accepts your identity psychologically—reliable, trustworthy, worth a long-term partnership—many later actions become easier to accept. They may even fill gaps in your reasoning and rationalize it for you. Without that acceptance, impeccable logic and disciplined action may still encounter resistance.

· When accepted, even a flaw receives understanding: “This person is always reliable; there must be a reason.”

· When rejected, even perfection attracts suspicion: “They are showing off again” or “They want to steal credit.”

The social-deduction game *Werewolf* illustrates this vividly. It appears to test logic and expression, but victory often depends on whether others classify you as “one of us.” Once you enter the trusted camp, your words are readily believed; once suspected, even clarity is distorted through the filter.

Identity acceptance cannot be built by declaring, “I am a good person.” It arises from long-term reliable behavior, stable habits of cooperation, alignment at crucial moments, and showing that you understand the other person’s objectives and will accept responsibility for shared goals. A master communicator is not someone who speaks beautifully, but someone others willingly treat as one of their own.

### Communication Is a Lifelong Practice

Communication is a meta-skill with a layered structure: clear expression, problem-solving, clarified value, stated position and intent, trust, and identity acceptance. High-quality communication often begins with listening and depends on recognizing levels of understanding and subjective judgment.

It is not a trick learned once, but a framework of capability. It connects cooperation, trust, and opportunity and determines whether your value is seen, recognized, and amplified.

This concludes one stage of our exploration of core workplace capabilities. The next chapter enters a new field: investing.

## Chapter 18: Investing—Avoid Traps, Build a System, Understand the Market, and See Through the Noise

This chapter addresses an important but often frustrating subject: investing.

Investing matters because, once you understand its underlying logic, your assets can begin to appreciate on their own, creating a virtuous cycle in which money generates more money. Over the long run, investment skill can reduce your dependence on trading time for income and give you more freedom of choice. Yet investing is troublesome for precisely the same reason: the temptation is great, and so is the potential cost. Without sound judgment and methods, wealth accumulated over many years can disappear in a short time. In more serious cases, a bad decision can lead to debt, psychological distress, and even instability in everyday life.

Investing is a vast and difficult field in which genuine knowledge coexists with enormous noise. The more important and complex a field is, the more time we must spend building our own framework instead of outsourcing decisions to emotion, tips, or other people's opinions.

One warning deserves emphasis: this chapter will not turn you into an expert investor overnight. Claims of “getting rich quickly” or “guaranteed profits” usually conceal a trap. Even advice from a legendary investor cannot ensure that every decision will be right. Investing is an expensive discipline: you can lose not only money, but also time, attention, and confidence.

The goal of this chapter, then, is not to offer a “can't-lose strategy,” but to help you:

· recognize and avoid common traps so that you pay less unnecessary tuition;

· build a basic investment framework that you can refine over time;

· gradually replace following the crowd with your own judgment, and luck with experience you can review and learn from.

To organize these ideas systematically, I revisited my old study notes. During my PhD in economics at Cornell University, I specialized in empirical corporate finance and did extensive theoretical derivations and model-based training. But the real world is far more complex than any textbook. Many theories are logically rigorous yet can fail—or even mislead us—in actual markets.

The key to investing has never been merely knowing. It is knowing what to do and within what limits a principle applies. Beyond my academic training, I have learned from accomplished practitioners, tested ideas in practice, and distilled some investment methods of my own. I will share them here.

This chapter condenses what I consider the three most useful academic concepts and three pieces of practical wisdom.

Academic concepts—the theoretical foundation:

(1) The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH).

(2) The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM).

(3) The Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model.

Practical wisdom—the rules of the real world:

(1) How finance professionals get paid: management fees and performance fees.

(2) Perspective determines strategy: allocate and evaluate from a strategic point of view.

(3) Where excess returns come from: distinctive channels and social resources.

We will then explore what excess return, or Alpha, means; what market risk, or Beta, means; and whether ordinary investors can find an Alpha of their own—and, if so, how to build it.

### Three Academic Concepts: Understanding the Market's Underlying Logic

Let us begin with three theories that may sound dry but are essential.

Theory provides the foundation for a coherent mental model. Without it, techniques and tips remain disconnected fragments; you cannot form a complete map of the terrain or make independent judgments. These theories are also the common language of finance. Understanding them helps you understand how finance professionals think, whether you plan to work with them or face them across the table.

Each of the following theories can take weeks to teach properly in business school. Here I will compress them heavily, focusing on the central ideas rather than the mathematical derivations.

#### The Efficient Market Hypothesis: Markets Are Not Easy to Beat

The central claim of the EMH is that market prices promptly and fully reflect all publicly available information.

Suppose some public information could reliably and repeatedly generate profits. Rational market participants would quickly trade on it until the opportunity embedded in that information was exhausted and the price once again reflected what was known. The public information would no longer offer a durable source of excess return.

For example, suppose public information suggests that Asset A is underpriced. Investors buy it, driving up its price until it is no longer “cheap.” The market has absorbed the value of that information.

Two points are crucial to understanding the EMH.

First, it concerns public information. Trading on inside information is illegal and lies outside the scope of this chapter.

Second, market rationality is not the same as individual rationality. A common criticism is that “people are irrational, so markets cannot be efficient.” But the EMH does not require every individual to be perfectly rational. It assumes that the aggregate behavior of capital tends toward rationality. More rational and professional institutions with sophisticated models and information channels—quantitative funds and hedge funds, for example—may profit from irrational money. In doing so, this “smart money” corrects at least some short-term pricing errors.

The EMH offers ordinary investors at least two lessons.

First, information is incorporated into prices quickly. When public information gives you an investment idea, pause and ask whether the market already knows it. If it does, more sophisticated participants have probably acted on it, and the information is likely already priced in. Little room for arbitrage may remain.

Second, delayed information is one reason ordinary investors buy high and sell low. By the time they realize that an asset seems undervalued, the market may already have repriced it upward. By the time they recognize overvaluation, the decline may already be well underway. Failure to understand both the EMH and one's own informational lag helps explain much herd behavior.

The EMH has a familiar corollary: the Random Walk Hypothesis (RWH). Given all historical information, it says, short-term price movements are random. This is particularly important for investors who put excessive faith in technical analysis. Many try to predict future prices from candlesticks, golden crosses, death crosses, and other historical patterns, but very few earn stable long-term profits this way.

The reason is straightforward. If observing historical charts could reliably predict the future, that pattern would itself become public information and the profit opportunity would quickly be traded away. It is usually easy to fit a seemingly successful rule to time-series data. More often than not, however, the rule is an elaborate reading of noise rather than the discovery of a real signal.

The conclusion: complicated chart patterns are generally not a reliable way to predict markets consistently.

#### The Capital Asset Pricing Model: Does Return Come from Risk or Skill?

CAPM's central idea is that an asset's expected return is positively related to the systematic risk it bears. In simplified form:

Expected return on an asset = risk-free rate + *β* × (expected market return − risk-free rate)

The risk-free rate can be approximated by the return on the safest government bonds or bank time deposits.

*β* (Beta) measures how sensitive an asset is to movements in the overall market, such as a broad market index.

*β* > 1: the asset is more volatile than the market.

*β* < 1: the asset is less volatile than the market.

*β* = 1: the asset's volatility is comparable to the market's.

“Expected market return − risk-free rate” is called the market risk premium.

For a rough intuition, assume the risk-free rate is close to zero. Then:

Expected return on an asset ≈ *β* × expected market return

CAPM offers two main lessons.

First, it gives us another way to think about the source of returns. In addition to company fundamentals and growth, CAPM suggests that an asset's return above the risk-free rate can often be separated into the overall market return and the asset's sensitivity to market fluctuations—its Beta. This matters because our counterparties may be making decisions through a similar framework.

Second, it reminds us to practice attribution and avoid mistaking Beta for Alpha. In a rising market, high-Beta assets may gain far more than average, but that does not make us investment geniuses. When we profit, we should ask whether the return came from the asset's distinctive strengths—possibly Alpha—or from a rising market or sector, which is mostly Beta or compensation for risk. When the market turns, high-Beta assets may also fall much further.

CAPM rests on Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT). MPT's central idea is to reduce risk through diversification—not merely by “buying more things,” but by lowering the correlation among assets. Suppose Assets A and B each have an expected return of 10 percent, but their prices do not move perfectly in sync: their correlation is less than one. Investing only in A or only in B exposes you to all of that asset's risk. Splitting your capital between them leaves the portfolio's expected return near 10 percent, but because their fluctuations partly offset one another, total portfolio risk falls significantly. MPT seeks a portfolio of assets with different correlations that either maximizes return for a given level of risk or minimizes risk for a given return. This is the idea behind the “efficient frontier.”

In practice, correlations often arise from deeper shared risk factors: industry, macroeconomic conditions, policy, and so on. Owning shares in two leading companies in the same industry and country may look diversified while leaving you exposed to nearly identical risks. Conversely, technology stocks and shares in a local property company belong to different categories yet may still be highly correlated because both respond to the same macroeconomic forces.

#### Discounted Cash Flow: A Share Price Reflects Future Value

We often assume that the stock of a “good company” ought to rise. In theory, however, a company's quality—especially its future earning power—is often already reflected in today's price. The DCF model explains why.

The current value of an asset equals the sum of all expected future free cash flows discounted back to the present.

In simplified form:

Present value ≈ CF1/(1 + *r*)1 + CF2/(1 + *r*)2 + … + CFn/(1 + *r*)*n*

There are two key variables:

· Future cash flow (CF), which reflects future earnings prospects. Higher expected growth means greater future cash flow and therefore greater present value.

· The discount rate (*r*), which can be understood as the investor's minimum required return. It includes both the risk-free rate and a risk premium, accounting for the cost of time and compensation for risk.

When market interest rates rise, discount rates usually rise with them. Even if expected future cash flow does not change, discounting it at a higher rate lowers its present value—and therefore its share price. This is one reason equities often come under pressure during a rate-hiking cycle.

The DCF model offers at least three insights.

First, interest-rate changes directly affect company valuations. Fundamentals can remain unchanged while a new rate environment moves the valuation. Investing requires us to consider not only a company's growth but also the interest-rate conditions under which that growth occurs.

Second, high growth expectations are often already in the price. When you buy shares in a company with a high valuation, you are frequently prepaying for future growth. If that growth disappoints, the price may correct sharply. Even meeting expectations does not guarantee a high investment return, because the expectations themselves may have been priced in long ago.

Third, dividends usually redistribute value rather than create it from nothing. In theoretical terms, a dividend converts part of shareholders' equity into cash, and the share price adjusts accordingly on the ex-dividend date. In real markets, dividends may send a signal, but in terms of value they are closer to a transfer and distribution.

Taken together, these three theories suggest that:

market prices aggregate the intelligence and contest of many participants, making the market hard to beat consistently;

information travels extremely quickly, and many opportunities vanish in an instant;

investment results are difficult to attribute: profits may come from luck, compensation for risk (Beta), or genuine excess return (Alpha).

### Being Non-Consensus and Right: A Potential Source of Alpha

The EMH says that public information is priced in quickly, making it extremely difficult for an individual to beat the market. But another question remains: what if an investor reaches a conclusion that differs from the consensus and ultimately proves correct?

A non-consensus view + correct judgment = one source of Alpha

Suppose the market broadly believes that the rise of a new model such as DeepSeek will weaken the competitive position of chip giant NVIDIA. Capital flows out and NVIDIA's share price falls. One investor, however, reaches the opposite conclusion based on a deeper understanding of the technology: expanding model applications will increase demand for computing power and deepen reliance on high-performance chips. If that judgment proves correct, the market will gradually revise its expectations for NVIDIA's future cash flow, and the share price will recover or even reach new highs. The investor will have earned a return from being non-consensus and right.

This also helps explain Peter Lynch's argument that individual investors can sometimes outperform institutions. There are at least two possible reasons.

First, individuals may differ in what they can observe and know. Someone working in a particular industry may see real commercial and technological changes earlier and form a non-consensus view, while institutional investors are more likely to consume a homogenized stream of financial-market information.

Second, individuals and institutions face different time horizons and performance pressures. Many institutions are judged on short-term results and cannot hold a position that contradicts the mainstream for very long. An individual who can tolerate volatility may be able to wait until value is realized.

But this must be stressed: non-consensus does not mean correct. Finding Alpha in a contrarian view requires knowledge and judgment, and it imposes costs. You must endure volatility, withstand doubt, and remain loyal to your reasoning at critical moments.

At minimum, you need a deep understanding of market mechanisms, the ability to think and judge independently, and enough psychological resilience to withstand short-term losses and distracting noise.

Holding on while an asset falls 10, 30, or even 50 percent is a severe test of temperament. Understanding a theory is one thing. Uniting knowledge with action in practice is a much deeper challenge.

### Three Pieces of Practical Wisdom: See the Rules of the Game and Human Nature

Alongside theory, we must understand how the real world works and how people behave.

#### How Finance Professionals Get Paid: Management Fees and Performance Fees

Legitimate finance professionals, such as fund managers, earn most of their income from management fees and performance fees.

· Management fees are usually charged as a percentage of assets under management—often 1 to 2 percent. As long as the money remains under management, this income is relatively stable regardless of profit or loss.

· Performance fees are usually a share of profits—often 20 percent, as in the familiar “2 and 20” structure.

It follows that a fund manager's central incentive is to expand assets under management (AUM).

The logic is simple: the more money a fund manager controls, the more fixed management fees they collect. Even if the fund's eventual gains come only from the market rising—Beta—rather than the manager's skill—Alpha—the manager can still share in the profits.

At heart, this is an asymmetric incentive: managers participate in gains but rarely bear losses. A structure that promises a share of the upside without equivalent exposure to the downside inevitably distorts behavior. Behind every recommendation to a client may lie a calculation about expanding AUM rather than maximizing the client's interest.

#### Perspective Determines Strategy: Allocate and Evaluate Strategically

Investment outcomes are shaped by randomness, luck, and market volatility. We should not judge a decision by a single gain or loss. One profitable trade does not prove that a strategy is perfect, and one loss does not prove that it is invalid.

A more robust approach shifts the object of evaluation from outcome to strategy. Focus on the quality of the decision process. Design, execute, and assess the strategy itself. Develop an approach suited to you as soon as possible, then follow it as consistently as you can.

Constantly switching strategies prevents you from building a system and makes it impossible to identify what works over the long term. A strategy needs time and a sufficient sample to be tested. Above all, it must be executable and reviewable.

#### Where Alpha Comes From: Distinctive Channels and Social Resources

Beta is the gain or loss produced by overall market movement. Alpha is the excess return that remains after market effects are removed—the central objective of active investing.

Under the logic of the EMH, any public, transparent, and repeatable method for producing Alpha will quickly be discovered and copied, causing its edge to disappear. More durable Alpha therefore tends to come not from public knowledge or a standard path, but from an individual's distinctive private channels and social resources.

Three common sources are:

Channel advantage: an asymmetry in information or supply. If a special channel lets you acquire an otherwise identical asset or product below the market price, the difference is Alpha.

Relationships: good opportunities created by trust. An example is the chance to make a small early investment in a friend's startup—an opportunity unavailable to most people.

Investment in human capital: leverage through time and skill. You might join a promising but cash-strapped startup and exchange your abilities for options or equity, using nonmonetary resources to obtain a scarce stake.

These sources of Alpha share three traits: they are nonstandard, difficult to copy, and dependent on an individual's particular knowledge and resources. They often form a more enduring and sustainable hidden advantage.

### Inner Discipline: Beware of Cognitive and Behavioral Biases

After learning the theory and the rules, the final challenge in investing is often overcoming our own psychological weaknesses. Behavioral finance reveals recurring patterns of irrational behavior. The following deserve particular attention.

(1) Survivorship bias and overconfidence: seeing only the winners.

When we hear that someone made a fortune trading cryptocurrency or taking a concentrated position, we are tempted to imitate them. We overlook the many distinctive conditions behind that success: ample starting capital, rapid access to information, precise timing, or sheer luck. Seeing only successful cases inflates our estimate of our own odds, leading us to buy near the top and lose our rhythm.

(2) Misjudging probability: overfearing extreme events while neglecting long-term risks.

The media favors dramatic events, making us overestimate spectacular risks while underestimating slowly accumulating but highly consequential ones, such as inflation. Human intuition is poor at grasping differences in probability and scale, which is why it so often fails us in investing.

(3) Loss aversion and the disposition effect: refusing to cut a loss is natural, but costly.

The pain of a loss usually outweighs the pleasure of an equivalent gain. Many people therefore cling to losing assets or even buy more to lower their average cost and restore psychological comfort. But an unrealized loss is still a loss, and it ties up capital that could be used for better opportunities. A classic test is to ask: if this position were cash today, would you buy the asset now? If the answer is no, you should consider selling.

(4) Failing to recognize your psychological limit.

When an account suffers a drawdown or violent swings, our willingness to tolerate pain often proves weaker than we imagined. The result is buying high and selling low. A sound strategy must fit both your financial circumstances and your psychological limits.

To understand these mechanisms systematically, I recommend *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, especially Charlie Munger's analysis of twelve cognitive biases. It rewards repeated reading.

### Moving to Action: Build Your Own Investment Framework and Strategy

We study theory, rules, and psychological bias so that we can make fewer costly mistakes in practice. Specific methods—regular index investing, value investing, and so on—must be chosen according to your own circumstances. There is no universally optimal strategy. The key is to build your own framework and approach on top of sound first principles.

I have five broad recommendations.

(1) Understand yourself deeply and form sound beliefs. Know your risk preferences and the limits of your competence. Build a logically coherent investment philosophy as the foundation for every decision.

(2) Invest enough time and energy in understanding markets. “Enough” matters: do not make a large investment solely because of a friend's recommendation or a news item. Nor should your research be confined to one market or asset. Explore different regions—mainland Chinese A-shares, Hong Kong shares, US equities—and different asset classes, including bonds, funds, gold, commodities, and options, as well as nonstandard assets such as property and opportunities in operating businesses. The broader your field of view, the more likely you are to find opportunities and allocations genuinely suited to you.

(3) Build a strategy gradually from what you understand. Define your approach—regular index investing, a balanced stock-and-bond portfolio, active stock selection, or something else—and understand its logic instead of copying others. If you choose regular index investing, for example, it should follow from your understanding of the EMH, the difficulty of market timing, diversification, and long-termism—not merely because the method “looks simple.”

(4) Reflect, attribute, and adjust in practice. When you profit, ask whether the cause was luck, a favorable market, or a sound strategy. When you lose, ask whether the problem was the strategy, its execution, or an extreme event. Keep the strategy relatively simple. An overcomplicated system is hard to execute and hard to analyze; a simple approach that you understand and can sustain is often more robust.

(5) Keep finding and building your personal Alpha at work and in life. Use distinctive advantages—professional knowledge, industry insight, relationships, and information channels—to discover nonpublic, nonstandard opportunities for Alpha. These advantages are usually more individual and more sustainable.

Recall the personal value formula introduced earlier in this book:

Personal value = understand the market + build products + use leverage

Investing is, in one sense, another application of that formula:

A sound investment strategy understands the macroeconomic environment and the industry, allocates assets scientifically, and allows compounding to work over the long term.

I hope this chapter helps you avoid common traps, reduce the cost of trial and error, form a clear investment philosophy, and gradually build a methodology of your own. Your path as an investor will require continuous learning, practice, and iteration. In an uncertain market, may you keep thinking clearly and let time work in your favor.

## Chapter 19: Making Money Is a Skill to Be Honed

This chapter develops one central idea: making money is not an outcome but a skill that must be learned and mastered.

When people talk about “making money,” they usually think first about how much someone earned—the result—and then about what that person did—the event. Deeper causes are often dismissed as “good luck,” while the repeatable capabilities supporting the result are ignored.

By this point, you should recognize that making money is not accidental. It rests on a craft that can be trained. What, then, lies at the heart of that craft?

### The Essence of Making Money: Create Value and Convert It into Income

The first step in making money is creating value. Economic schools may describe it differently, but they share a basic premise: to earn income, you must provide value to other people and make them believe that what they give up is worth it.

What matters is not how much you have objectively done. It is whether the other person perceives the value you provide as greater than the cost they bear—money, time, or something else. Only when they think, “This is worth paying for,” will they choose to pay.

Reality complicates this seemingly simple principle. Some people do profit through improper means, giving “making money” a negative connotation. Others, afraid of appearing mercenary, avoid talking about money altogether and may even regard earning it as undignified. So we must be clear: creating value is indeed a prerequisite for making money, but it is nowhere near sufficient.

Many capable people genuinely create value yet stumble at the final step: turning that value into actual income. You may work relentlessly to deliver a critical project, know that you made a major contribution, and believe you deserve a promotion and a raise. Yet when it is time to state your case clearly to your manager, you hesitate: “Will I seem greedy?” “What if they say no?” You either speak vaguely or say nothing at all.

In this situation, value was created but never realized. These two steps require different abilities. Being good at the work does not mean being good at securing a fair return for it.

### From the Student Mindset to the Money-Making Mindset: Breaking Three Habits

Why do so many people struggle to realize value? One common reason is that they retain habits of thought formed as students. These show up in three main ways.

#### Dealing Only with Tasks and Neglecting People

Throughout our education, the central task is solving problems and earning marks; human interaction is secondary. In society, however, recognition of value, access to opportunity, and the movement of resources happen almost entirely through people.

#### Waiting Passively Instead of Taking Initiative

Many people are accustomed to completing assigned tasks, which means that someone else sets the ceiling on their value. To break through it, we must proactively discover and create value beyond expectations.

#### Contributing Quietly but Neglecting to Monetize

We may create real value yet lack either the courage or the knowledge to claim a commensurate return.

Breaking these three habits requires three corresponding abilities.

First, the ability to interact with and influence people: understand their needs, establish trust, communicate effectively, and bring about cooperation.

Second, the ability to discover opportunities and value proactively: identify and create points of value without depending on external assignments.

Third, the ability to realize value and build long-term relationships: turn a single contribution into a return, then accumulate enough trust for an ongoing exchange of value.

These three abilities indicate the direction. To make them trainable and repeatable, I will reduce them to two core skills: street smarts and dealmaking.

### Two Core Skills: Street Smarts and Dealmaking

#### Street Smarts: Identify Critical Information and Act amid Uncertainty

To be “street smart” is to perceive the complex realities of an environment, relationships, and available resources, then act effectively despite uncertainty and ambiguity. Street smarts are easily confused with several other abilities, but they differ in important ways.

· Street smarts differ from being book smart. Book-smart people excel at complex reasoning when rules and structures are clear. Street-smart people are better at catching clues, making choices, and adjusting quickly when information is incomplete and conditions are changing. If book smarts are a high-performance processor, street smarts are a sensitive antenna that detects signals and responds quickly amid chaos and change. A good phone needs both a powerful processor and a strong signal.

· Street smarts are not slickness or ruthlessness. They do not mean glib talk or abandonment of principle. They mean finding the most practical and effective response within the rules and constraints.

· Street smarts are not about exploiting loopholes. They emphasize efficiency and resource mobilization under constraints, not breaking the rules to get what you want. They matter most when there is no standard answer and uncertainty is everywhere. Often, what enables the decisive next step is not the elegance of an idea but the ability to turn it into action and correct course through feedback.

#### Dealmaking: Understand, Communicate, and Realize Value

Dealmaking is fundamentally about understanding and exchanging value. Money is only one medium. The core of a transaction lies in recognizing, communicating, and realizing value. Why practice dealmaking deliberately? Because many people in modern workplaces spend years under a fixed-salary model, exchanging one month of time for one month of pay. This gives us little practice in concrete, immediate exchanges of value. Over time, our ability to create value proactively and negotiate its realization weakens or disappears.

Yet the highest levels of workplace ability often reveal themselves in nonstandard dealmaking situations.

Can you identify what the other person truly needs?

Can you help them quickly understand and accept your distinctive value?

Can you move a collaboration forward when the rules are unclear?

Training your dealmaking ability broadly involves the following:

· Need discovery: understand what the other person truly wants, not merely what they say on the surface.

· Value shaping: do not merely possess value; make it visible, comprehensible, and comparable.

· Negotiation and pricing: secure reasonable terms without sacrificing the long-term relationship.

· Trust building: trust grows from a record of promises kept across many small transactions.

· Cooperation design: distill from one collaboration a mechanism for sustainable exchange.

A common mistake is to equate dealmaking with selling. Neither word has a fixed meaning, and discussions of sales often include need discovery, value shaping, trust building, and cooperation design. I use “dealmaking” rather than “selling” to emphasize what happens beyond the sale itself: every transaction also involves insight, design, and positioning.

### Three Dimensions of Street Smarts

To understand street smarts more clearly, I divide them into three dimensions.

#### First Dimension: Observation and Information Gathering

Street-smart people have feelers in many directions. Beyond information and data, they watch user feedback, moves by peers, and subtle signals from competitors. They can detect trends or opportunities in faint clues. This “nose” is not innate; it is intuition built through long accumulation.

#### Second Dimension: Rapid Iteration and Action

Street-smart people dare to judge and act. They tend to start first and refine later. Rapid iteration is not recklessness; it means testing an idea at the lowest feasible cost, gathering feedback through action, and correcting direction in practice.

#### Third Dimension: Integrating Information and Connecting Resources

Street-smart people find opportunities in blind spots. They also connect resources in unexpected ways, combining seemingly unrelated assets into new solutions and opportunities.

Here are two examples.

#### Case One: Networking by Burger, Across a Thousand Miles

I once interviewed a guest named Yang Fan, who urgently wanted to establish a business relationship with Tencent's payments team in Shenzhen. He was in Beijing and had no obvious route in.

The opening appeared on social media. One day, Yang Fan posted a photo of a Shake Shack burger. A friend at Tencent jokingly commented, “Now I'm craving one!” Yang immediately recognized an excellent icebreaker.

The next day, he did something unexpected: he boarded a flight to Shenzhen carrying a large bag of burgers and invited the friend and the team to lunch. In the relaxed atmosphere created by the food, he naturally introduced his product. He not only won their goodwill but also secured a substantive collaboration.

This was not a trick. It was street smarts in action: detecting a social signal, quickly recognizing a potential opening, and creating a connection in a natural, nonintrusive way. The burgers were merely the medium. Behind them was a practitioner's precise grasp of interpersonal dynamics and the alignment of resources.

#### Case Two: Lei Jun Creates Something from Nothing—Twice

Lei Jun's business career is also an example of street smarts applied to a high-level game.

The first instance was building Xiaomi's smartphone business from zero to one. China's handset market was then dominated by telecom operators and international giants. Lei Jun spotted a narrow opening for an “internet phone.” But recognizing it was nowhere near enough. How could a new brand win users' trust? How could it persuade top suppliers to provide components? How could it calibrate its pace amid uncertainty? The answers were not in a textbook. They depended on street smarts in a complex reality.

The same was true when he later built Xiaomi's electric-vehicle business. With the industry's structure unsettled and competition intense, Lei Jun used precise control of timing and momentum to concentrate talent and resources before the new-car launch. This ability to “win before the battle begins” is street smarts evolved to its highest form in a complex commercial setting.

### How to Develop Street Smarts: Start at Work and Review Repeatedly

The workplace is the best place to begin training street smarts because the cost is low, the feedback is fast, and the process is repeatable. Practice in the following ways.

Observe deliberately. Do not look only at the event; examine the interests and deeper motivations behind it.

Investigate motives. Notice the needs behind what people say and do, and understand what they genuinely want.

Run small experiments. Test ideas at low cost and develop judgment through modest opportunities.

Review regularly. Examine the causal links among judgment, action, and outcome, strengthening your ability to transfer experience to new situations.

### Deconstructing a Deal: Four Tests of Value Exchange

The essence of a deal is an exchange of value. To complete one successfully, you must pass at least four tests.

#### First Test: Discover the Need

Understand the other person's system of value. Something you consider excellent will struggle to create value if it does not match their needs and standards of judgment.

#### Second Test: Present the Value

After understanding the need, present your value in a form the other person can understand and accept. They should be able to feel clearly: “This meets my standard and is worth what I must give.”

#### Third Test: Negotiate the Terms

Prices and conditions for nonstandard offerings—consulting and customized solutions, for example—usually require negotiation. Finding the balance among your target price, your walk-away point, and the other party's willingness to pay is a demanding practical skill.

#### Fourth Test: Build the Relationship

Treating a deal as a one-off event wastes enormous value. Use every successful exchange to deepen the other party's trust and build a lasting relationship. Regard each successful transaction as “the first of countless future transactions.” Your conduct will become steadier, and its long-term value much greater.

The central principle is this: a good deal is neither haggling someone down nor deceiving them. It is an exchange based on genuine value that both parties willingly sustain.

#### Case Study: The Promotion Divide in Consulting

My first job was an internship at Boston Consulting Group. By fortunate timing, in addition to my main project, I had the chance to accompany senior leaders in pitching for new business and was temporarily assigned to another core project team.

This combination of experiences taught me early that slide-making, data analysis, and presentations are merely the price of admission in consulting. They are foundational skills. The ability that truly determines how far a consultant can rise is the ability to make deals. Excellent consultants do more than deliver the current engagement. They also display the following qualities.

· Discover deeper needs while serving the client. While executing an existing project, such as an organizational redesign, they detect pain points the client has not clearly expressed—or may not yet recognize.

· Extend value and drive further collaboration. Drawing on their understanding of the business, they propose new solutions and turn a one-time service into a long-term strategic partnership.

· Continue discovering and creating new points of value while serving the current project well. This ability often determines the ceiling of a consultant's career.

Why? Because a consulting firm's revenue and profit depend on both the number and quality of its projects. Winning new engagements is scarce capability; making good slides is highly replaceable.

### How to Train Dealmaking: Start with Small Deals

Begin inside your workplace by treating every initiative as an internal deal.

Discover the need: what pain point will this solve for a colleague or manager?

Present the value: explain clearly how it will benefit them.

Negotiate the terms: secure the necessary time, people, and budget.

Build the relationship: reinforce trust afterward and prepare the ground for future collaboration.

Add two more essential exercises.

Switch roles. Put yourself in the other person's position and ask what they care about most and fear most.

Combine facts with narrative. Reason alone may not close the deal; give the other person a clear vision that makes them willing to commit.

### Long-Termism: The Return on Time

The long-term value of a transaction is crucial.

I once invited Xu Hao, a Silicon Valley executive and visiting lecturer at Stanford University, to share the secrets of career development with my community. One remark stayed with me. He said that when he was young, he knew that relationships and reputation mattered but did not feel their full weight. Looking back twenty years later, he realized that the behavior and impression of someone with whom he had worked only briefly early in his career could still affect him today.

Look across the technology industry. Leaders who grow over the long term and achieve extraordinary results are rarely extreme egoists who repeatedly harm others. In fields that depend heavily on cooperation and reputation, short-term tricks do not carry anyone very far. Time filters for people with genuine long-term value.

### The Ultimate Exercise: Start Nearby and Act Now

Theory must ultimately become practice. Combining street smarts with dealmaking, try these two exercises.

#### Exercise One: Practice Inside the Workplace

(1) Choose an idea or project you want to advance.

(2) Use street smarts to observe the environment and identify the key people and constraints.

(3) Use a dealmaker's mindset to uncover those people's needs and pain points, then shape the value of your idea.

(4) “Sell” the idea to the key people and win their support.

As you carry it out, deliberately deepen the relationships involved and prepare for future cooperation.

#### Exercise Two: Practice Outside the Workplace—Strongly Recommended

Help a small business you know solve a real problem.

(1) Choose a target: a restaurant, convenience store, barbershop, or another small business you visit regularly.

(2) Talk in depth: observe and listen to understand its actual operating difficulties—low efficiency, insufficient foot traffic, excessive costs, and so on.

(3) Design a solution: offer some modest help within your abilities, such as improving the queue, designing a coupon, or setting up a customer registry.

(4) Pitch the solution: explain clearly how it addresses the pain point and produces a benefit. You might offer a free trial or charge a small fee or commission.

The great advantage of this exercise is its extremely low cost of failure. You may be rejected or embarrassed, but each attempt is valuable training in the craft of making money.

Here are two experiences of my own.

Restaurant website project: early on, I noticed that a restaurant's system for taking orders and collecting payment was inefficient—it used Google Forms and Venmo. I offered to build an online ordering website and charged a small commission on orders as a maintenance fee. The experiment worked remarkably well, and the project even became the starting point of my career as a content creator.

Restaurant partnership exploration: I once approached the owner of a popular restaurant to discuss opening a branch together in the United States. No partnership resulted, but our in-depth conversations created a relationship and taught me a great deal. The experience reminded me that many possibilities reveal themselves only after a real attempt.

This chapter has explored two core money-making skills: street smarts and dealmaking. I hope you incorporate them into everyday action, even if you begin with one very small experiment. Through deliberate practice, your craft will grow steadily more refined, and your confidence in your abilities will grow with it. Once the skill matures, making money becomes a more natural result.

## Chapter 20: Stop Daydreaming—Turn the Desire to Make Money into Action

This chapter addresses a widespread but often overlooked question: why do so many people want to make money yet never act?

There is nothing wrong with wanting to make money. The problem is that many people spend enormous energy rehearsing possibilities in their heads and trying to feel prepared without ever taking the first step. You may recognize these self-defeating lines: “I'm not cut out for it,” “I'm not ready,” or “Now isn't the right time.” Some people substitute simulated action for the real thing. They read and listen to podcasts, using endless “learning” to avoid a genuine first move.

These reasons and behaviors may sound sensible, but fundamentally they avoid the uncertainty that comes with action: rejection, judgment, imperfect performance, or disappointing results. Daydreaming is intoxicating because it is cheap and feels good. It creates the illusion that we are moving forward without exposing us to the risks of actually moving. But thinking about making money produces no feedback. Without feedback, there can be no correction or growth.

This chapter will not give you more methods. It will redirect your attention from “think it all through, then act” to “act first, then review.” When it comes to making money, the first step is rarely a perfect product. It is making one real exchange of value happen.

### The First Psychological Barrier: You Cannot Bring Yourself to Ask

Making money means creating value and completing an exchange. The principle is simple, but many people encounter a psychological barrier: they feel embarrassed discussing payment.

Imagine that you are good at editing résumés and a friend happens to need help. You say, “Let me revise it for you. I'm pretty good at this, and I think it will help.” Your friend gladly accepts.

But if you add, “Could I charge you a token ten yuan?” the atmosphere may immediately become awkward.

The discomfort usually has little to do with the amount. It arises because the relationship has been redefined. Once money enters the conversation, help is no longer merely help; it may be interpreted as a transaction. Your motive is placed under examination—by the other person and by you.

Understanding where this psychological barrier comes from is the first step toward crossing it.

### My Journey: From “This Isn't Worth Anything” to Confidence in My Value

To make the psychological difficulty of asking for money more concrete, I will share my own experience as a content creator through several stages.

#### Stage One: A Low Sense of Self-Worth—May 2020 to October 2022

Even after running my media channel for more than two years, I still believed deep down that my content was not worth paying for. At one point, I publicly recruited a partner and promised them all the channel's revenue because I did not believe it could generate meaningful income.

#### Stage Two: Intellectual Agreement, Practical Hesitation—Early 2023

In interviews, I often encouraged other people to commercialize their work—for example, by accepting advertising. But when it was my turn, I always found a reason to avoid trying. Agreeing with an idea intellectually did not mean I was ready, at a subconscious level, to accept that I could charge for the value I created.

#### Stage Three: Experiment and a Shift in Belief—September 2023

Inspired by Bilibili's “charging” feature and YouTube memberships, I decided to experiment with paid content. The process brought a clear psychological struggle. I caught myself wanting to prove to others that “I wasn't doing it for the money.”

On September 9, 2023, I announced a members-only channel. Just five days later, on September 14, the first paying readers sent feedback that moved me deeply. For the first time, I felt clearly that earning money could be positive. It was not a source of shame but a form of recognition.

#### Stage Four: A Coherent Logic and the Value Flywheel—January 2024

I gradually realized that charging is not merely a mechanism for receiving a return. It can also power improvements in the content. Revenue lets me invest more time, better tools, and other resources, which produce higher-quality work that benefits the audience in turn. This creates a cycle: value creation → income → further creation.

#### Stage Five: Speaking Openly and Trusting My Value—End of 2024

Once annual content revenue reached a meaningful scale—about 1.5 million yuan, for example—I could discuss the number openly. I no longer treated making money as a subject to avoid. I saw it as evidence that value had been validated: I had provided something useful and received an appropriate return.

Many people move through this journey from hesitation and resistance to acceptance and confidence. The real turning point is rarely something you finally “figure out” in your head. It is whether you make your first real attempt to charge.

### The Problem Is Not Pricing—It Is the Fear of Being Misunderstood

After learning to ask, many people run into another difficulty: setting a price. On the surface, the question is “How much should I charge?” At a deeper level, it is “Will people misunderstand me for charging at all?” Logically, income comes from value creation. If you create 100 yuan of value for someone, charging 10—or even 90—is reasonable as long as the other person still receives a net benefit. The deal works. This appears to be only a pricing question. Yet as soon as you charge, even a token sum, attention often shifts rapidly from price to motive. Why do you want this money? Are you trying to help me, or are you only trying to profit? You may even begin questioning your own original intent. Money triggers speculation about motives for two main reasons.

First, market noise: information asymmetry and the crowding out of good actors by bad ones.

Consider paid knowledge products. Promises of value are easy to imitate, consumers cannot reliably assess quality before experiencing the product, and refund or regulatory mechanisms may be weak. Many sellers therefore focus on packaging and conversion rather than genuine value; some monetize exaggerated claims and anxiety. In this environment, those sincerely trying to help bear an additional psychological burden: they must continually prove that they are not there to exploit anyone. Decent people often dislike and lack skill at this self-justification, so they simply decline to charge.

Second, social environment and culture: the belief that talking about money poisons relationships.

Many people grow up absorbing the idea that transactions are cold, feelings are warm, and money corrupts a relationship. Among acquaintances especially, any mention of fees, commissions, or division of financial interests causes motives to be scrutinized. Even when your words and intentions align, you cannot make the other person completely certain, and the ambiguity can make you doubt yourself. To avoid misunderstanding and embarrassment, many people shut the door on money altogether.

This is why we so often see capable people who create real value yet struggle to earn a fair return—not because the market refuses, but because they are unable or unwilling to ask.

### Talking about Money Need Not Damage a Relationship: Two Ways to Reduce the “You're Only in It for the Money” Misunderstanding

Here are two ways to address the suspicion that so often follows a discussion of money. The goal is not to prove your virtue. It is to reduce room for speculation about motives so that value can be exchanged more smoothly.

#### First: Reduce Ambiguity—Explain the Motive Instead of Making Others Guess

What matters is not how elegantly you explain yourself, but whether you make the rules clear in advance. Suppose you hold a paid lecture and publicly promise that all proceeds will support a particular charity. You complete an exchange of value while also establishing a positive reputation. More importantly, when participants experience your sincerity and the value of the event, trust begins to form. Later, when you price your own labor in the market, people will find it easier to accept.

You can also combine free and paid offerings. Begin by providing valuable services for free—answering questions in a community or sharing practical resources, for example—so that people see you are not motivated solely by money. At the same time, explain that modest fees support long-term, sustained content production. Use action, not rhetoric, to demonstrate that you are willing to share value and also need a basic return. That balance is the basis of sustainable trust. When people see that you genuinely want to do good work, rather than merely enrich yourself, they can better understand why you charge and are more willing to support you.

#### Second: Build External Anchors of Trust—Do Not Make Your Price Rest on Your Word Alone

Once the market has validated your product or service, charging is no longer an awkward request. It becomes a normal exchange of value under an accepted industry convention. You can create external anchors of trust in several ways.

· Price by comparison. People may not understand an absolute value, but they understand relative value: “Comparable services cost *x* yuan; I currently charge *y*.” That makes the reasonableness of your price easier to judge.

· Explain opportunity cost. Your time, experience, and attention are finite resources. The price does not reflect only this one action; it reflects capabilities accumulated over many years.

· Build trust through genuine testimonials. Third-party assessments are far more effective than declarations you make about yourself. Deliver consistently and accumulate verifiable feedback until trust becomes an asset.

### Seeing the First Step in Practice: Action Produces Feedback

You may understand all of this but still have no finished product. Start with a very small experiment. The goal is not to get everything right immediately, but to make a transaction happen and receive feedback.

One member of my community experimented with inexpensive consultations for people in her industry—a “coffee chat” for 9.9 yuan. The sum was tiny, but the meaning was large: she had taken the first step. Practice then produced feedback. She discovered that the price might be too low and recognized her lack of confidence in her own value, so she gradually adjusted it. That process itself developed her ability.

Another member offered life-coaching services. She actively asked clients for feedback and displayed their authentic comments, using reputation to accumulate trust. She also used tools such as Calendly to show her available appointment times. As slots became scarce, the market naturally received a signal of limited availability. Clients spent less time asking whether it was worth the price and more time asking how to book.

These examples show that if you are willing to begin and take feedback seriously, you can gradually build market recognition and trust. What matters most is not how large you begin, but that you begin.

### Action Decides Everything

This book can offer methods, perspectives, and examples, but external guidance always has limits. The result ultimately depends on whether you move from “thinking about it” to “doing something.” In making money, doing something is not impulsiveness. It means establishing a feedback loop:

You provide value → you attempt an exchange → the market responds → you correct and iterate.

Once that loop exists, you will understand with increasing clarity what you can offer, how to price it, and how to accumulate trust.

The central idea of this chapter is simple: action creates feedback, and feedback creates change.

After reading this chapter, make one small, testable attempt. Even a tiny step has more power than a long period of daydreaming.

## Chapter 21: Find Your Own “Long Slope and Deep Snow”—Persistence and Self-Knowledge

Welcome to the final chapter. We now return to two central themes: long-termism and compounding. In an uncertain world, how do we persist and grow? How do we ultimately find a path worthy of sustained commitment and capable of producing superlinear returns—your own “long slope and deep snow”?

### Compounding Is Everywhere—and Its Power Is Enormous

Any discussion of compounding must include superlinear growth. Its essence is an exponential effect: time combined with a sustained growth rate makes small differences expand continually. Three calculations illustrate the point.

First, steady growth often beats violent fluctuation. For example:

(1 + 20%) × (1 + 20%) > (1 + 90%) × (1 − 30%)

On the left is growth of 20 percent in each of two consecutive years. On the right is a 90 percent surge in the first year followed by a 30 percent decline in the next. A sudden explosion may feel faster, but steady progress is more likely to carry you far.

Second, compounding magnifies tiny differences by orders of magnitude over time. For example, (1 + 3%)²⁰⁰ is about seven times (1 + 2%)²⁰⁰. The difference between them is only one percentage point per period, yet long-term compounding dramatically amplifies it. This is why seemingly insignificant daily accumulation can eventually create a structural lead.

Third, most returns often arrive late. In the example of (1 + 3%)²⁰⁰, the increase from period 150 to period 200 accounts for roughly three quarters of the total gain. This means that progress may be painfully slow through a long period of accumulation—slow enough to make you wonder whether the work is worthwhile. That is the nature of compounding: inconspicuous at first, striking only later.

Compounding rewards people who do the right thing for long enough. Understanding this is essential. Only when you can see long-term value will you willingly pay the present cost of efforts that seem small. Otherwise, the heavy investment and limited early return involved in writing, starting a business, or learning a skill make it easy to quit halfway.

Many people dream of finding a track on which they can “roll a snowball,” yet underestimate the time, energy, and patience required before the snowball takes shape. They also overlook the loneliness that accompanies the journey. This chapter will turn compounding and persistence into a more practical logic and help you decide what path truly suits you and deserves your long-term commitment.

Compounding is not exclusive to finance. It appears throughout life as a positive feedback loop.

· Professional ability: stronger skills bring access to more challenging and valuable assignments; better assignments accelerate skill development.

· Knowledge: deeper learning reveals the boundaries of what you know; clearer boundaries make further learning more targeted.

· Personal influence: greater influence attracts better opportunities; better opportunities increase influence.

· Networks: strong connections can generate more strong connections, causing the value of a network to grow nonlinearly.

· Health: sustained good habits continually improve physical function and mental condition.

· Collaboration with AI: approaching AI like an architect and using it to solve real problems lets you accumulate tools, save time, and sharpen your thinking, creating compounding returns.

All these factors share one feature: once a positive cycle begins, further growth requires progressively less effort. Thus:

Success ≈ skill × persistence

Skill improves through sustained practice. Persistence—especially at the beginning, when the direction is unclear and the return uncertain—places even greater demands on judgment and temperament.

### A Real Example: Five Years of Compounding as a Content Creator

To make persistence and compounding concrete, I want to share five years of back-end data from my media channels. I do not present these numbers to boast. I want the real shape of a compounding curve to set realistic expectations for long-term commitment and give you confidence to keep doing the right things.

My Bilibili channel's audience growth followed a classic compounding pattern.

· Extremely slow at first: during the first year, the weekly audience increase was usually only a few dozen to one hundred people.

· Occasional leaps: a breakout video would sometimes produce a sharp short-term jump.

· A return to normal: after the leap, growth would usually settle back into a long period of slow fluctuation.

· Acceleration in stages: only later did stretches of growth with a visibly steeper slope appear.

Even in the later period, an increase of dozens or hundreds per week remained normal. The thousands of new followers brought by a hit video were rarely sustainable.

View counts fluctuated in close relation to follower growth, and each video's relevance decayed quickly. Most traffic arrived soon after publication; the long tail brought relatively few new viewers. This reveals a harsh truth about content creation: effortless “passive growth” is unreliable. Consistent output is what sustains compounding.

The income curve demonstrates delayed gratification even more clearly. Before the channel reached five thousand followers, it earned almost nothing. Only after experiments in monetization, such as memberships, did revenue gradually stabilize and begin to scale.

Throughout those five years, one key variable remained steady: publishing frequency. I released videos at roughly the same weekly rate for the entire period. This was one of the few variables entirely within a creator's control.

Imagine that I had begun with the expectation of getting rich quickly. Faced with a long initial period of virtually no return—only a few hundred views, zero income, and constant doubt about the future—I probably would not have persisted.

Many early videos received only a few hundred views. Behind those numbers was a contest of temperament: when the outside world gives you no response to your effort, can you continue? Once you internalize the logic of compounding, you see more clearly that the performance of any single release is not what matters. What matters is continuing to produce valuable content, learn, and improve. You cannot predict exactly when results will appear, and you need not be consumed by anxiety over it. What you can do is take the one controllable variable—creating and publishing consistently—and do it as well as possible.

In 2024, my content business earned 1.5 million yuan. In the first quarter of 2025, revenue from one self-study course exceeded my total revenue for all of 2024—including live classes and that course—even though I had not promoted it aggressively.

Those numbers look impressive, but they grew from a very ordinary starting point: a few hundred views, no revenue, and five years of persistence. When people ask the secret of my success, my most honest answer is that I began making videos five years ago, published at least one every week, tried to provide value, and kept doing so for five years. If you want to try, you could begin there too.

### Find the “Long Slope and Deep Snow” That Belong to You

The path I chose—content creation—suits me, but it may not suit you. One reason I persisted is that the work itself provides an intrinsic reward. External recognition and income are more consequence than inner motive. That is why the phrase “that belong to you” matters.

Recall the earlier discussion of high-value work: cultivate taste, sustain non-consensus thinking, communicate proactively to build information channels, develop quantitative and qualitative reasoning, embrace long-termism, and act before optimizing. All of these depend on developing your own thoughts, views, and judgments. Relying on yourself actually makes it easier to find directions of genuine value. The same is true of building products. The market will tell you what it does not want, but you must remain the axis: make something, test it, and form a solution of your own.

Do not mistake liking something for short-lived novelty. Many people feel excited by an activity and conclude that it is their passion, but excitement rarely carries us through plateaus and tedium. Anything valuable and every craft worth mastering inevitably includes both: the plateau in physical training, the difficult passage in learning an instrument, the frustrating stage of learning to program.

A more reliable test is your response when you reach the tedious plateau. Instead of immediately quitting, do you become curious? “Why am I stuck here? I want to understand it. I want to break through. I want to know how to do this better.” If you repeatedly enter that state, you have probably found a direction better suited to long-term commitment.

### Why Learn to Work Well Before Learning to Make Money?

Finding and staying on your own path requires a relatively calm state of mind. Under chronic anxiety, it is difficult to hear your inner voice or discern what you genuinely want to pursue. Anxiety makes you hypersensitive to the outside world: you rush to follow whenever someone else succeeds and waver at the first expression of doubt. Persistence in this state feels like gritting your teeth against pain and rarely lasts.

That is why the book proceeds in stages: first build the foundation, then transform and explore.

(1) Learn to work well. Master your current work and establish stability and control.

(2) Find high-value tasks. Proactively take on projects that promote growth and accumulate capital.

(3) Build human capital. Continually improve your abilities, accumulate experience, and establish a professional reputation.

(4) Exchange it for autonomy. As your human capital grows stronger, you gain more choices and flexibility.

(5) Enter a virtuous cycle. Once you possess discretionary capital—time, energy, money, and relationships—you can explore more possibilities at lower risk.

(6) Learn to make money. When you have more resources and a calmer mind, you are better positioned to discover value beyond your job and monetize it.

This growth path balances the probability and magnitude of success. It does not encourage blind risk-taking. It emphasizes steady progress: establish before disrupting. For most people, mastering the fundamental ability to make money and continuing to create value from it will never be easy. Only by keeping our feet on the ground and accumulating step by step can we build a broad road of our own.

### Seven Principles from This Book

I have now shared the understanding and experience I have accumulated over the years. I hope they help you move from working well to making money and realize yourself with greater coherence and fulfillment. I distill the book's central ideas into seven principles. Write them down and revisit them; they will reveal something new each time.

(1) The world is complex, multidimensional, and uncertain. Stop searching for simple, universally applicable answers.

(2) Learn, work, and make money as you would master a craft: focus on accumulation, keep improving, and respect the process.

(3) Action matters more than thought; thought matters more than merely acquiring knowledge. Learn by doing.

(4) Take responsibility for yourself and focus on your own growth.

(5) Respect the market. Build products and provide services around what it needs.

(6) Keep investing time, energy, and attention in things that compound.

(7) Ground everything you do in self-knowledge.

The seventh point deserves further explanation. Many people grow up absorbing a mistaken belief that value equals external possessions—wealth, status, and fame—rather than the inner state of *being*. They become accustomed to setting one target after another: “become rich,” “reach a particular net worth,” and so on. They rarely stop to ask, “Do these goals truly come from within me?”

As a result, even achieving the goal can lead to fresh confusion, because it was merely a temporary stopping point rather than a destination. They were taught to be ambitious and pursue impressive achievements, but those demands came mostly from the outside world and may not represent what they truly want. Sometimes what we call “self-actualization” is only another way to seek external approval.

Knowing yourself is a difficult lifelong inquiry. It has no standard answer and admits no shortcut. Seen from another angle, however, many themes in this book ultimately point to the same center: understanding yourself more deeply. The further you pursue that inquiry, the more likely you are to find work you genuinely love and can sustain—and to discover your own “long slope and deep snow.” The road may not be a shortcut, but it will be steadier, longer, and more likely to bring lasting fulfillment and the greatest meaning.

In discussing financial freedom, we also noted that it is only the first layer of genuine freedom. If you do not know what you truly seek, no amount of money can necessarily bring inner freedom and peace. The long-term accumulation worth pursuing is therefore not merely material return. It is also the continuing process of conversing with yourself and building a sense of existence and value.

The road is not easy, but it is worthwhile. This brings the central argument of the book to a close. May we each embark on our own journey—and travel far with steady steps.
