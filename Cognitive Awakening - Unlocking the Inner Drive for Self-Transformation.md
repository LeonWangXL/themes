# Author's Preface: Unlocking the Inner Drive for Self-Transformation

We belong to a fortunate generation. We have lived through the greatest leap in human development to date—an age of technological progress, material abundance, and universal connectivity. We live longer, possess greater intelligence and wealth, and can pass some of these advantages to the next generation through either genes or financial assets. Yet no matter how advanced technology becomes, one thing can never be transferred directly: the mind.

By “mind,” I mean, in plain language, the attitudes with which we regard people and events, and the judgments and choices that follow. Everyone enters the world with no ready-made philosophy of life, worldview, or system of values. Every disposition, habit, and pattern must develop from the original state of human nature. This is true of you, me, our parents, and our children; no one can simply skip this stage. For many people, that original state is chaotic. By nature, they seek simplicity, ease, comfort, and certainty. These impulses govern them and become the physiological starting point of their joy, anger, sorrow, and pleasure—yet most people caught within them understand very little about what is happening.

Our ignorance of ourselves makes us resemble people who are “asleep while awake.” I know that a person cannot literally be awake and asleep at the same time; the phrase is an obvious contradiction. But before dismissing it as a logical error, follow me for a moment through the usual course of a human life. You may find yourself agreeing with it.

Barring the unexpected, most people grow up along the familiar route of education, work, marriage, and family, carried forward by life's momentum. When young, almost no one imagines that the future could turn out badly. We assume a good life will arrive on its own. Innocent of the world, we think: even if we cannot yet explain exactly what to do, confidence is enough. Youth, after all, feels invincible.

Reality does not always unfold as we imagine. After years sustained by that confidence, most people discover that they have not become exceptional. They have spent their time complying with social rules and handling life's annoyances, gradually drifting with the current: scrolling on their phones, playing games, feeling neither much pressure nor much drive. Life seems tolerable, hope still flickers somewhere inside, and they occasionally struggle or cry out—only to return to short-sighted choices and immediate comfort. They understand little about how the world works: not how things are structured, not the routes and methods by which effort produces results, and not what they truly want, what they are capable of, or who they will ultimately become.

Then, at some point, they abruptly realize that they are powerless before the world. Their dreams and reality stand far apart; pressure from life and work closes in; outstanding peers have disappeared far ahead. Anxious, impatient, and suddenly awake, they ask: “Why didn't I learn the truth about the world sooner? Why didn't I wake up while I was still at my best age?” But however bitterly they ask, the best opportunity seems to have passed. Life is a one-way road; it cannot be restarted. In the end, they are forced to break their proud hearts and accept an ordinary life amid resignation and sighs.

A smaller group is more fortunate. They “open their eyes” at the right age. Escaping the traps of growth, they begin improving themselves deliberately and preparing for a better future. They slowly pull away from the crowd and move to the front of their peers. Yet they soon encounter a bottleneck. They want to work hard but repeatedly lose to inertia. They exert themselves but remain inefficient. They seek refinement but keep taking detours. They read many books and forget them; make great efforts and see them wasted. The harder they push, the more confused they become; the more effort they make, the more lost they feel.

This is the portrait of someone asleep while awake. It was also my portrait. For a long time, I moved through life as though not fully awake: I did not understand myself, held no firm view of how to live, and exercised no choice over my fate. I devoted myself to my job, but almost all my free time went to activities requiring no thought. Whenever I could, I met friends and often drank myself senseless. I stayed up late, never chose to read or exercise, and passed the time with comedy clips, celebrity gossip, and mobile games. When nothing else remained, I wrapped myself in a blanket and slept. Somewhere below awareness, I assumed this carefree life would continue indefinitely.

Then, one day, accidents radically changed the lives of two close friends. I could not stop asking myself: if such things happened to me—if everything I now possessed were stripped away—what would remain? What could I do? What trace had I left in the world? The answers chilled me, because I suddenly realized that I could do almost nothing. An unfamiliar anxiety rose inside me. I knew with great force that I could not continue this way. I wanted clarity instead of confusion, more skills instead of helplessness, and achievements I created rather than circumstances I merely endured.

In 2017, at thirty-six—an age many already considered late—I began exploring with determination. I discovered that having tasks every day is not awakening, and neither is working hard every day. True awakening is an inward longing: it takes a long view, remains patient, employs the power of cognition, and makes time an ally. I discovered that the fundamental difference between people lies in cognitive ability. Cognition shapes choices, and choices alter destiny; growth, at its core, is the process of making the mind's understanding clearer. I began ranging widely across neuroscience, cognitive science, psychology, behavioral science, sociology, and other fields. In them I saw the possibility of my own growth. I began to understand what I wanted, grasp some of the underlying patterns and truths, and see methods and paths toward my dreams.

From confusion to alertness, from bewilderment to clarity, I gradually uncovered the secrets of awakening desire and awakening method. I learned how to ignite and sustain an inner drive for self-improvement, replacing bitter reliance on willpower with motivation grounded in a scientific understanding of the mind. When I shared these ideas and methods online, they resonated with many readers. They were surprised by how quickly I had changed and wrote to tell me that my articles made difficult ideas accessible, brought sudden clarity, gave them strength, and restored hope. They also kept sending questions about growth and asking for help. In those questions, I saw countless people longing to improve. I decided that while continuing my own development, I would help others with theirs.

On the last day of May 2018, I opened a question-and-answer column on my WeChat public account, *Clear Mind*, and took on an additional role as a personal-growth consultant. Although it was a sideline, it exposed me to a large collection of real cases of confusion; many examples in this book come from those questions. After addressing problems of every kind, I found that the methodology I had developed could resolve most people's confusion and distress. Whether explaining foundational ideas such as brain structure, the subconscious, metacognition, and deliberate practice, or offering strategies for self-control, focus, action, learning, and emotional regulation—including the essential habits of rising early, meditating, reading, writing, and exercising—I could present distinctive principles and specific, workable methods. These accumulated ideas formed the basic shape of this book.

When you see the book clearly, however, you will find that practice and change are its true center. Treat it less as something to read once and more as a practical reference: return to it, reflect, and act until real change occurs, rather than merely indulging the mind with ideas. You may be drawn to a particular subject and jump directly to that chapter, which is perfectly reasonable. But if time permits, I still recommend beginning at the start. The foundational concepts gradually lock together like building blocks, and background introduced earlier may affect how you understand what follows.

I believe this book can benefit anyone who wants to grow, regardless of profession, age, or role. It is especially intended for people who lack patience, demand quick results, and live with intense anxiety; those who lack a goal and drift through life in a haze; those who want to improve but know only how to prop themselves up through sheer willpower; those who want better ways to learn and improve their academic performance; and those who want to understand the deep laws of growth and create achievements deliberately. If you recognize yourself here, this book can bring sudden clarity and help you internalize a genuine cognitive drive. I especially hope that young people—particularly students who have not yet entered society—will read it. You stand at the beginning. If this book helps you awaken, you can avoid many detours and move one or several steps ahead of your peers, directly reducing the amount of life you waste.

If you think you have already missed the best age, that does not matter. “Now” is always the best time to begin. This is not consolation; it is fact. Grandma Moses began painting at seventy-six and held a solo exhibition at eighty. Wang Deshun walked the runway at seventy-nine. Chu Shijian began a new business growing oranges at seventy-four. Even if you are sixty, they could still tell you, “Child, don't worry. You have at least twenty years in which to start again.”

The remark is playful, but the truth is plain. If you abandon growth, you will certainly remain the same five or ten years from now. If you change, however, you may discover an entirely new self. Life is not predetermined. Time will pass whether or not you venture beyond the familiar, so doing is better than not doing, and beginning is better than surrendering. As long as hope remains in you, now is the best time to start.

If you have read this far, let the next turn of the page be our handshake: “It is a pleasure to meet you. I am Zhou Ling. Let us begin this journey of awakening together.”

# Part One

# Look Within and Free Yourself from Anxiety

## Chapter 1: The Brain—Where Every Problem Begins

### Section 1: The Brain—Getting to Know Yourself Again

I suspect that many people do not truly understand themselves and perhaps never have. That is why their own problems leave them so confused. By “yourself,” I mean specifically your brain: without it, we are nothing; with it but without understanding it, we can live only by vague feeling—and that is rarely the life we want.

Begin with the brain and get to know yourself again. You will “evolve” a second time.

#### The Triune Brain

Humanity owes its position as the most advanced species on the planet entirely to the intelligent brain. To us it appears extraordinarily precise, almost perfect; even modern science and technology have not fully unlocked its secrets. Yet the evidence shows that it is far from perfect. Indeed, it is riddled with problems, and those problems are the source of much of our helplessness and pain. To understand why, we must first look at the brain's evolutionary history.

At first, Earth contained no life. Billions of years ago, however, certain “replicators” appeared in the ancient oceans. Under evolutionary pressure they gradually became single-celled organisms, which then evolved into animals, plants, microorganisms, and more. One animal branch later developed into primitive fish that filled the seas.

About 360 million years ago, some began moving onto land, ushering in the age of reptiles. To adapt to terrestrial life, reptiles evolved the earliest **“instinctive brain.”** Its structure was simple: a primitive reflex module that enabled rapid instinctive responses to the environment—fight or flee from danger, hunt prey, pursue a desirable mate. Reptiles possessed neither emotion nor reason, and because their body temperature changed with the environment, they could not remain active in the cold. Yet these simple instinctive responses enabled them to survive, and some, including crocodiles, lizards, and snakes, remain with us today. Scholars therefore also call the instinctive brain the primitive brain, basal brain, crocodile brain, lizard brain, or simply reptilian brain.

Mammals arrived about 200 million years ago. To adapt more successfully, they not only maintained a constant body temperature but also evolved emotions. Emotion helped mammals seek advantage and avoid harm in hostile environments, greatly improving their chances of survival. Fear moved them away from danger; excitement concentrated attention on the hunt; pleasure strengthened bonds with companions; sorrow drew care from sympathetic others. This is why we enjoy keeping cats and dogs as pets: they readily communicate with us emotionally and know how to please and care for us. A distinct emotional region—the limbic system—accordingly developed in the mammalian brain. Neuroscientists call it the **“emotional brain.”** Since monkeys are frequently observed and used in experiments, it is also commonly called the monkey brain.

Only about 2.5 million years ago did humans emerge from among the mammals and evolve a “neocortex” in the frontal area of the brain. Not until roughly 70,000 to 200,000 years ago did this neocortex fully take shape as an unmatched brain region. It gave us language, art, technology, and civilization, granting us an overwhelming survival advantage on this planet. Enamored of our unique rationality, we call this new region the **“rational brain,”** though some also call it the reasoning or thinking brain (see Figure 1-1).

Everyone knows Aesop's fable “The Farmer and the Snake.” On a cold winter day, a farmer finds a frozen snake by the road. Taking pity on it, he places it inside his clothing and warms it with his own body. When the snake revives, it does not thank him; it bites him. As the farmer dies, he laments that he deserves his fate for pitying the wicked. In truth, had the farmer understood a little about brains, he would not have made such an elementary mistake. A cold-blooded reptile such as a snake has no developed emotional brain and knows nothing of gratitude. It acts by instinct, fighting or fleeing when threatened. The ignorant farmer imagined that a snake, like a human, possessed a moral sense and would repay kindness, and the error cost him his life.

Human beings are therefore profoundly different from the world's other animals. From the inside out, our brains contain at least three layers: the ancient instinctive brain, the somewhat younger emotional brain, and the very young rational brain.

Most people know none of this. They simply assume that all animals have a single brain and that humans are merely a little smarter. Like the farmer who rescued the snake, this mistaken understanding often leads us to do foolish things.

#### Hierarchy and the Struggle for Power

Our brains were plainly not designed in advance. They were assembled by stacking different modules, like a computer cobbled together with an old motherboard, an outdated graphics card, and a new processor. Compatibility problems are inevitable when they work together.

The encouraging part is that the advanced rational brain is uniquely human. It gives us foresight, allows us to weigh alternatives, and enables delayed gratification in service of the future. From this perspective, the instinctive and emotional brains are indeed less advanced. But we should not congratulate ourselves too soon, because **although the rational brain is more advanced, it is desperately weak beside the instinctive and emotional brains.** There are at least four reasons.

**First**, the instinctive brain is nearly 360 million years old, the emotional brain almost 200 million, and the rational brain less than 2.5 million. If the instinctive brain were a hundred-year-old elder, the emotional brain would be a fifty-five-year-old adult and the rational brain an infant not yet one year old. However clever the baby, it remains badly outmatched by two adults (see Figure 1-2).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_3.jpg)

Figure 1-2 An age analogy for the triune brain

**Second**, the three brains mature at different times. The instinctive brain is already relatively developed in infancy. The emotional brain approaches maturity in early adolescence. The rational brain comes last, becoming broadly mature only in early adulthood. Without insisting on exact ages, we can think of them as maturing at roughly two, twelve, and twenty-two—about ten years apart. This is why our minds seem so immature through the first twenty years of life.

**Third**, the human brain contains about 86 billion neurons, and the instinctive and emotional brains account for nearly 80 percent of them. They therefore exert greater control. They are also closer to the heart and receive priority blood supply in an emergency. This is why the mind often goes blank under extreme stress: the rational brain on the outer layer is deprived of blood.

**Fourth**, although the instinctive and emotional brains appear primitive, they govern the subconscious and the body's physiological systems. They continuously manage sight, hearing, touch, breathing, heartbeat, blood pressure, and more. Their processing speed is therefore immense—at least 11 million operations per second, comparable to the world's fastest personal computers. The rational brain manages only about forty operations per second at best, and it consumes a great deal of energy while doing so. If this is new to you, the contrast may be startling.

Every sign suggests that the rational brain has weak control over the whole. **Most decisions in life therefore arise from instinct and emotion rather than reason.** Whatever part influences the decision is trying to protect us, but decisions made by the instinctive and emotional brains are often mismatched with modern society because those systems still behave as though we lived in the primitive world.

This is understandable. For hundreds of millions of years, our ancestors lived as hunters and gatherers in dangerous, scarce environments. Survival came before everything. They needed instinct and emotion to respond instantly to threats, enjoy food immediately, and seek comfort intensely—or risk being eaten or starving.

Survival also required conserving energy. High-energy activities such as thought and exercise could be perceived as threats and rejected by the instinctive brain, while effortless entertainment delighted both the instinctive and emotional brains. In the primitive world, failure to conserve energy and seize pleasure when available might mean becoming a wild animal's next meal.

Survival pressure thus shaped the genes behind the instinctive and emotional brains. Their natural defaults became **short-sightedness and immediate gratification**. Because they dominate the brain's decisions, these became humanity's default tendencies too.

Then society suddenly accelerated. Humanity entered agricultural society about ten thousand years ago, industrial society roughly three hundred years ago, and the information age around fifty years ago. To the ancient instinctive and emotional brains, these changes occurred in an instant. They had no time to adapt. Suddenly, basic survival was no longer a constant concern, while comfort and entertainment were everywhere. The old brains did not know what to do. Today we may sit in reinforced-concrete towers wearing suits, but underneath we remain “primitive humans,” always ready to fight, flee, or seize immediate pleasure in order to survive.

Evolution has not yet had time to redesign us completely. Traits that offered an advantage in ancient society have become obstacles in the modern one. Almost every problem we encounter in personal growth can be traced to short-sightedness and immediate gratification. In today's language, however, they are better described as **avoiding difficulty and demanding quick results**.

· Avoiding difficulty means doing only what is easy and comfortable, circling around the core work and hiding in the comfort zone from the real challenge.

· Demanding quick results means expecting immediate outcomes from everything and quickly abandoning anything whose results cannot be seen at once.

The picture is now clear. We fail to accomplish things not because our wishes are too weak or because we lack willpower, but because our default nature is overwhelmingly strong. We know we should not eat too much sugar or high-calorie food, yet something seems to urge us toward one final bite. We know compulsive phone use is harmful, yet cannot move our hands or eyes away. Whenever the rational brain fights the instinctive and emotional brains, reason usually loses. Sometimes instinct and desire hijack the body before reason has even started.

To make this easier to understand, imagine the brain as a company. The instinctive and emotional brains are employees: one very senior, the other in its prime. Neither is educated or ambitious; both care only about immediate ease and comfort. The rational brain is the manager. It has foresight and rank, but because it is so young, it lacks authority, and the two veteran employees frequently disregard its decisions. This structure traps us in the cycle of **“I know perfectly well, but I still can't do it; I want it desperately, but I still can't get it.”** For example:

· We know reading matters, then turn around and pull out the phone.

· We know running is good for us, but stop after two days.

· We know important work should come first, but spend all day circling trivia.

Worse, once the old employees take control of the company, they force the manager to produce rational explanations for their poor choices: since you are so clever, explain why what I am doing is right. The weak manager obediently complies.

· There isn't enough time to read many pages now; I might as well relax with a game.

· How can I have the strength to lose weight if I don't eat enough?

· I'll enjoy myself today. Tomorrow I'll study twice as hard and make up for the wasted time.

Now the company looks harmonious and no one has to feel awkward. In reality, the rational brain rarely exercises independent judgment. **Most of what we call thinking is actually rationalization of our behavior and desires.** This is why humans have been called self-explaining animals.

#### Growth Is the Process of Overcoming Our Nature

Human beings are born in confusion. The fundamental reason is that our rational brain begins too weak to escape the pressure and control of the instinctive and emotional brains. Awakening and growth mean strengthening reason as quickly as possible so that it can overcome those defaults. Those who take the initiative gain a greater survival advantage in modern society, because people with a more developed rational brain are better able to:

· take the long view and leave the comfort zone voluntarily;

· restrain themselves against potential risk and delay gratification for possible returns;

· remain patient and persist in apparently “useless” activities whose effects cannot be seen in the short term;

· resist temptation and choose differently from others when confronted with comfort and entertainment.

Ordinary people grow haphazardly through instinct and feeling; whether they find a path of deliberate awakening and scientific growth depends largely on luck. The good news is that you now know the secret. The better news is that sustained practice guided by sound methods can strengthen the rational brain more quickly. The brain, like a muscle, follows the rule “use it or lose it.” If we habitually act on emotion without reflection, emotional thinking will dominate. If we regularly think and reflect, rational thought gains the upper hand.

**Habits are difficult to change because they reinforce themselves: the more we use them, the stronger they become, and the stronger they become, the more we use them. The best way out of an established habit is not self-control but knowledge.** Depending on willpower alone is deeply painful, while knowledge can readily create new understanding and new choices. Later chapters will explain exactly how to change.

Strengthening the rational brain does not mean eliminating the instinctive and emotional brains; that would be impossible. The three form an inseparable whole. Nor is elimination desirable, because **the instinctive brain's immense processing power and the emotional brain's powerful capacity for action are precious resources.** Understand and guide them patiently, and they can serve you. Indeed, their power may become the key to your achievement.

Neither should the rational brain fight or replace the other two. Direct confrontation pits one system's weakness against another's strength and offers no way forward. Many people experience extreme pain while trying to grow because they repeatedly use willpower against instinct and emotion. They exhaust themselves for little return.

To avoid that mistake, remember this: **the rational brain is not the part that performs the work. Work belongs to the instinctive and emotional brains because they have the strength. Heaven gave the rational brain wisdom so that it could direct instinct and emotion, not replace them.**

Like the manager in our imaginary company, its duty is neither to fire the two employees, fight them, nor personally perform every task. Its role is to learn, improve understanding, use strategy, respect and accommodate the veteran employees, and guide them cleverly until they do the work willingly and happily. Only then can the “company” of the brain become united, harmonious, and prosperous.

### Section 2: Anxiety—The Root of Anxiety

Anxiety is surely an old friend. It accompanies us like background music. Although intimately familiar, we have never really known what it is. After silently enduring it for years, I finally summoned the courage one day to look it directly in the eye. From that point, it slowly retreated. It occasionally counterattacks, but it can no longer come close. Today I give you the cognitive weapon I discovered, in the hope that anxiety will never again torment you.

#### The Battle with Anxiety

The year I turned thirty-six was the first year of my awakening. Before then, I had lived in confusion. As described earlier, I was committed to my job, but activities requiring no thought consumed almost all of my free time. Once awakened, I experienced an anxiety unlike anything before. I urgently wanted to change, because **whether we speak of individuals or groups, human beings derive security from possessing a distinctive advantage in some area—ability, wealth, power, or influence.**

After a burst of activity, however, I found that I had no idea where to begin and could see no change or opening. Longing for an advantage I could not obtain felt like being a child faced with a favorite toy forever beyond reach.

Fortunately, the era of online learning had arrived. Accomplished people of every kind appeared on the internet, along with communities full of others who, like me, wanted to improve. I saw hope. I enrolled in many courses and bought many books, expecting an immediate transformation. The moment a new book arrived, I felt almost as if I already possessed its knowledge. Only later did I discover the vast distance between the difficulty of reading a book and the ease of buying one.

To relieve my anxiety, I began unconsciously demanding more and faster progress. I fell into a shallow form of diligence concerned only with volume: I raced through every book and remembered nothing afterward. Looking up, I saw that some peers were impossibly far ahead, while even people younger than me had accumulated impressive achievements and I had to begin from zero. I grew irritable and anxious and sank into a period of low spirits. One sentence echoed constantly in my mind: there isn't enough time; it is too late; everything is too late.

#### Facing Anxiety Directly

Like a drowning person, I was completely enveloped by anxiety and seemed to be sinking slowly toward the riverbed. As the shimmer on the surface faded, one moment of despair brought a realization: doing something is always better than doing nothing. Wang Deshun walked the runway at seventy-nine; Chu Shijian began growing oranges at seventy-four. These were real examples, and I was only in my thirties. Much of life still lay ahead. Beginning now could hardly be called late. I should not compare my present condition with that of successful people today. If I had to compare, I should compare myself with them at the beginning. Better still, I should compare myself with my own past. Even the smallest improvement would be worthwhile.

Perhaps I had hit bottom and rebounded. My anxiety slowly subsided, and I finally noticed that I had always endured it passively. I had never held the initiative. I knew neither when anxiety would appear nor how to send it away. If I wanted to avoid repeating these emotional swings, I needed to face anxiety and resolve the problem at its root.

One afternoon, I took out pen and paper and listed every annoyance, worry, concern, and desire in my mind, from my vision for life down to what I needed to do ten minutes later. Gradually, several forms of anxiety emerged.

**First, completion anxiety.** I habitually overfilled my schedule and lived beneath constant deadlines. I wanted to learn many things at once but lacked the time. I imposed so many daily routines on myself that missing a single day left me unable to breathe. I made casual promises and allowed unimportant matters to disrupt the calendar. Whenever my inner desires spread too broadly or my external schedule grew too tight, depth and composure became impossible.

**Second, position anxiety.** If, as a complete beginner, you stare at what the masters of a field are doing today, anxiety is inevitable: that person is already so influential at such a young age. They caught the trend and seized the advantage—when will I ever get there? I thought only I had this brilliant idea, but someone else has already built the product. Positioning yourself against the wrong reference point makes everything feel too late. In truth, the comparison itself is wrong.

**Third, choice anxiety.** Too many options can also make us anxious. A stretch of free time suddenly appears, but because we want to do so many things, indecision consumes it all. We cannot settle down to the most important task—or do not know what that task is. Advice from accomplished people creates further conflict. Person A says to read every word; Person B advocates thematic reading and says there is no need to finish a book. Both sound right, but their methods are opposites. Which should we use? Human beings prefer uniqueness and certainty. Our natural habits are poorly equipped for plurality and uncertainty.

**Fourth, environmental anxiety.** External constraints are sometimes unavoidable. Family or work may prevent us from doing what we want, while forcing us to spend large amounts of time on things we do not want. The resulting inefficiency and powerlessness can drive us mad.

**Fifth, difficulty anxiety.** Some books really are hard to read. Some articles are hard to write, knowledge hard to understand, and skills hard to acquire. Anything that genuinely makes you stronger contains a core difficulty that cannot be avoided. If you refuse to attack it directly and keep circling the edges, your anxiety only grows with time.

#### The Root of Anxiety

Anxiety has two basic causes: **wanting to do many things at once and wanting to see results immediately.** Wang Xiaobo wrote that all human suffering is, in essence, anger at one's own incompetence. Anxiety fits the same pattern: desire exceeds ability, while patience is desperately scarce. Anxiety arises when the gap between desire and ability grows too wide.

At a deeper level, anxiety does not come entirely from conscious thought. It arises from the physiological structure of the brain. We already know that human beings naturally avoid difficulty and demand quick results. The seeds are planted deep within us: **because we demand quick results, we want to do many things at once; because we avoid difficulty, we want immediate results without much effort.**

That is the true root of anxiety. Anxiety is natural—the human default. People have always been this way. The information age has merely amplified the tendency through faster rhythms and stronger competition. We need neither blame nor shame ourselves, and we need not wrestle blindly with our nature. We should understand its mechanism and find ways to change. The simplest approach is to reverse the defaults:

· restrain desire instead of doing many things at once;

· face reality and see your actual level of ability;

· put first things first and find a way to do only what matters most;

· accept the environment and do what lies within your power under its constraints;

· face the core challenge and push yourself hard enough to break through it.

Of course, this advice sounds like a collection of correct but useless platitudes. Everyone knows the principles. What matters is how to improve ability and sustain patience in practice.

I would like to provide an immediate answer, but a sentence or two cannot explain it. Improving ability touches almost every aspect of life, so the answers will appear throughout the book. Patience, however, is one subject we can address now.

Patience may be one of humanity's most precious qualities. It runs directly against our desire for quick results and avoidance of difficulty. The patient, one might say, inherit the world. Let us therefore begin with patience.

### Section 3: Patience—The Patient Inherit the World

During the 1980s and 1990s, Jin Yong's martial-arts novels swept China. Decades have passed and Jin Yong himself is gone, yet his works remain beloved classics. Their success plainly owes much to his inventive imagination and superb writing. In my view, however, there is a deeper and more hidden reason: his stories touch the most primitive and instinctive part of human nature—**immediate gratification**.

Jin Yong's stories often follow a familiar pattern: an ordinary boy encounters a miraculous opportunity, effortlessly masters an extraordinary discipline, and achieves greatness. Skills that would take an ordinary person decades are acquired in a short time, sometimes overnight. The stories also emphasize the heroes' goodness, as though fortune visits only the pure of heart. Readers may be led to believe that purity matters more than effort and that success requires only a simple heart. Naturally, people like this conclusion; remaining innocent is far easier than remaining diligent.

The fantasy of acquiring tremendous power without tremendous effort is irresistible. In real life, whether studying, taking examinations, working, or earning money, excellence demands long training. Stories are stories and reality is reality. We may lose ourselves in fiction for a time, but eventually we must return to the rules of the real world: achievement requires patience and delayed gratification.

#### How We Have Misunderstood Patience

Many people speak of patience while their behavior hovers at the edge of immediate gratification. They begin the day's work with the easiest and most comfortable part, then lose themselves in entertainment and peripheral trivia, unable to address what matters. They spend hours searching for useful articles and saving them, perhaps never to open them again. Their New Year's plan is perfect; the moment it is written, they feel as if it had already been completed, but within days it disappears. At times they become astonishingly “diligent,” forcing up their reading volume, applying the ten-thousand-hour rule, and repeating the same activity every day—yet success never comes. At the first hint of change, sometimes when they have no more than an idea, they rush to announce a new life to the world, then grow discouraged and quit at the slightest setback. Seeing the gulf between themselves and their peers, they panic, enroll in many courses, read many books, undertake many projects, and expect immediate change. In short, they hope to become learned after a few books, form a habit in twenty-one days, lose weight by eating a few bites less, or transform themselves immediately after one practical article.

I list these faults not to prepare a moral pedestal from which to lecture. I have fallen into every one of these traps; I was once one of “them,” and I know the experience intimately. Such habits can ruin a life, or at least condemn it to mediocrity. Without patience, any amount of effort can be wasted.

Yet no one ever taught us what patience actually is or how to develop it. We were merely told again and again: “Be patient. Don't rush. Stop jumping from one thing to another.” As a result, patience came to mean enduring boredom, bearing pain, gritting our teeth, and forcing ourselves to hold on—in short, fighting through willpower. Failure could only mean weak self-control.

That is not the truth. Our understanding of patience is so shallow that we spend much of our time struggling in pain. Since patience matters so much, we have every reason to complete the lesson we missed.

#### Impatience Is Human Nature

We reached this agreement in the first section: impatience is nothing shameful and says nothing about moral character. It is simply an inborn trait, shared by everyone. If the idea still feels abstract, observe the infants, children, and adults around you.

At birth, the rational brain contributes almost nothing; an infant lives entirely by instinct. During the first six months, babies believe themselves omnipotent, as though the whole world moves according to their wishes—the strongest form of immediate gratification. Young children switch from laughter to tears in an instant. Satisfaction brings an immediate smile; frustration brings instant rage. They make no attempt to hide either their demand for immediate gratification or their lack of patience. Once school begins, the rational brain develops and knowledge increases, strengthening patience. The differences are obvious across primary school, secondary school, and university. By adulthood, physiology stabilizes. But if self-exploration stops then, the capacity for patience may remain permanently at that level or even decline. Look closely and you will find that **society's most accomplished people are usually better at overcoming their natural defaults. They possess greater patience and a stronger capacity to delay gratification.**

Whether viewed historically, practically, or physiologically, every clue about patience points to the prefrontal cortex—the uniquely human rational brain. Understanding this can be profoundly liberating. Yet knowing ourselves is not enough. We must also look outward for patterns that can help us develop patience. Looking within and looking at the world have always formed a single whole.

#### Understanding Patterns Multiplies Patience

We often lack patience with difficult things because we cannot see the whole picture or locate our present position. We measure everything with the short ruler of instinct and assume achievement should be simple. Understanding some basic laws of development lets us replace that ruler with the objective measure of reason, greatly increasing patience. The **compounding curve** in Figure 1-3 is one such rational tool.

Compounding reveals a universal law of value accumulation: **growth is extremely slow at first, then accelerates rapidly after an inflection point.** This is the force behind the so-called eighth wonder of the world. To harness it, however, we must calmly endure the slow early stage and persist until the curve turns upward.

Any person or group without special resources can eventually accomplish something by believing in and practicing this law of accumulation. The conditions are choosing the right direction, following the principles of deliberate practice, and expanding one's abilities little by little at the edge of the comfort zone.

The **edge of the comfort zone** reveals another fundamental law of skill development. **For individuals and groups alike, ability is distributed across a comfort zone, a stretch zone, and a difficulty zone. Efficient growth requires remaining at the edge of the comfort zone. Leaping rashly into the difficulty zone produces frustration; remaining entirely within the comfort zone produces stagnation** (see Figure 1-4).

Human nature points in exactly the opposite direction. **In desire, we demand quick results**, trying to become fat from a single bite and spending our days frustrated in the difficulty zone. **In action, we avoid difficulty**, remaining in the comfort zone and accomplishing nothing in reality. Learn to work at the comfort zone's edge, and both results and confidence change completely.

The edge of the comfort zone is an important concept. Do not worry if you do not yet understand it fully; remember it for now, because it will recur throughout the book. You may also have noticed that **the compounding curve and the comfort-zone edge make excellent partners**. Together they reveal the power of patience on the largest scale, and that power is available to every ordinary person.

With these broad patterns in place, we can examine the small-scale ones. For learning and growth, everyone should first understand the **relative weight of growth activities**. It reveals the relationship among learning, thinking, action, and change: **after learning, thought matters more; after thought, action matters more; after action, actual change matters more. If we ignore change at the core, no amount of learning at the surface will be efficient. By weight: change > action > thought > learning** (see Figure 1-5).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_8.jpg)

Figure 1-5 The relative weight of growth activities

Many people suffer anxiety because they watch only the surface measure of learning. They read many books, enroll in many courses, log attendance and persist daily, and work hard enough to move themselves—yet never examine their thinking, action, or actual change. They feel that all this study has produced nothing and conclude that they are not working hard enough, so they increase the volume and enter a vicious cycle: the more they learn, the more anxious they become; the more anxious they become, the more they learn.

Once again, our nature is at work. Maintaining the input of information is easy; thinking, acting, and changing are harder. Without awareness, we instinctively **avoid difficulty** and lose ourselves in the surface quantity of study.

Surface learning also produces the most immediately visible results: today I read a book, studied for five hours, memorized one hundred words. Deeper change is much harder to observe, so our demand for quick results pushes us toward the former.

Figure 1-5 vividly demonstrates the paradox that more can be less and less can be more. Remain on the surface and we are drawn into a whirlpool of desire, wanting to learn and possess everything, constantly busy but achieving little. Go deeper and focus on actual change, and we escape the cycle of blindness, anxiety, and agitation.

When reading, for example, do not try to remember everything in a book. One or two ideas that produce real change in you are enough. Their value and meaning far exceed those of reading many books while stopping at mere awareness. Use this standard regularly, and your gains will increase, anxiety will diminish, and patience will naturally grow.

Another small-scale pattern worth understanding is the **learning plateau**. **Progress does not maintain the linear relationship with time that we imagine, in which each unit of study creates an equal result. It follows an undulating upward curve** (see Figure 1-6).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_9.jpg)

Figure 1-6 The learning curve

Almost all learning follows this pattern. Progress is rapid at first, then slows into a plateau. During the plateau, we may exert great effort while appearing not to improve at all; we may even seem to regress. But the appearance is deceptive. Neurons in the brain continue to connect and strengthen until, at a certain point, progress enters another rapid ascent.

Building a new linguistic “filter” while learning English, for example, usually requires about six months to break through the plateau. Many people do not know this. After five months of effort without visible progress, they give up. This is a great pity: the neuronal connections they worked so hard to create weaken and disappear when practice stops, forcing them to begin again next time. People who continually immerse their ears in English, by contrast, often discover one day that speech they could not understand has suddenly become intelligible. This is a classic breakthrough from a plateau. I suspect everyone has experienced something similar.

**Once we understand these patterns, we can make a different choice when facing prolonged obscurity, setbacks, or failure: others quit, while we continue.** We no longer feel defeated by slow progress or anxious about someone else's rapid growth. Consider writing for a public account. Patient writers remain fixed on long-term value; their gaze rests five or ten years ahead. Low readership today does not destroy their motivation, and an article by someone else receiving more than one hundred thousand views does not make them panic. Each person occupies a different stage. Continue creating value, and another person's today can become your tomorrow.

From this perspective, **patience is not the product of willpower but of a long view.** This also helps explain why lifelong learning matters. The more patterns we understand, the better we can locate our current stage, estimate future outcomes, and strengthen our patience for sustained action. Knowledge of how the outside world works undoubtedly multiplies patience.

#### How to Develop Patience

Many people prepare everything they need for the road ahead except patience. Fortunately, patience is not difficult to develop. Merely understanding brain structure and the laws governing development has already strengthened it invisibly. But that is not enough. We need additional paths, including the following.

**First, when facing your nature, put down the psychological burden and accept yourself calmly.**

Once we understand that impatience is natural, we can accept it. From now on, do not blame or shame yourself for agitation, anxiety, or frustration. When you notice patience slipping away, say gently: “Look, the primitive human inside me has come out again. Leaving the jungle to live in the city really isn't easy. I should understand him.” Speak kindly to yourself, and the “primitive human within” becomes willing to listen. Developing patience may take a long time; do not expect to acquire it at once. Feeling anxious because you cannot improve immediately is itself impatience. Developing patience must begin by accepting the fact that you lack it.

**Second, when facing temptation, learn delayed gratification and replace confrontation with communication.**

The instinctive and emotional brains love comfort and temptation. Abandoning them completely means confronting those brains directly. The rational brain cannot win that fight; failure is only a matter of time. The wiser approach is communication, at which reason excels. As in the dialogue above, tell them gently: “You won't lose any of the pleasure you deserve. It simply will not come now; it will come after the important work is done.” This strategy works because the older brains will not agree to renounce enjoyment, but they can accept postponing it.

Take the phone. At first, I kept mine beside the pillow while sleeping so that I could reach it the instant I woke. Later, I placed it on the desk. I still found myself walking irresistibly toward the desk each morning, but **that distance gave me an opportunity to speak with the primitive human in my body.** I told myself: “The messages have been sitting in the phone all night. Another little while won't matter. Read them later; they aren't going anywhere.” After several attempts, I discovered that I could stay away. Nothing was lost, and I experienced the fulfillment of concentrating on reading or running. Before beginning work in the morning and afternoon, I used the same method: “Hold on for now. Do the important work first. Afterward, there will be half an hour or an hour devoted entirely to the phone, and you can use it however you like.” Through dialogue and guidance, the instinctive and emotional brains felt safe and usually allowed reason to move briefly to the front of the queue.

The advantage of “entertainment afterward” is that pleasure rests on the satisfaction of completing an important task. It feels relaxing, secure, and rewarding. “Entertainment first” feels delightful at the beginning, but energy disperses without limit and important work is postponed. As time passes, emptiness and anxiety take over.

After enough experiences, the primitive human within begins to support entertainment afterward because it is ultimately more comfortable. If you are fortunate, the satisfaction produced by hard work may even replace the direct pleasure of entertainment. Once a higher form of enjoyment becomes available, dependence on the lower form naturally weakens.

Of course, forming a habit is nowhere near as easy as this explanation makes it sound. Sometimes we wake, reach out, and open WeChat before we know it. The urge feels unbearable. What then? Continue the dialogue: “I'll only glance at the notification titles so I know what is there, then close it immediately.” Do not force a confrontation or blame yourself. Letting the impulse subside a little can be effective. If you still open an item, tell yourself: “I'll stop as soon as I finish this one.”

This is how patience works. Do not demand quick results. Permit yourself to change slowly and even fail often. Whatever the result, speaking with yourself has an effect.

**Finally, when facing difficulty, change perspective deliberately and give the action meaning.**

Why do some people quickly abandon difficult things while others persist? Knowing the patterns described above is part of the answer. Another part is that persistent people are better at investigating principles. They actively shift their cognitive perspective until they discover meaning and benefit in the action. Understand the true nature and value of reading, and you may put down the phone and pick up a book. Understand the significance of deep learning, and you may abandon audio summaries and speed-reading in favor of close reading and output. Understand the real benefits of exercise, and you may leave laziness behind and train consistently. Find ways to see the meaning and advantages in what you want to do. The more dimensions you perceive, the greater your patience.

Even this is not the highest-level method. You may never guess that **the most advanced strategy is to enlist the instinctive and emotional brains themselves in solving difficult problems.**

You heard correctly. The instinctive and emotional brains fear difficulty and seek pleasure, but who says they cannot find pleasure in something difficult? They do not care whether you are scrolling on a phone or solving equations; they care only whether the experience feels good. Scientists forget food and sleep because they genuinely delight in research. Runners go out in wind and rain because they do not want to stop; running feels good to them.

The rational brain's most advanced strategy, then, is to help the instinctive and emotional brains discover pleasure in hard things and become absorbed in them. Learn to release the immense power of instinct and emotion, and no obstacle can defeat you.

## Chapter 2: The Subconscious—Life's Hidden Gift

### Section 1: Ambiguity—Life Is a Contest to Eliminate It

What is the greatest difference between a robot and a human being?

A robot has no subconscious.

Every movement it makes—turning its “body,” bending a “finger,” raising the volume of its “voice”—is controlled in its “brain” by precise numerical values. Cut the power and the robot stops. Human beings are different. If a person faints and loses consciousness, the body may collapse, but heartbeat, breathing, digestion, and other functions do not immediately stop. The subconscious controls them, and unless physical death occurs, it never disappears.

If human beings had to control every muscle, regulate every hormone, and process every neural signal numerically as a robot does, survival would be impossible. Even an apparently simple movement of the hand requires the brain to process an enormous volume of information. To improve our chances of survival, evolution cleverly layered consciousness: the subconscious manages physiological systems while consciousness manages social ones. This division frees the conscious mind to devote itself fully to higher social activity.

Such is the power of evolution. But evolution is a double-edged sword. The layering of consciousness brought humanity immense benefits and one important side effect: **ambiguity**. Because the two layers process information at radically different speeds, consciousness finds it difficult to enter the subconscious, while the subconscious readily influences consciousness. We therefore keep doing things we do not understand. We intend to study, then turn around and pick up the phone. We know certain worries are meaningless, yet cannot stop falling into anxiety. It is like a shadow behind you: it affects you, but you do not know what it is, and when you turn, all you see is a blur. Ambiguity creates confusion and fear; confusion and fear disturb cognition, emotion, and action, ultimately altering the course of a life.

Ambiguity is the source of life's troubles. Life itself resembles a contest to eliminate it: the more ambiguity you carry, the more confused you are; the less you carry, the more awake you become.

#### Learn to Eliminate Cognitive Ambiguity

Human cognitive ability must be built from zero, while the subconscious is present from the beginning. This is why we must learn throughout life. The more tools we possess, the stronger our cognition and the greater our ability to dissolve ambiguity. Learn about metacognition and you understand how to observe yourself. Learn about deliberate practice and you understand how to refine a skill. Learn how exercise reshapes the brain and you understand how to ignite enthusiasm for movement. Experts in every field know more than other people, have fewer blind spots and clearer cognition, and consequently wield greater influence.

Unfortunately, human beings naturally dislike learning and thinking because both consume a great deal of energy. Throughout evolution, life's first task was survival, so the primary principle written into our genes was energy conservation. Anything energy-intensive looked like a threat. The subconscious does not reason; it operates by instinct. It tries to guide the body down a low-energy path, enticing us toward entertainment and pleasure. Instinct therefore tends to obstruct learning, yet without learning we cannot overcome instinct. This loop ensures that we begin life in confusion and usually struggle to escape without external pressure or guidance.

Fortunately, our era offers more opportunities and better environments for learning, making deliberate growth against our instincts increasingly possible. Curiously, **the purpose of learning is to eliminate ambiguity, and the method of acquiring knowledge is also to eliminate ambiguity. The purpose and method are one.** This has become a common rule among capable learners everywhere. Consider a few examples:

· Masahito Ueda, author of *The Power of Thinking*, says that thinking essentially means discarding all information already digested until the core of the problem emerges.

· The central method in *Peak* is not to repeat what you already know how to do, but continually identify the parts that remain slightly difficult.

· Ray Dalio, author of *Principles*, enumerates rules for work and life so that they can guide his conduct clearly.

· *Beyond Feelings* teaches that clear logic rests on one discipline: never judge by vague feeling; seek clear evidence.

All these examples reveal the same fact: **we improve our ability to think by continually identifying the central difficulty and our key insight, then concentrating on it.**

Few people recognize this clearly. Most circle within ambiguity and pace around the comfort zone, repeating what they have already mastered while ignoring the real difficulty. The subconscious is behind this behavior because our genes regard it as the lower-energy route.

Outstanding performers are more willing to do energy-intensive work. The secret of top students often lies in their error notebooks: they spend time identifying mistakes precisely and concentrate on conquering them. Average students prefer diligently repeating what they already know. They look away from the real difficulties and hope to muddle through, allowing unclear points to accumulate until they can no longer cope. The difference between top and ordinary students lies not only in how hard they work, but in the pattern of effort: who is more willing to perform the demanding task of eliminating ambiguity and creating clarity?

The importance of eliminating ambiguity in learning and cognition cannot be overstated.

#### Break Down Your Troubles to Eliminate Emotional Ambiguity

Cognitive ambiguity comes from within; emotional ambiguity comes from the outside world. We encounter troubles every day, but most people habitually endure them and few willingly confront them. German psychotherapist Bert Hellinger described our attitude toward problems this way: suffering is easier than solving a problem; bearing misfortune is simpler than enjoying happiness. This fits perfectly with the human reluctance to think. Problem-solving requires thought, and happiness requires thought to balance subtle relationships, whereas suffering asks only that we remain where we are. Passive suffering consumes enormous energy too, but under genetic influence we resist spending energy proactively. This is why Meituan founder Wang Xing's observation resonates so strongly: most people will do anything to avoid real thinking.

Avoiding pain does not make it disappear. It pushes the pain into the subconscious, where it becomes an indistinct feeling. **Once a specific event turns vague, its boundaries expand without limit. A small and manageable problem becomes impossible inside the blurred subconscious.** It is like hearing “countless frogs” croaking in a pond and becoming unbearably irritated, only to investigate and discover that there were just a few.

The actual difficulty is almost always smaller than the imagined one. Procrastination, indecision, dread, and fear usually arise not because the task is so hard, but because our thoughts about it have become vague. Before a three-thousand-meter running test, nameless fear may leave the whole body trembling. Once the run begins and we must confront the fear, we discover that the test is not so terrible after all. If we become more proactive—facing, decomposing, and seeing it clearly from the start—the tension may never trouble us, and we may even “enjoy” the race calmly.

Some experiences become very difficult to remove once they enter the subconscious. Unhappy childhood events, for example, may be forgotten by consciousness while their imprint remains below awareness, covertly shaping character and behavior. Some patients with severe depression or mental disturbance undergo hypnotherapy. Every method a hypnotherapist uses serves one purpose: awaken the painful event in the subconscious so the patient can face it again, see it clearly, and finally resolve it.

Remember: no painful event disappears by itself, however small. **The only way to stop it troubling you is to face it, see it, break it down, and resolve it—deny it the chance to enter the subconscious and become vague. If it is already there, find a way to bring it out. When an indescribable discomfort arises inside you, sit down immediately and question yourself.**

· What exactly is making me restless? A public speech, a meeting with someone, or tangled thoughts?

· What specifically am I afraid of? Insufficient ability, inadequate preparation, or the possibility of a particular event?

· What can I do in this situation, and what can I not do? If I fail or make a mess of it, what is the worst possible outcome?

Keep digging layer by layer until you can go no further. Calmly acknowledge and accept even the thoughts that feel difficult to admit. Make the emotion completely transparent. Facing it may not eliminate the pain immediately and may even intensify it for a short time, but it returns command of the situation to you. At the very least, the emotion can no longer terrorize you without cause.

**Fear is a bully. Hide from it and it bares its teeth; face it and its true weakness appears.** Once you see it completely, the emotion slowly dissipates from the subconscious, and life becomes incomparably easier.

#### Clarity Within and Without: Eliminate Ambiguity in Action

Clear cognition and calm emotion must ultimately lead to decisive action. Many people attribute weak execution to environmental distraction or poor willpower. In fact, **the real cause is ambiguity of choice.**

Choice ambiguity means being unable to select clearly and decisively among many possibilities. The condition is common. When your mind is crowded with desires and competing threads, or when a block of discretionary time appears, you enter a state of “I want to do this and that; I could do this or that.” You stand perpetually at a crossroads without knowing which road to take, trapped in uncertainty.

Choice ambiguity is uncertainty, and **human beings instinctively avoid uncertainty**. In ancient times, when our ancestors saw movement in the grass without knowing its cause, they experienced intense stress in preparation for a lion that might leap out at any moment. To survive, “avoid uncertainty” was written into our genes. When the mind contains many vague options, we unconsciously select the clearest, simplest, and most certain one. In other words, **without a sufficiently clear instruction or goal, we easily choose pleasure and abandon the mentally demanding activity we ought to continue.**

To gain an advantage in modern life, we must spend more mental and emotional effort creating sufficiently clear goals. Refine and specify both objective and process. **Build a one-way channel through the many possibilities so that you remain in a state of “no choice.”**

Life, in short, is a contest to eliminate ambiguity. We compete not only in the speed of growth but in the mode of growth. Those who lead the field all do the same thing, consciously or otherwise: they eliminate ambiguity in cognition, emotion, and action.

Doing so requires us to act against instinct and is therefore a harder road. But there is no need to fear it. Gather your courage and face it.

### Section 2: Intuition—The Highest Form of Growth Turns Out to Be “Following Your Feelings”

Human beings compete through mental power, yet few know that the body contains an even more advanced system. Use it well and extraordinary achievement becomes possible.

In 1941, Germany launched fierce air raids over Britain. Prime Minister Winston Churchill often traveled by car at night to inspect antiaircraft positions. One evening, after inspecting a site, he prepared to leave. As an aide opened one car door for him, Churchill walked around, opened the door on the opposite side, and got in. Moments later a bomb exploded near the vehicle and almost overturned it. Had Churchill entered through the door his aide opened, he might have died. His wife later asked why he had changed sides. Churchill replied, “As I was about to get in, a voice told me, ‘Stop.’ Heaven seemed to tell me to open the other door and sit there, so I did.”

At this point, someone is bound to object: “Zhou Ling, don't you always advocate science and reason? Do you believe stories like this?” Be patient. I cite the story because I do believe it, and I will offer a reasonable explanation. First, however, let me tell a better-known story about another famous leader: US President Abraham Lincoln.

A friend once recommended a man for Lincoln's cabinet. After meeting him, Lincoln declined to appoint him. When the friend asked why, Lincoln said, “I don't like his face.” The friend protested: “How can you judge a man by his appearance? That is too harsh. He cannot be responsible for the face he was born with.” Lincoln answered, “No. After forty, every man is responsible for his face.”

Two famous figures made important decisions by feeling alone. Are you sure you do not want to understand the knowledge behind intuition?

#### The Wisdom of the Subconscious

Until now, I have repeatedly emphasized reason and treated vague feeling as part of the natural tendencies we must overcome. This time I want to restore intuition to its proper place.

For convenience, I will call reason the conscious mind and intuition the subconscious; that is essentially what they are. Academic views of the subconscious differ. Freud regarded it as dangerous territory harboring evil impulses that could return us to savagery. Carl Jung regarded it as wise, containing much that reason cannot reach and perhaps even humanity's collective wisdom.

Which was right? Modern research suggests that each was half right. The subconscious does not think. It cares only about what is immediately present and favors what is instant, certain, simple, and comfortable. This is its **instinctive side**. Yet it also processes information extraordinarily quickly—at least 11 million operations per second—and perceives signals too subtle for awareness. This is its **intuitive side**. Conscious rational thought processes only forty operations per second. The subconscious is 275,000 times faster. The difference in capacity is vast.

Imagine two people traveling from Beijing to Tianjin. One strolls there slowly; the other travels by rocket—even a plane or high-speed train cannot convey the size of the difference. This contrast creates a cognitive mismatch. The subconscious has already detected a great deal while consciousness still knows nothing. You may feel faint discomfort on first meeting someone, then quickly produce a polite smile. The subconscious detected an adverse signal, but the moment passed too rapidly for thought to register it. Consciousness instead generates analyses and reasons to make you accept the person, often with an unfortunate result. Churchill was not superstitious and Lincoln was not capricious. They caught a faint signal emitted by the subconscious. This is what “following your feelings” means: noticing information from below awareness.

In her 2015 TED talk, “Neuroscience Reveals the Secrets of How Men and Women Think,” Professor Daisy Hung described the following experience.

While she was studying at the University of California in the 1970s, a young assistant professor was robbed at an ATM. Too frightened to remember the robber's appearance, she could give the police no useful description. Afterward, however, she developed an inexplicable dislike for one of her graduate students. He was heavyset, wore shoulder-length hair and ripped trousers, and so on. She explained her dislike by saying that he ate hamburgers with onions and smelled unpleasant. Later, the police arrested several robbers and asked her to identify them. She recognized the culprit at once: he was heavyset, had shoulder-length hair, and wore ripped trousers.

Professor Hung explained that the assistant professor's subconscious had in fact remembered the robber and sent a signal that made her dislike a student who resembled him. The information never entered consciousness, so reason had no idea what was happening and invented an entirely unrelated explanation.

The intuitive subconscious is remarkably powerful. How many people realize that such an advanced system lies hidden within the body? Failing to use it would be a great waste. Its help can produce unexpected gains, especially in learning and growth.

#### Learn by Feeling

How can we use this advanced system while learning and growing? Cheng Jia, author of *Study Well*, provides an excellent example. In two essays—“The Simplest Method in History for Not Reading” and “The Invisible Key: An Ability More Important Than Knowledge”—he describes a fascinating approach: learn by feeling.

In the first essay, he introduces what he calls the “circuit-breaker method of not reading.” The moment you encounter something illuminating, trip the circuit breaker and stop reading. What should you do then? Ask yourself questions around the trigger:

· Why did this point inspire me?

· Can I apply this insight to three different situations?

· Is there similar knowledge connected with it?

In the other essay, he proposes that whenever something in life or learning strikes us emotionally, we should consciously ask why:

· Why did this scene in the film move me? What happened?

· Why do I like this product so much? What makes it different?

· Why have I become involuntarily absorbed in this plot?

This is how expert learners work: **first use intuition to choose, then use reason to think.** The emotional response comes from intuition; the questions come from reason. Feeling comes first and reason follows. The underlying principle is to perceive signals from the subconscious.

Learning expert Li Xiaopeng offers a similar method in *The Three Engines of Expert Learning*. When his secondary-school niece Zhao Lu asked him for the secret of learning, he gave only one answer: follow your feelings.

Zhao Lu could hardly believe him. He explained: “Whatever your present level, this works. Follow your feelings. Ignore questions whose answers are obvious at a glance. Also ignore questions that give you a headache at first sight and leave you with no idea what they mean. Work on questions where you can see the beginnings of an approach but still need to think. That middle ground is where you improve fastest.”

Can you see the pattern? Following your feelings is an advanced method because it helps identify what genuinely suits you and what you need, keeping you in the stretch zone of learning. If we use reason alone, we tend to compare ourselves with top students and focus on the hardest problems while wondering how to catch up. If we submit to instinct, we circle around the easiest ones (see Figure 2-1).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_12.jpg)

Figure 2-1 The choices favored by reason, intuition, and instinct

The same applies to reading. Using reason alone, we may spend enormous time after finishing a book reconstructing the author's framework and argument to prove we have understood it thoroughly. Following instinct, we may put down the book and play with the phone. A better method may be to wait a few days after finishing and ask: what point still affects me most? Hold tightly to that point, connect it with other things, and put it into practice. Set the rest aside for now. This is my own reading method: **take the single point in the entire book that affects me most, then practice it and change as much as possible. This creates greater value and less anxiety.**

Intuition can identify what truly suits us, direct our energy toward it, and accelerate improvement. In the stretch zone, the difficulty is lowest, the fit with our needs is closest, results arrive fastest, and flow is easier to enter. Learning may not be easy, but in the right zone it can still feel light and engaging. If it always feels painful and tedious, something is probably wrong: either you are suffering in the difficulty zone or circling inside the comfort zone.

#### Use Feeling to Find a Purpose in Life

Learning is only the tip of the iceberg. Intuition applies throughout life, especially when we face major questions such as choosing a partner, determining a career, or finding a life purpose. One of readers' greatest concerns about growth is the inability to find that purpose.

Without a purpose, a person can have food, drink, books, and work every day yet still feel lost—without inner joy or passion for life and perhaps even filled with self-disgust—because **a goal is where we place our passion and energy.**

To find a purpose, many people laboriously analyze what would be most worthwhile and arrive at “become very rich” or “be admired.” Such goals are not necessarily wrong, but they rarely endure or supply genuine motivation. They are products of rational calculation, weighing advantage against disadvantage and gain against loss. Their motivation often comes from self-acquisition and external judgment. Over time, they easily lead us astray and drain our drive.

People who are truly awake tend, consciously or otherwise, to **replace analytical thought with perception**. Yijia, author of *A Guide to Operating a Good Life*, offers six questions for finding a life's mission:

· Of all the things that can be done in this world, whom do you most want to help?

· What makes you forget food and sleep?

· While doing what do you move yourself most deeply?

· What moments in your life have moved others most deeply?

· If you faced no financial pressure, how would you spend the rest of your life?

· What kind of information attracts most of your attention in your free time?

We need to feel what moves us most, not calculate what offers the greatest advantage. Rational analysis cannot solve for the heart's true need. Only intuitive awareness and insight can bring the answer to the surface. And the right answer is often altruistic, because enduring meaning and happiness can come only through the response of other people.

Caroline Adams Miller, author of *Getting Grit*, asks three similar questions:

· Imagine that you are about to leave the world. Looking back, what would you regret?

· Who is the person you admire most?

· How did you spend your free time when you were young?

Answering these questions also requires **perception rather than analysis**. Looking directly at death simplifies everything and refocuses attention on what truly matters. Whether the people you admire are fictional or real, anyone who fascinates you deeply reflects the ideal self within. And before family and work burdens arrive, youthful pursuits tend to follow the heart without so much interference from external pressure.

Perhaps the seed of a life purpose was planted in each of us long ago. Under adult pressures, rational thought begins weighing gains and losses, refusing to acknowledge or deliberately ignoring the original dream, while intuition continues to protect and preserve it. If you have not yet found a purpose, try the methods above. They may surprise you.

In summary, rational thought is advanced, but it may have no advantage in judgment and choice. Its clumsy performance cannot match the speed and sensitivity of intuition. Choosing first by feeling and then thinking by reason may therefore be a better strategy, especially for major decisions. As Professor Hung advises: follow your head in small matters and your heart in great ones. There is wisdom in that.

#### How to Catch Intuitive Signals

Intuition may be powerful, but it seems elusive. How can we catch it? Consider the following methods.

**(1) The “most” method.** Notice whatever affects you most: the person or event that lights your eyes and stirs your heart, the idea that suddenly flashes through your mind, the pain you encounter. Learn to catch these points and investigate them deeply. They often yield rich rewards.

**(2) The “always” method.** Notice recurring thoughts that always surface without invitation or matters you can never put down. These are usually the things you care about most and the source of emotional fluctuation. Examine and resolve them consciously, and you become calmer.

**(3) The first unconscious response.** Notice the instant reaction or first thought that appears when you first meet someone, enter a room, or do something. Therapists often tell a patient, “Don't think. Tell me the first thing that entered your mind.” The first thought frequently contains authentic information from the subconscious. But it passes quickly and can be missed without deliberate practice because rational thought takes over almost at once.

**(4) Dreams.** Dreams are one way the subconscious transmits information. They may reveal genuine inner thoughts or provide inspiration. The German chemist August Kekulé, profoundly fatigued, dreamed of a snake biting its own tail. The image gave him a clue to the structure of the benzene molecule. His intelligent subconscious had found an answer and used a dream to suggest it; fortunately, Kekulé caught the signal.

**(5) The body.** Yijia once favored high-intensity exercise because reason told her, “No pain, no gain.” Yet every four months she became seriously ill for no apparent reason. A practitioner of traditional Chinese medicine finally told her: “Your body is striking. It is telling you that it dislikes your way of exercising. Learn to listen to its feedback.” She understood at once, adopted gentler forms of exercise, and stopped falling mysteriously ill. The body cannot speak, but it is utterly honest. Physical and psychological discomfort alike appear faithfully through it. Pay attention to that feedback.

**(6) Intuition itself.** Give unexplained information from an unknown source a green light, as Churchill did.

## Chapter 3: Metacognition—Humanity's Ultimate Ability

### Section 1: Metacognition—Growth Is Slow Because You Do Not Know How to “Fly”

On October 24, 1946, a group of scientists launched what was then the world's most advanced V-2 liquid-fueled rocket from the White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico to study the Sun's ultraviolet radiation. The rocket carried a 35 mm camera. At an altitude of about 104 kilometers, the camera activated and took a picture. That grainy, blurred, black-and-white image began a new era in which humanity could look back at itself from space.

After several more attempts, on August 7, 1959, the US satellite Explorer 6 captured the first full view of Earth. Humanity had acquired a “God's-eye view,” looking down on this extraordinary blue planet from an unprecedented perspective. Assisted by satellites, we could suddenly observe Earth with clarity, and social progress accelerated enormously. Communications, meteorology, navigation, mapping, and other technologies developed at extraordinary speed, and our immense planet became a “global village.” Today, digital-Earth technology is available to almost everyone.

I remember the first time I saw my hometown in Google Earth. I marveled inwardly: so this is what flying feels like. It was as if I had grown wings and could roam freely over every corner of the world. Yet humanity acquired a similar ability 150,000 years ago—not the physical power to fly, but the capacity to separate awareness from the self, “rise” to a higher place, and look back at ourselves.

Observe the world's outstanding people carefully and you will find that almost all of them move forward by “flying.” Perhaps we fail to keep pace because we do not know how. Most people never realize that they possess a pair of wings and never consider flapping them deliberately. Let me awaken that capacity in you, help you spread your wings out of confusion, and teach you to use humanity's ultimate ability: **metacognition**.

#### The Source of Humanity's Unique Intelligence

The Chinese prefix *yuan* can mean head, first, origin, or supreme—as in the term for a nation's head of state. Metacognition is cognition at the highest level: **the capacity to recognize and understand one's own thinking process** (see Figure 3-1).

The term sounds abstract, but metacognition is simply the familiar ability to reflect. This ability is uniquely human and is the source of our status as the most intelligent of creatures. Other animals do not possess it. Even gorillas, our closest genetic relatives, can do little more than recognize themselves in a mirror. They cannot detach from self and present circumstance to imagine “another self.” They lack the wings of metacognition and are born unable to fly.

Human beings are different. The brain's neocortex gives us exceptional powers of perception and thought, allowing us to live by reason while other animals survive through instinct and emotion. More remarkably, we can observe our own mental activity, identify what is irrational, revise it, and continually make better choices. The mind is like a hammer that can not only drive nails but reproduce another hammer with which to reshape itself. Applied correctly and revised often, the tool evolves into something more advanced.

Perhaps because we are born with this ability, we take it for granted. But viewed across evolutionary history, it is a gift every other species lacks. We have no reason to neglect it.

#### Levels of Metacognitive Ability

If everyone can reflect, why do people differ so dramatically? The answer is simple: metacognition has levels. In a world where everyone possesses it, only those with more advanced metacognition prevail. Psychology's levels of intentionality suggest at least six degrees, which can ultimately be grouped into two kinds: **passive and active metacognition**.

Ordinary people usually activate the ability only when a problem forces them to—when accusation or criticism compels reflection and correction. In favorable circumstances, they continue living by instinct: using the phone, sleeping late, and remaining unaware of whether their behavior is helpful or harmful. People who use metacognition passively seem to flap their wings only a few times when necessity leaves no alternative. Others practice even without a threat, continually evolving and moving far away from danger.

The shift from passive to active is decisive. When a person voluntarily adopts a third-person perspective and begins observing their own thought and behavior continuously, genuine awakening has begun and rapid growth becomes possible.

#### How Metacognition Changes Our Destiny

Looking back at oneself is where metacognition begins. Once you observe your own thought, something remarkable happens: **you become aware of what you are thinking, then whether those thoughts are wise, then how to correct the unwise ones, and finally how to make better choices.** Without self-observation, people act unconsciously according to feeling and preference. Physically and mentally, they pursue whatever is easiest and most comfortable now, unaware that anything is wrong until they collide with reality. A life consists of countless choices, and accumulated differences in choice produce different lives. If your life feels unsatisfactory, this is very likely where the problem lies.

Fortunately, change begins as soon as metacognition awakens. To experience it, imagine a “soul companion” always beside you, guiding you. When attention wanders, the companion reminds you to focus on something more important. When you feel lost, it rises above your life and reveals the larger situation. When you become angry, it helps organize the emotion and find a better option. When no answer appears, it descends to the underlying pattern and shows you what to grasp. When you grow lax, it stands at the end of your life and warns you what must be done now.

As Figure 3-2 shows, this is advanced metacognition. It continually lets you see your present self from higher, deeper, and more distant perspectives—remaining awake rather than lost, motivated rather than lax, calm rather than impulsive. With such an ability behind you, how could you fail to improve?

#### How to Acquire Metacognitive Ability

Once you know the concept, active metacognition has been irreversibly switched on. But that is not enough. Its scope is much broader, and developing the ability requires a systematic approach.

**First, Figure 3-2 shows that the tools for strengthening metacognition come from the “past”: learn from the wisdom of others and reflect on your own experience.**

Much accumulated wisdom is available in books. After reading *Peak*, for example, we need not avoid or fear difficulty in learning; we can use the theory of the comfort-zone edge to face challenge positively. After reading *Beyond Feelings*, we need not attack or disdain someone we dislike; we can try to learn whatever is real and useful from them. This is why lifelong learning matters. The wisdom of earlier thinkers gives us a broader overview—height—and a deeper command of underlying laws—depth. It helps us escape ignorance and make better choices.

Neuroscience and cognitive science deserve special attention because they directly describe our patterns of behavior. Studying them is like observing ourselves. Understanding the brain's structure reveals a “primitive self” and a “modern self” inside us, and shows that all behavior results from their contest. We can then learn how to guide the modern self toward victory and become stronger.

**Second, your own experience is a unique form of wealth.** Daily life flows past like a river; unless we pause, it is difficult to extract its wisdom. Reflection and review let us ask what experience can be preserved and what lesson learned, so that the next similar situation does not produce the same unwise choice. The ancient scholar Zengzi said, “Every day, I examine myself on three counts.” The ancients were already practicing reflection and review.

**Third, if learning and reflection are static, how can the self in the dynamic present use metacognition actively? It is simple: call on your soul companion.** Let it monitor you continually, like antivirus software watching every operation and sounding an alarm the moment a suspicious file appears.

Recall a familiar scene. You need to look up a document and open your phone. A red notification dot appears on WeChat, so you tap it without thinking. Someone has posted a funny video in Moments. You watch it and recognize in the background a song you have searched for a long time, so you open a music app to find it. Half an hour passes unnoticed, and the document you meant to locate has vanished from memory.

**We are always like this: we begin by looking for a piece of string and end up pulling out an elephant.** Sometimes we become trapped in Weibo, news feeds, or mobile games. Sometimes we touch one thing and then another, becoming frantically busy without knowing what we are doing. Sometimes an emotion traps us and consumes us for no reason. All are signs of weak metacognition: following our nature toward what is pleasant and comfortable, scattering energy without awareness, and letting any random interruption steal attention.

With a soul companion monitoring you, you can examine the behavior, step outside the process, and tell yourself: “This is optional. Wait until the important task is complete. Stop and decide what matters most; don't blindly do what is easy but unimportant. A few years from now, today's distress will look trivial. Instead of consuming myself, I can set the emotion aside and do something useful.”

Such alertness and change will not feel as comfortable as remaining the same, but it returns attention to the original goal, where you can focus and grow.

**Metacognition lets you survey the whole from above rather than plunging into life's details and becoming lost.** Look carefully and you will also find that **a future perspective is always a compass for present action**. It navigates the vast uncertainty of life and helps you choose what is more important rather than what is merely more interesting.

**Fourth, there are many ways to improve metacognition, but the most unexpected is meditation.** Yes, the practice in which you sit quietly, relax the body, and concentrate attention entirely on breathing and sensation.

The intense focus produced by meditation acts like exercise for the brain. With sustained practice, the brain can physically improve metacognition. Whenever you notice the mind wandering, gently return attention. Connect this with the soul companion described above and you will see that both practices do the same thing: **monitor attention and redirect it to where you need it.**

Feedback is the mechanism of evolution. Any system—mechanical design, software, or something else—can begin to evolve when feedback forms a loop. Metacognition is the feedback loop of human cognition. It is what gives us access to rapid evolution.

Useful and remarkable though metacognition is, I must offer one warning: mastering it is not easy. It requires practice, practice, and more practice. You will often find that you performed poorly. That is fine; begin again. Before long, you will notice that you are slowly becoming different from who you were.

That change is feedback too. Collect it and use it to encourage yourself. One day, you will become exceptional.

### Section 2: Self-Control—We Are Born to Take the Helm of Thought

Without metacognition, we could hardly call ourselves human. Without strong metacognition, we are unlikely to distinguish ourselves from the crowd. It is so important that it has been called humanity's ultimate ability. Is it merely the self-awareness described above? No. Self-awareness is only its foundation. In actual life, metacognition also provides powerful guidance for self-control. We might say that **metacognition combines awareness and self-control.** In practical terms, it can be redefined as **the ability to examine oneself, exercise deliberate control, and prevent the subconscious from taking command.**

#### We Are Naturally Governed by the Subconscious

Perhaps you dismiss “self-examination and deliberate control,” believing that you can examine yourself at any moment and easily control your thoughts, words, and actions. If so, you may have understood only the phrase's surface. Consider the following scenes.

On waking, our first response is usually to reach for the phone without thought. That habitual, thoughtless reaction comes from the subconscious. What we should actually do is get up, dress, and wash, then check messages once everything is in order. Otherwise, information pulls us from one item to another, and tens of minutes later we are still in bed. In that moment, do we possess self-examination and deliberate control? Apparently not.

Now widen the frame to a full day. At day's end, many people ask themselves: what did I do all day? I hardly touched the most important work, but completed a mountain of miscellaneous tasks. They awaken, resolve painfully to begin with the most important task tomorrow—and then unconsciously repeat the same cycle. The subconscious still seems to rule, and self-control remains absent.

Widen the frame again to completing a major undertaking or achieving a life goal. Seeking a good life, many people set goals of rising early, running, reading, and writing, then abandon them within days. Most were adopted under social influence: others said they were good, so we wanted them too, though we did not genuinely need them. Look closely and you will see another first reaction governed by the subconscious. No real self-examination or deliberate control occurred.

From this moment, through each day, to the span of a life, the subconscious naturally governs us.

#### Growth Means Taking Deliberate Control

At birth, the rational brain is undeveloped and has virtually no strength. We possess only instinct. Show a baby anything and it captures their attention; the environment directs attention and natural instinct controls behavior completely.

As we grow, the prefrontal cortex develops and the rational brain gradually becomes stronger. Its power appears in two forms: **ordinary cognitive ability, focused on learning, understanding, memory, and calculation**, which schools mainly train; and **metacognitive ability, focused on observation, reflection, judgment, and choice** (see Figure 3-3).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_15.jpg)

Figure 3-3 Ordinary cognition and metacognition are two expressions of the rational brain's power

Unfortunately, while most of us train ordinary cognition intensively in school, we receive little training in metacognition. That is why many people still struggle with execution, concentration, and willpower after decades of life. To master the sails of our own destiny, we must train metacognition deliberately. The rational brain must participate more in decisions and take the leading role. Then we can travel faster and farther than most.

The transfer of control has a visible sign: a person can direct attention deliberately and is not ruled at will by random, entertaining information. Walking down a street, someone with weak metacognition is easily captured by roadside music, video screens, vendors' cries, and sudden events. Someone with strong metacognition spends a second or two deciding whether the stimulus deserves attention.

In Xiao Masong's *Top Students in Your WeChat Moments*, one subject, Liu Danni, observes: “The purpose of education is to teach you how to regard something when you encounter it. Your natural disposition is always present in your reaction. If someone insults you, for example, you want to insult them back. But if there is an interval—even a fraction of a second—in which you think or examine the reaction, that interval is the meaning of your education and experience.” This describes metacognition's role in decision-making beautifully. That fraction of a second is crucial.

It is now easy to understand why short-video apps such as Douyin and Kuaishou are so difficult to stop watching. The instant one video ends, the system automatically starts the next. Instinct and emotion hijack the brain throughout the process, leaving no opportunity for reason to activate. If you want to pull yourself out, tell yourself beforehand: “When this video ends, pause for a few seconds.” Once the rational brain receives time to examine and reflect, we can usually regain control.

The same principle applies throughout life. A few seconds to think after waking may help us choose getting up over checking the phone. Pausing when a WeChat notification appears may lead us to do important work instead of tapping the red dot. **Whenever a choice appears, pausing for a few seconds can activate the rational brain, use metacognition to inspect current thought, and produce a different decision.**

The gap between influential, outstanding people and everyone else consistently appears in metacognition. At choices large and small, the former escape subconscious control. They observe and think about their environment, behavior, and relationships, develop reasoned views, and make better decisions. Some see more dimensions of meaning and invest a goal with powerful value, giving them greater concentration, execution, and willpower. Some perceive other people's thoughts and restrain their own words and actions, appearing more emotionally intelligent. Their true competitive advantage lies not in learning ability but in powerful metacognition. Many brilliant students possess strong rational brains and exceptional learning or computational ability, yet still fail to live well. We must exercise metacognition as we exercise muscle; frequent training makes it stronger and easier to activate.

This is not easy. Cognitive exercise requires as much energy as physical exercise, as well as sustained practice and sound methods. Fortunately, the method itself is simple: **spend more “meta-time” at points of choice.**

#### Take the Helm of Your Life's Thought

“Meta-time” is a term I coined. It is useful because although every second in a twenty-four-hour day looks the same, its importance is not. Some moments carry far greater weight than others. I call them meta-time.

**Meta-time usually appears at points of choice: the beginning or end of a task, a phase, or a day. Using these moments well greatly improves the quality of all the time that follows.** Any moment involving a choice can therefore be called meta-time. We must not surrender initiative then and let instinct carry us into the next stage, especially when temptation or difficulty appears. What should we do in meta-time? One simple thing: **think clearly**.

Fail to think clearly at a point of choice and you enter ambiguity—the territory of the subconscious—which triggers the instinctive response of seeking entertainment. The basic strategy is therefore to **inspect your first reaction at the point of choice and form a clear, explicit position.**

If you want to speak well, for example, adopt one rule: think twice before speaking. Words blurted out usually come from instinct. Pause for one or two seconds before they emerge and let the rational brain inspect them. You may change your mind, choose different words, or remain silent. Sometimes the best answer is no answer.

The instant we wake, pick up the phone, or return home, a new choice appears. We must deliberately spend mental energy examining it. This is more tiring, but it is also the best moment to train metacognition—as if lifting dumbbells with the mind and strengthening the rational brain.

**Thinking clearly requires not only inspecting the first response but forming an explicit position.** On a weekend, our first choice may be sleeping late. After awareness and examination, we may choose to study instead. Yet the choice can remain vague, because everything we wanted to do during the week has accumulated: this book and that one, an article to write, exercise, and more. Each seems equally important, so indecision wastes the time. This is plainly not strong metacognition; we are suspended among many options and have returned to ambiguity.

**One defining feature of strong metacognition is zero tolerance for ambiguity.** It means using every available method to identify the single most important option, leaving only one road through a given period. The logic is simple: if all the options truly carry similar weight, choosing any of them entails no loss. Indecision—wanting to do everything and doing nothing well—is the greatest loss.

Think back: **whenever your capacity for action was weak, the concrete next steps in your mind were almost certainly unclear.** The best rescue is to list everything you want to do, rank the items, identify the most important one, and restore clarity.

Ambiguity must be removed not only from small matters but from major choices such as life goals. In practice, we plunge into concrete activity without thought, while remaining profoundly unclear about what matters, what matters most, or what the activity means over the long term.

To you, reading may mean moving the eyes over words and finishing a book quickly. To someone else, it means conversing with a person of a higher level. Give reading that meaning and motivation changes completely. When meaning remains obscure, we enter the state of “others say it is good, so I want it too.” We try to learn everything, demand immediate results, act blindly, accomplish nothing, and become more anxious.

Anxious people rarely understand meta-time. They act without thinking and like to move at full capacity in order to impress themselves. The ratio between time spent thinking and doing becomes grotesquely unbalanced. They refuse to devote even a little time to deep thought and let instinctive desire lose itself in self-satisfying activity.

Autopilot is certainly easy. But on autopilot, we merely watch the roadside scenery rush past without knowing where we are going or where we will end. A life permanently out of control is a tragedy.

To summarize, there are three ways to take the helm of thought:

· In the present moment, remain aware, inspect your first response, and form a clear position.

· Across the day's schedule, remain awake and always know what you will do next.

· For long-term goals, keep thinking and clarify their enduring meaning and inner motivation.

**People with strong metacognition do this: whether directing present attention, arranging the day, or choosing a lifelong aim, they seek clear meaning, examine themselves, and exercise deliberate control instead of drifting with the current.**

If life is an ocean, each of us is a small boat. People with strong metacognition keep a hand on the rudder and deliberately steer the vessel of life. Those with weak metacognition prefer to remain on deck as busy sailors, letting the boat drift wherever it will.

Maxim Gorky once wrote that every act of self-restraint makes us stronger than before. I did not understand him in the past; now I do. Each time we restrain ourselves, we perform an act of self-examination and deliberate control—one repetition of training. Exercise metacognition often, and the rational brain's self-control can only grow stronger.

## Chapter 4: Focus—Where Emotion and Intelligence Meet

### Section 1: Emotional Focus—One Move That Restores Your Attention

Observing attention through metacognition is fascinating. You can probably recognize this phenomenon easily: the body is doing A while the mind is thinking about B.

· While running, the arms and legs move, but the mind arranges tomorrow and the day after.

· While eating, the mouth moves, but the mind worries about a relationship.

· While sleeping, the body lies still, but thoughts pour out like a waterfall.

These scenes are so common that we call them distraction or mind-wandering. You may not regard them as a problem and may even feel proud of your ability to do two things at once. Yet the pattern of doing A while thinking B genuinely affects us. Without noticing, we create needless trouble and gradually dull the mind. In one sense, this is the source of both our distress and our incapacity.

#### Action Is the Body; Feeling Is the Soul

To see this clearly, divide attention into its components. In any activity, part of our attention focuses on action and another part on feeling:

· While running, running is the action; everything else is feeling.

· While eating, eating is the action; everything else is feeling.

· While sleeping, sleeping is the action; everything else is feeling.

At first, action and feeling are united.

We experience an activity with our whole being and place all attention on what relates to the present. Running is simply running, eating is eating, sleeping is sleeping. This is usually how we act while first learning a skill or while still children. We immerse ourselves readily, remain receptive, peaceful, and free of worry, and possess abundant vitality.

As an action becomes familiar, it requires less attention, leaving more to scatter elsewhere. We stop experiencing the action patiently. Distraction replaces focus, and body and mind separate (see Figure 4-1).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_16.jpg)

Figure 4-1 Unity and separation of body and mind

Action without feeling resembles a body without a soul. A person without feeling is absent from everything, looking without seeing and listening without hearing. More precisely, we have placed a chaotic soul inside the body, forever doing A while thinking B: the mind wanders while brushing teeth, walking, showering—at every moment.

When the mind wanders, action loses perception. Attention, deprived of feeling, cannot form a feedback loop, and the body and movements unconsciously become numb or distorted. Test it now: when your mind wanders, is some part of the body always rigid and some part of the expression always tense?

The physical effect is minor compared with the lasting harm that separation of body and mind does to emotional state and ability.

#### Why the Mind Wanders—and What It Costs

There are only two reasons for distraction. The present feels too boring, so we seek something more interesting; or it feels too painful, so we seek something more comfortable. The body is trapped in reality, so thought escapes into fantasy.

Wherever we are and whatever happens, the slightest dissatisfaction lets the mind soar away from difficulty and enjoy imagined comfort. Mental escape costs almost nothing, while our nature demands quick results and avoids difficulty. By default, then, we unconsciously remain in a psychological comfort zone.

Mind-wandering feels good for a moment, but afterward we pay its costs. The greatest is a lower quality of life. While distracted, we are immersed in the past, worried about the future, or fantasizing about the impossible. The wandering mind can let us live anywhere except the present.

Life consists of present moments. Moments in which body and mind are united create a focused, happy, high-quality life. Moments in which they are separated create a distracted, low-quality one. Wandering also causes procrastination and inefficiency. Emotion lags behind behavior, so entering a task takes time while our feelings make the transition.

Mind-wandering is therefore a form of escape. When difficulty appears, people whose body and mind are divided retreat unconsciously into the comfort zone; those whose body and mind are united can more readily leave it and face the difficulty.

Over the long term, a person's capacity for focus may predict the scale of future achievement. When Bill Gates and Warren Buffett first met, Gates's father gave each a card and asked him to write one word describing what had made him successful. Their answers were identical: focus.

We need not blame ourselves excessively for distraction. At the smallest scale, mind-wandering is another natural human tendency. It affects everyone and arises from the brain's memory mechanism.

Human memory cannot match a computer in either capacity or precision, yet we retrieve memories quickly because the brain uses context-associated memory. Background and cues call specific content to mind: a name, voice, time, scene, or other single element can instantly evoke a person or event. A computer, by contrast, treats information uniformly and searches the database item by item. Contextual association greatly reduces the brain's energy use and compensates for neurons' slow processing speed.

Evolution is a double-edged sword, however. One side effect of associative memory is that anything our senses hear, see, touch, taste, or smell can evoke other memories. Because the senses are controlled by the subconscious, which never disappears, distraction can occur whenever we are awake. This is another reason to train metacognition. Growth means overcoming our natural defaults. Unless awareness and self-control restrain them, the subconscious will govern us without our knowledge.

#### Recover Feeling and Return to the Present

A person who develops complete engagement and clear boundaries in childhood gains not only intellectual sharpness but emotional peace. Reinforced over time, this habit creates an enormous gap from people who never realize that attention contains both action and feeling. Learning the principle early and applying it deliberately could change the course and quality of a life. Yet it is not too late now. One move can reverse the situation: **bring feeling back into action.**

While running, recover feeling and experience each lifted leg, swinging arm, inhalation, exhalation, and breeze against the face. While going to sleep, feel tension and relaxation in the body. While eating, experience the flavor of every bite and the entire process through which taste appears and fades; do not force in the second bite before finishing the first.

**Bodily sensation is always the best medium for entering the present, and observing the disappearance of a sensation is excellent training in focus.** It shows that **uniting body and mind means not only focusing on the present but enjoying it.** That enjoyment makes us composed rather than hurried.

Practice recovering feeling and returning attention to the present. Troubles gradually decrease, energy rises, emotion becomes calmer, the body softer, perception more sensitive, and thought deeper. This habit touches every part of life. Changing it changes a foundational pattern of behavior, and its power should not be underestimated.

Let me close with a story you may have heard. With today's discussion in mind, its deeper meaning should be clear.

A traveler asked an old monk, “What did you do before attaining the Way?”

The monk answered, “Chopped wood, carried water, and cooked.”

“And after attaining the Way?”

“Chopped wood, carried water, and cooked.”

“Then what does attaining the Way mean?”

The monk replied, “Before attaining the Way, while chopping wood I thought about carrying water, and while carrying water I thought about cooking. After attaining the Way, when I chop wood, I chop wood; when I carry water, I carry water; when I cook, I cook.”

### Section 2: Focus in Learning—Deep Immersion Is the Safe Handle of Evolution's Double-Edged Sword

Two million years ago, humans, chimpanzees, and gorillas still belonged to the same species. Humanity then broke away from the ape family and rapidly evolved toward *Homo sapiens*. Evolution gave us a highly developed nervous system and exceptional capacities for perception and thought, through which we created civilization.

But evolution is a double-edged sword. It brought ability and pain together. We grow restless because we perceive too much information, suffer because we generate too many desires, and become anxious because we fear our abilities are insufficient. In prosperity or adversity, we find no peace. Today we may have adequate food and clothing yet still suffer because we cannot escape the phone's distractions, acquire enviable skills, or obtain the life we want. Lower animals have no such trouble. Their minds contain only information that actually exists in the environment, directly concerns them, and can be judged intuitively. A hungry lion notices only what helps it catch an antelope; a full lion rests its attention on the warm sun.

Enjoying evolution's benefits means bearing its pain. But there is no need for despair. Some wise people have consciously or unconsciously escaped this limitation by adopting a highly effective pattern of behavior that keeps both emotion and ability peaceful and efficient. If evolution is a double-edged sword, these people have found and grasped its safe handle. While the crowd unknowingly grips the blade and injures itself as it strikes, they hold the hilt and cut a path forward.

#### The Fundamental Difference in Human Emotion and Ability

Begin with the ability to select information deliberately. Differences in human emotion and ability arise from differences in how attention is directed. Compared with a distracted person, a meditator can direct attention toward breathing and sensation while excluding unrelated thoughts.

What is true of emotion is true of ability. Less capable people are easily distracted. They can learn only in an ideal environment; any disturbance unsettles them. They continually want to do something more interesting. A trending story or amusing conversation can pull attention away from important work. Capable people are the reverse. Their advantage is the ability to block interference, select relevant information, and immerse themselves in it. Some even practice deliberately in noisy places, developing the ability to enter deep reading and thought anywhere.

Differences in immersion ultimately place people at different levels. Broadly speaking, those with strong immersion often occupy positions of control, while those with weak immersion are often controlled. Anyone who hopes to stand out must train this quality deliberately. It may be the golden key that changes our fate.

#### How to Enter Deep Immersion

I introduced two concepts above: deliberate selection of information and deep immersion. Selection is only the entrance; immersion is the key. Someone able to choose information may still fail to become absorbed. Many people can put down the phone, pick up a book, abandon entertainment, practice a skill, and even train in such volume that their effort moves them—yet never become outstanding. They have found the safe handle of the double-edged sword but do not know how to grip it.

Many can focus, but few excel, partly because most lack deep immersion. Passion alone cannot create it. Immersion is a technique with a method. Unfortunately, accomplished predecessors who possess the ability rarely explain what it is or how to acquire it. Fortunately, *Peak* provides a broad answer.

After extensive research, psychologist Anders Ericsson and science writer Robert Pool concluded that genius is not mysterious. At heart, it is the combination of the right method and a great deal of practice. If we do not become exceptionally good, either the method is wrong or the practice is insufficient.

Most unguided effort is what they call “naive practice”: repeating an activity while expecting repetition alone to improve performance. This is very different from focused effort using the right method, which generally has four characteristics.

**First, a clearly defined goal.** If you are practicing piano, tell yourself: “Play the piece three times in a row at the appropriate tempo without a single error,” rather than setting the vague target “practice piano for half an hour.” The more precisely the goal is defined, the finer attention's resolution, the more concentrated the energy, and the greater the refinement of skill. If a goal is too large, break it into smaller ones to make it concrete and precise.

**Second, extreme focus during practice.** Everyone knows concentration matters, but immersion requires it in the extreme. Investing 100 percent of your energy for a short period is better than investing 70 percent for a long one. The true fuel of concentration is not willpower or patience but the continual discovery of subtle differences in technique and of points that still require attention. The more concentrated the energy, the subtler the perception.

Extreme focus is not only the key to learning but also a source of inspiration. As Figure 4-2 shows, Barbara Oakley explains in *A Mind for Numbers* that the brain uses two modes in learning: the conscious focused mode and the subconscious diffuse mode.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_17.jpg)

Figure 4-2 Working modes of consciousness and the subconscious

In focused mode, concentration on a subject causes the prefrontal cortex to send signals along neural pathways toward brain regions related to the thought, linking them together. This may or may not produce an answer, because the real answer does not necessarily lie in the areas receiving conscious attention. The diffuse subconscious mode can then help. It lets the brain leave its original working area and allows neurons to connect randomly with unrelated regions, perhaps producing a solution.

The subconscious can work only under one condition: waking consciousness must shut down completely and forget the original problem. The two modes resemble beams from a flashlight. In focused mode, the beam is narrow and penetrating, striking a small area. In diffuse mode, it spreads; the intensity decreases, but the illuminated area grows. One flashlight cannot produce both beams at once.

The secret to becoming smarter is therefore to focus intensely first and, if no answer comes, shift attention entirely to something unrelated. Concentrate beforehand and let consciousness invest itself completely; forget afterward and let consciousness release the problem completely. Inspiration and answers are then much more likely to appear.

Archimedes could not devise a way to determine whether a crown was genuine despite exhausting his conscious thought, so he went to a public bath to relax. As his body entered the tub, the displaced water supplied the insight. Many examples show scientific discoveries and other intellectual breakthroughs appearing when people expected nothing and were thinking about something else.

A sound learning pattern therefore gives A complete attention while doing A and B complete attention while doing B, with a sharp boundary between them. Think of B while doing A and A while doing B, and conscious thought never reaches sufficient depth while the subconscious cannot activate smoothly. Poor boundaries seriously damage ability. Li Dazhao put it well: “When you study, study in earnest; when you play, play to your heart's content.” Clear boundaries cultivate both character and competence.

**Third, effective feedback.** Whatever we do, feedback helps us identify precisely where and why we remain deficient. Without it, we make mistakes and lose attention easily, while ability improves slowly. Guidance from a coach is excellent; criticism from a teacher is valuable too. Practicing in isolation both encourages distraction and traps us at a low level. Timely, effective guidance and feedback are therefore essential to continual refinement. When conditions are limited, feedback can also come from books and videos, conversations with others, or self-reflection.

**Fourth, always practice in the stretch zone.** Repeating what you have mastered is pointless, but tackling an excessively difficult task creates frustration. Neither produces immersion. The best state lies between them.

In *Flow*, psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi proposes the model shown in Figure 4-3. When an activity feels boring, raise the difficulty. When it creates anxiety, remain at that level and practice with concentration. Alternating in this way keeps us in the flow channel and absorbed in the work.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_18.jpg)

Figure 4-3 The flow channel

Every one of us has become so absorbed in something we enjoy that we lost track of time and felt no fatigue, whether the activity was entertainment, learning, or research. Such immersion is a spontaneous state that arrives unpredictably. Achievement in a field cannot depend on this unstable accident; we need a reliable pattern of behavior. We face not only interests but intimidating core difficulties.

Every day, then, we should do things that feel somewhat difficult but remain achievable through effort: leave the comfort zone, avoid the difficulty zone, and stay in the stretch zone.

Fortunately, the four principles above let us establish a deliberate pattern of immersion (see Figure 4-4). Practice it often and it solidifies into a foundational capacity for deep immersion that influences every area of life.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_19.jpg)

Figure 4-4 The four elements of deliberate practice

I began applying deliberate practice the day I learned the theory.

Previously, when my daughter practiced piano, her mother asked her to play a new piece ten times. Once she reached the count, the task was complete. I changed the approach using deliberate-practice principles.

I first listened to her play and noticed several uncertain, error-prone passages. I asked her to practice only the first section that day and leave the rest alone—breaking a large goal into a small one. She practiced only the passage she had just played incorrectly—working in the stretch zone—and would finish after playing it fluently three times in succession without an error—a concrete, clear goal. During practice, I promptly corrected fingering and keystroke errors—timely, effective feedback. She quickly entered a focused state—immersion—and soon played the first section very well.

At the end, my daughter exclaimed that she was exhausted, but she was plainly full of accomplishment because the hardest passage no longer frightened her. Without those requirements, she would have played familiar parts again and again, skimmed over the difficult ones, and repeatedly stopped without attention. Such practice is profoundly inefficient.

Internalize these four elements and you can enter deep immersion, moving from focus toward excellence. True excellence, however, requires one further ingredient: extensive practice.

How much? The young pianist Chen Anke offers a reference point. She began at three and a half and, eighteen months later, performed a Grade 8 piece on television. In one interview, she said she practiced four hours every day. In an earlier interview, she had already said, “I practice every day. I never miss one.”

Genius requires enormous practice—or rather, genius is created by the right method combined with enormous practice. Whoever acquires the capacity for deep immersion can rise toward the heights of a field.

From now on, examine yourself carefully.

· Examine your attention: is it captured passively or selected deliberately?

· Examine your immersion: is the mind wandering or completely focused?

· Examine your practice volume: do you merely sample the work, or invest at scale?

The wisdom of those before us is enough to lead us toward excellence. Gather it with care, and we can realize ourselves in the tide of evolution while benefiting others.

## Chapter 5: The Ability to Learn—Learning Is Not Simply Working Harder

### Section 1: Matching—A Universal Method at the Edge of the Comfort Zone

The deep immersion described in the previous chapter is not the true heart of deliberate practice. Its real core is matching difficulty to ability.

The word “matching” is easily overlooked, but once we study it, we discover a method that applies almost everywhere. This is no exaggeration; the principle's reach is extraordinarily broad.

#### Good Growth Always Moves Along the Edge of the Comfort Zone

As I was writing this section, a reader named Amy Cao happened to share several realizations with me. I smiled as soon as I read them, because her experience perfectly illustrated matching. Let us begin with her story.

The first example concerned running. She wrote: “I used to require myself to run for an hour every day. I kept it up through willpower for quite a while, but eventually stopped. Recently I changed each run to thirty minutes, ideally at least four times a week. After the adjustment, I found that I no longer need as much willpower. I actively look for ways to continue, and I feel relaxed afterward instead of exhausted and uncomfortable as I did after an hour. I can genuinely feel the difference between actively choosing to do it now and forcing myself through willpower before.”

The second concerned English: “An hour of study every day used to make me irritable. Now I stop after thirty minutes, no matter what. As a result, I can study every day without growing tired of it.”

She concluded: “Finding a way you can sustain matters more than following a standardized duration and method. I used to think that only more time could produce better learning and results. In fact, I was demanding quick results, which made persistence harder. Now that I have lowered the difficulty and standard, my ability to act has grown more sustainable. Reaching the goal may take longer, but I believe persistence will eventually compound.”

What do you make of Amy Cao's story? To me, its greatest value is that she deliberately lowered the intensity and difficulty into the range she could best tolerate, preserving both accomplishment and challenge.

For most people, this feels counterintuitive. When we want to accomplish something, we usually tell ourselves to work desperately hard, set an extremely high standard, and repeatedly pump ourselves up with slogans such as “persistence is victory.” That is our default way of thinking—but default does not mean scientific. What does a scientific approach look like?

Recall Figure 1-4. The optimal learning region lies in the stretch zone at the edge of the comfort zone. There we experience both accomplishment and challenge and improve fastest. That is what matching means: neither too difficult nor too easy. Moderate difficulty creates the flow channel for learning.

Amy Cao began in the difficulty zone. Wanting rapid change, she designed study and training plans far beyond her present ability. The experience became so painful that she quit midway. This resembles a common motivational pattern. Many inexperienced young people try to achieve too many goals, all too large and within too little time, unconsciously pushing themselves into the difficulty zone. They begin excitedly, work feverishly for several days, and then lose all energy. This is why so many efforts end halfway.

The matching principle applies far beyond learning. Almost every field I can observe follows it.

In strength training, for example, pushing against resistance creates tiny tears in muscle fibers. These cause soreness without injury. After rest and nutrition, the muscle repairs itself and becomes stronger. When a trainer asks us to continue until failure, they are pushing the muscle out of its comfort zone and into the stretch zone.

The same is true of other forms of exercise. Many people try to lose weight by running and begin with an all-out sprint, mistaking pain for proof of effort. Professional advice looks almost lazy by comparison. A coach may suggest jogging until slightly breathless, switching to a brisk walk until breathing settles, then jogging again, repeating this for half an hour. During roughly the first twenty minutes of aerobic exercise, the body mainly uses sugar; after thirty minutes, the proportion of fat burned rises substantially. We therefore need only persist briefly at the comfort zone's edge, return to comfort to recover, and then move out again. During the next ten to fifteen minutes, if strength permits, run as fast as possible—or at least at greater intensity than during the first thirty minutes—to burn more fat, since the body has adapted and can move farther beyond the edge.

Consider reading. Many people ask accomplished readers for book lists, assuming every recommendation must be excellent. They buy a large stack enthusiastically, only to discover that the books are not nearly as good for them: some are obscure, difficult, and impossible to continue. Interest disappears within days. Each person has a different knowledge background. A book may sit perfectly in an expert's stretch zone but fall within our difficulty zone. Set it aside for now and read something that interests you and that you can understand. Match interest, difficulty, and need at the edge of the comfort zone, and you will read with delight.

The same is true of academic study. Students with poor results often begin their attempt to catch up by competing through effort. They tackle the same difficult problems as top students. The top students work comfortably while they suffer, and the gap grows wider. The same material may lie in a strong student's stretch zone and in their own difficulty zone. The correct response is to settle down and deliberately lower the difficulty.

Once you understand the principle, spend substantial time identifying what lies in your stretch zone: material **you can do but frequently get wrong, or cannot yet do but can understand with modest effort.** Work there. If you are a parent, spend time finding your child's stretch zone and guide them to work at the edge of comfort. Do not respond to poor results only with anger, comparisons to other children, more work, and greater difficulty; that usually produces the opposite of what you want.

People also complain that their minds wander while studying. The cause is often the same: they have not deliberately monitored difficulty or adjusted the pace. My friend Song Dinghua is a senior engineer whom everyone calls Brother Song. His child, now in secondary school, consistently ranks near the top while never attending extracurricular classes and still having plenty of time for games. During one gathering, I happened to sit beside Brother Song and asked whether he used any special learning methods. To my surprise, he answered immediately: “Two. Treat homework like an exam, and when there is a problem, look only for causes within yourself.”

I was puzzled, especially by the first rule. Later I understood: treating homework like an exam creates an appropriate learning rhythm. At home, most children face few constraints. They drift, visit the bathroom, fetch water, stare blankly when stuck, or ask for help immediately. It looks like continuous study, but it is really time-wasting inside the comfort zone—inefficient and error-prone. Under exam conditions, they must concentrate, answer as many questions as possible in the shortest time, and answer them correctly. This naturally pushes them to the comfort zone's edge, where attention, efficiency, and results improve.

#### We Cannot Control What Lies Too Far Away

Difficult activities require movement along the comfort zone's edge. But what about things requiring no effort and offering pleasure—such as suddenly receiving abundant time or money? Most people assume more is always better. Be careful: **we usually cannot control what lies too far from our present capacity, whether painful or pleasurable.**

In the summer of 2019, a young teacher wrote: “Others envy my winter and summer vacations, but I dislike them. I simply cannot control so much free time. None of my plans are completed, and even my sleep schedule collapses. I have no control over my holidays at all.”

Many university students send similar messages. The intense structure of their final year of secondary school suddenly disappears. They begin the term with discipline but soon grow lax, stay in the dormitory playing games and watching Douyin, and lose the ability to concentrate. When alone and in control of their own time, they unconsciously choose the most comfortable entertainment. Their freedom has exceeded their capacity to manage it. They have lost control.

Do not assume that a life without constraints is ideal. Completely free time feels comfortable at first, but we quickly become lost among options. We could do this or that. **Choosing consumes enormous energy.** Without matching clarity and steadiness, most people eventually fall under the rule of their nature and choose entertainment. Under constraints, we are often more efficient and live more fully.

Sudden wealth is unlikely for most of us, but other people's experiences are instructive. In 2002, British refuse collector Michael Carroll won £9.7 million and became spectacularly rich overnight. He bought mansions and luxury cars, used drugs, and gambled. Within seven years the fortune was gone, his wife had left, and he returned to manual labor while living on benefits. In 2006, Wendy Graham in Britain won £1 million and spent it within a year, returning to poverty. Statistics have placed the annual bankruptcy rate among US lottery winners as high as 75 percent. We should learn from such cases, soberly assess our ability to control desire, and avoid the same tragedy.

The ideal is to acquire freedom and wealth continuously at a level matched to our current ability. Parents should take note: assess a child's capacity to manage freedom and money, then release control or offer encouragement in appropriate amounts at appropriate times. That is wisdom. Indulgent parents often give a young child broad decision-making power over food, play, and activity. The child lacks the capacity to manage it and becomes self-important and selfish. The root problem is failure to understand matching.

People often speak of foundational concepts and laws. What makes an idea foundational? In my view, **the more phenomena a concept can explain, the more foundational it is.** Once you understand matching, you can explain other questions yourself. Someone asks, “To improve as a writer, should I publish daily or weekly?” You can answer: “Either can work. What matters is whether your practice remains at the edge of your comfort zone. If everything you produce is casual writing from inside the comfort zone, volume will not help.” The answer remains open while reaching the heart of the issue.

This foundational concept supports a powerful conclusion: whatever you do and however well or poorly you currently do it, sustained practice at the edge of your comfort zone continually expands that zone. The stretch zone expands with it, and what was once difficult gradually becomes stretch work and eventually comfortable. **Growth is therefore inevitable.**

It also proves that **overnight mastery is impossible.** A circle of competence expands only a little at a time. Follow the law of matching, continue extending yourself at the edge of comfort, and make time your ally; you are destined to keep growing and remaking yourself.

#### Everything Serves the Match

The four elements of deliberate practice look independent, but they interlock and ultimately point toward matching.

The first element, goals, solves a central problem in action. Whenever action becomes blocked, we feel helpless. Look closely, however, and every blockage shares the same root: **the problem is too large and too vague.**

**Break the goal down.** Divide a large objective into small ones and the task moves immediately from the difficulty zone into the stretch zone, making action possible. Observe carefully and you will find that **almost everyone who excels at execution also excels at decomposing tasks.**

The same principle yields a strategy for moving from comfort into stretch: **refine the goal.** Work inside the comfort zone is characterized by mindless repetition. Habit and feeling run the activity, nothing demands particular attention, and the mind wanders while studying, running, or even trying to sleep. Little improvement can occur.

**Practice in the stretch zone always has specific points of attention.** The more numerous and precise those points, the more concentrated attention becomes and the more visible the improvement. The best way to leave comfort is therefore to discover and collect these points—the small goals of each attempt. When practicing piano, do not repeat the whole piece endlessly; isolate the passage with the most mistakes. When learning vocabulary, do not reread the list again and again; close the book, test yourself, identify the missed words, and repeat only those until they are mastered.

Once the goal is clear, extreme focus follows naturally. Self-testing, reflection, and error notebooks then provide feedback, continually refining the points of attention and the next small goals.

Learning is not merely trying harder. Growth does not depend only on motivational excitement or willpower. Stand at the edge of the comfort zone, move outward one step at a time, and make time your friend. Transformation will arrive almost without your noticing.

### Section 2: Depth—Deep Learning Is One of Life's Few Reliable Paths

Wang Yunwu, a publisher who taught the scholar Hu Shi English, taught himself to write in English through the following method. He selected a fine English essay by a master, read it closely several times, and translated it into Chinese. A week later, without consulting the original, he translated his Chinese version back into English. He then compared the result with the original and identified every error, misstep, and inelegant phrase.

Through repeated practice, Wang built a strong command of English and laid a firm foundation for his later work in language education and publishing. In an age when technology and information were far less developed, limited conditions forced people to settle down and learn deeply.

Decades later, society has changed beyond recognition. We live amid unprecedented material and informational abundance. Few people today willingly practice deep learning as Wang did. Many believe that learning no longer needs to be so laborious. Knowledge seems available effortlessly: listen to a book each day, attend an online course by a celebrity, subscribe to an expert column, or join a learning community. These methods feel easy, efficient, and packed with substance. Surely persistence alone must lead to achievement.

Unfortunately, this is an illusion. Technology and information have developed enormously in our generation, but the human learning mechanism has not changed at the same pace. The brain operates much as it did centuries ago. Worse, the convenience created by abundant information and varied formats is steadily eroding our capacity for deep learning.

Fast, simple, easy methods amplify our natural tendency to avoid difficulty and demand quick results. They suppress the rational brain's potential, while deep learning depends almost entirely on that higher brain.

I see a small intellectual elite still confronting core difficulties, researching deeply, and producing content, while most consumers continue enjoying shallow learning and consuming what others make. If we truly want a place in the current of our age, we must abandon the fantasy of effortless learning, train depth, swim against the current, and become scarce talent. Otherwise, the road ahead can only narrow.

#### What Is Deep Learning?

In 1946, American scholar Edgar Dale proposed the “Cone of Experience,” later popularized as the Learning Pyramid. A report associated with the National Training Laboratories in Maine divided learning into passive and active levels (see Figure 5-1).

Passive activities—lectures, reading, audiovisual material, and demonstrations—were said to produce average retention rates of 5, 10, 20, and 30 percent.

Active activities—discussion, practice, and teaching others—were said to raise retention to 50, 75, and 90 percent.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_20.jpg)

Figure 5-1 The Learning Pyramid

Whatever the exact numbers, the model clearly contrasts levels of learning depth. We can make a similar distinction in our own study. For reading, Figure 5-2 moves from shallow to deep: listen to a summary; read the book; read and copy striking passages; read and create a mind map or notes; read and put the ideas into practice; read, practice, produce an explanation, and teach others.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_21.jpg)

Figure 5-2 The reading pyramid

Many products now summarize a book in ten or fifteen minutes. At one per day, we could consume more than three hundred books a year. This convenient, novel, concentrated format looks easy and efficient, but occupies the shallowest layer of passive learning.

Reading the original is better. Yet if we never revisit or think after finishing and are satisfied with input alone, retention remains low; within days we cannot remember what we read. Worse, this kind of effort encourages an obsession with speed and volume and creates a feeling of diligence. In reality it is low-level diligence: the more we invest, the more we lose.

Many others read for themselves and produce notes or mind maps. Unfortunately, the notes often merely rearrange and list the book's contents like an outline. People become absorbed in this work and feel that they command the whole book, unaware that they have performed little more than transport. It contains an element of active learning, but simple restatement differs greatly from higher-order transformation.

At a deeper level, a reader applies the book's ideas. Even one or two points that change life are remarkable, because the knowledge has begun to transform.

Moving from knowing to doing is a great advance. Yet knowing or doing something yourself is different from enabling someone else to know or do it. Try explaining something you understand clearly to another person. You may find yourself incoherent. Try writing it, and you may not know where to begin.

Pay attention: that difficulty is where deep learning truly begins. You **must use existing knowledge to explain new knowledge**. When you can explain the new idea clearly, you have integrated it into your own structure, reached the level at which you can teach it, and perhaps created new knowledge.

Luo Zhenyu once described his method: “I require myself to write five reading reflections every day. They need not be essays; a few words are enough. Genuine learning is like sewing on buttons: attaching new knowledge to an existing structure. Five reflections force my existing structure to react to new knowledge. Writing fixes those reactions in place and completes the stitch.”

**Stitching is the key to deep learning. Most people acquire knowledge but omit the step of connecting it, leaving the learning process incomplete.** Some connect at a shallow level but produce no high-quality output, greatly reducing depth.

Shallow learning is satisfied with input; deep learning emphasizes output. Moving from idea to speech to writing transforms networked thought into a branching structure and then a linear text—as if turning thought from gas into liquid and then solid. Only the solid material truly belongs to us. All knowledge inevitably decays, and the loss continues constantly. Unless we fix what we learn into a stable form, time disperses it until almost no trace remains.

Once we have made something our own, we should teach it. Teaching and connecting reinforce one another and form a cycle. Liu Weipeng, author of *Dark Time*, says teaching is the best learning: if you cannot explain something clearly, you probably do not fully understand it. The highest level of teaching is to make an outsider understand in the fewest, clearest words.

**The path of deep learning is therefore to obtain high-quality knowledge, connect it deeply, and teach it to others in your own language or writing.**

#### How to Learn Deeply

Deep learning has three steps:

(1) Obtain high-quality knowledge.

(2) Connect new knowledge deeply.

(3) Produce something and teach it.

This process requires surrendering the security created by learning quickly and learning a lot. It consumes more time, creates greater difficulty, and may feel tormenting. Yet correct action often runs against our nature. What feels easy and comfortable rarely produces a good result; what feels difficult at first is what creates a genuine gain. The following methods can help.

**First, obtain and study primary knowledge whenever possible.** Read classics and original works, even academic papers. Time has already tested the value of classic primary knowledge, making it worthy of close cultivation. Abandon fantasies such as “finish it in minutes,” “one book a day,” or “ten lessons to mastery.” Such formats can inspire, but they remain fragmented and predigested. First-hand study is harder, yet the pleasure of deep understanding far exceeds the comfort of absorbing shallow secondary material. Reading is best not outsourced. In the long run, we must learn to mine for ourselves.

**Second, write what you learn in your own words whenever possible.** After reading a valuable book, reconstruct the author's ideas through writing. Explain and extend them through your own experience, knowledge, and position instead of merely listing key points. Simple restatement cannot create a deep connection. Only transformation lets the old knowledge structure stitch in the new. You may take only the ideas that affect you most and abandon the rest, however reasonable they are. When possible, spend a long time refining a single subject or view. **One carefully polished work that moves another person creates far more influence than daily writing without depth.** Writing also compounds. An article may be read at any time, indirectly creating discussion, exchange, and teaching.

**Third, reflect on life.** Learning is not confined to books; life experience can also be studied deeply. Cheng Jia, author of *Study Well*, places enormous importance on reflection. He spends about two hours each morning reviewing and asks his employees to do the same. His book devotes extensive space to its methods and benefits. He argues that the gap between people comes not from age, or even experience, but from the ability to summarize, reflect on, and elevate experience.

Influenced by this idea, I began writing a daily review in February 2017—sometimes a few sentences, sometimes more than a thousand words. Sustained reflection clarified things I had not understood, sharpened vague concepts, and unexpectedly revealed underlying connections among apparently unrelated events. It steadily increased my sensitivity to life's details and the amount I could extract from experience. The conception and design of this section also came from daily reflection. If I could recommend only one indispensable habit, it would be this one.

#### The Benefits of Deep Learning

**Deep learning not only dispels impetuousness and trains reason; it brings many further benefits, including stronger ability across fields.** In *What Possibilities Does Your Life Hold?*, Gu Dian divides human ability into three levels: knowledge, skills, and talents. Knowledge transfers least. A doctorate in medicine does not teach you to cook mapo tofu. Skills commonly consist of roughly 70 percent general and 30 percent specialized ability and therefore transfer better. At the level of talent, boundaries between occupations disappear.

This explains why some people cross fields easily. Deep learning has developed talents applicable elsewhere, so they need only a little time to acquire the new field's knowledge and skills. Without those talents, a change of industry requires rebuilding foundational knowledge and skills from the beginning—a far more strenuous task.

**Deep learning also produces more inspiration.** Einstein was traveling to work at the patent office when the clock tower in Bern prompted a sudden question: “If this vehicle moved at the speed of light, would the hands of the clock appear motionless from inside?” The question helped bring special relativity—one of the twentieth century's greatest discoveries—into view. The German chemist August Kekulé, deeply fatigued, dreamed of a snake biting its own tail, which became a clue to benzene's molecular structure. We marvel at scientists' intuition and inspiration, but neither could have appeared without deep study. Only after sufficiently deep exploration in a field can the subconscious reveal an insight. We may not be scientists, but depth still increases the likelihood of unexpected discovery.

**Deep learning also lets us perceive connections among different things and generate insight.** I once took my daughter to the film *The Monkey King 3*. After surviving mortal danger with Tang Sanzang, the Queen of Women's Kingdom tells him: “I had a dream. Many years from now, your hair had grown long and we were growing old together—but you were not happy.” I immediately thought: this is a future perspective. The queen looked back at the present from the future, made a rational choice, restrained her feelings, and allowed Tang Sanzang to continue west. A week earlier I had written an article about future perspective, “What Can Rescue Your Ability to Act?” Without that work, the scene would probably have meant nothing to me. My daughter saw only that the queen was beautiful and Sun Wukong was funny.

Accumulate enough understanding in several fields, and even films, celebrity gossip, or news—things that usually scatter attention—can activate higher cognition, connect with useful thought, and generate deeper, more distinctive insight.

Many serious learners still enjoy entertainment. Li Xiaolai, for example, likes films. I would argue that even there their rational brains remain in charge. They connect what they see with their understanding and gain inspiration instead of merely satisfying the primitive needs of instinct and emotion.

Popular entertainment is not valueless, and shallow knowledge has meaning too, but only when a foundation of cognitive depth exists. **Breadth is effective only when it rests on depth.**

#### A Defense of Shallow Learning

After so much praise of depth, how should we treat online knowledge columns, premium courses, book summaries, and similar products? Must we reject them completely? No. Deep and shallow learning do not conflict. Shallow learning has value; the key is not to reverse their relative importance. Use shallow learning as an entrance to new information, but do not entrust all growth to it. The more reasonable position is to **focus on deep learning while remaining open to shallow learning.**

Choose worthwhile people and remain connected with them. Valuable signals they release can lead you into a broader world. But ultimately, you must read, think, and act for yourself.

This book is the same. If it affects you, it has merely opened a new perspective. Whether you acquire the capacity for deep learning depends entirely on your action. No one can do it for you.

### Section 3: Connection—The Master's “Black Box”

If someone asks, “Can I become smarter quickly without extraordinary effort?” the usual answer is, “Stop daydreaming.” Yet I have found a way to become smarter quickly.

Who is Zhang Jigang?

You may not know his name, but you have probably seen his work *Thousand-Hand Guanyin*. At the 2005 CCTV Spring Festival Gala, twenty-one performers with hearing and speech impairments presented this visually stunning dance and astonished the world.

Zhang conceived the work in 1996 while gathering inspiration at the Yungang Grottoes, Mount Wutai, and the Hall of Great Compassion at Chongshan Temple in Shanxi. He refined it for seven years. Less widely known is that Zhang also served as deputy director of the opening ceremony of the 2008 Beijing Olympics and created its “Dreamlike Five Rings.”

The origin of that idea is remarkable. To meet the requirement of showing something never before seen or heard, the creative team debated for a long time without finding a strong solution. The Beijing organizing committee equipped each director with audio equipment, a television, and a magnetic dry-erase whiteboard. During one discussion, Zhang said, “Erase this and I'll present another plan.” But the marks would not come off. His first thought was that the board was defective, so he asked someone to contact the administration. Ten minutes later the messenger returned: “Director, how foolish of us—the protective plastic film on the new board was never removed.” Everyone laughed and began peeling it away. At that moment Zhang suddenly shouted, “Stop!” To the team's astonishment, he announced: “The Olympic rings have been born.”

At the opening ceremony, the world saw twenty-nine giant firework footprints “walk” toward the Bird's Nest. When the last dispersed above the stadium, sparks fell like stars. LEDs on the floor converged into the Olympic rings. Everyone knew the image appeared on an LED surface; no one expected the rings themselves to be “peeled up” from it.

This is a universal method of master creators: connect an inconspicuous A in the distance with a nearby B that needs a solution, releasing astonishing power.

Connection is the expert's secret. Because its operation remains hidden, it forms a black box. Let us open that box, expose the mysterious weapon, and make it available to everyone who wants to become an expert or master.

#### Without Connection, There Is No Learning

Connection is a foundational ability. It appears at high levels and in ordinary life.

Many people learn by reading large numbers of books and articles, collecting new information, making notes and diagrams, saving links, and occasionally sharing them online. They drop new terms and concepts into conversation to display broad knowledge. This learning is not worthless, but its effect is limited because it remains shallow.

In *Reading Is Enough This Way*, Zhao Zhou proposes three stages:

· Restate the information in your own words—that is, identify the point that affects you.

· Describe a relevant personal experience—connect it with other knowledge from life.

· Apply it—turn it into action and create real change.

These are also three levels of deep learning:

· know the information;

· connect the information;

· act and change.

Knowing is the shallowest level. Complete learning also includes connection and action. Yet many stop at the first level, either because it provides psychological satisfaction or because they do not know the other levels exist. They swim through an ocean of knowledge year after year without advancing. The fundamental obstacle is failure to realize that each newly learned point is isolated. However striking or shocking it seems, it will not survive long unless sufficiently connected with existing knowledge.

Earlier I mentioned Luo Zhenyu's method of “sewing on buttons.” Expert learners are not satisfied with acquisition; they focus on stitching new knowledge into place. That stitching is connection. Isolated facts are grains of sand. Connection gathers them into a tower, forms stable crystals of knowledge, and ultimately builds a personal cognitive system (see Figure 5-3).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_22.jpg)

Figure 5-3 Connection is an essential stage of learning

Understanding how the brain learns makes it easy to connect this diagram with neurons. Learning a movement or memorizing a formula is fundamentally the process of forming connections among nerve cells. In neuroscientific terms, repeated activity causes two or more previously unrelated neurons to form a strong association. Without that process, no number of brain cells would make you smarter.

This is why I often encourage writing. Reading alone can leave us satisfied with acquisition. Writing forces us to connect what we have learned, making it a natural path into deep learning.

According to their awareness of connection, people divide almost invisibly into two groups: **the majority view things in isolation and spend great amounts of time collecting and possessing information; a smaller group of pioneers prefer to manipulate relationships among pieces of information and grow smarter almost without noticing.**

#### If It Does Not Concern You, Do Not Connect It

Connection can rapidly improve ability, but this does not mean connecting everything we encounter at all times. That would be neither possible nor necessary. The world contains too much. Connection should focus firmly on our most urgent needs—in other words, make everything relevant to ourselves.

Tyler Cowen, author of *Discover Your Inner Economist*, provides a vivid example. It is said that he finishes only one out of every ten books he begins. A journalist once watched him read.

Cowen arrived at an airport with a large stack of new books. He flipped through them while talking with the journalist. Two hours later, as departure approached, he had nearly finished browsing. He kept one or two and handed the rest to the journalist: “Take them if you're interested. If not, throw them away.”

Ordinary readers feel that failing to finish a book betrays the author or wastes the purchase price. Cowen considered his method an excellent bargain. Only content genuinely relevant to him was useful, and in an age of scarce attention there was no reason to finish every book or every page.

Even the best book cannot be remembered in full. Return to it later and much will feel unread. Yet ideas we connected with ourselves are difficult to forget, and those that changed us remain especially vivid. This confirms the earlier principle: **the value of knowledge lies not in quantity but in whether and how thoroughly it connects with us.** Something useful to another person may have no relevance to us; abandon it decisively. Filtering for personal relevance greatly increases the power of connection.

**One more important hidden condition must not be overlooked: you need a clear goal or powerful need.** Zhang Jigang could connect Buddhist imagery with a dance and plastic film with the Olympic rings because he was an artist driven by a strong need to create. Once you hold a hammer, more things begin to resemble nails and become usable. Someone without direction can stand amid every possible scene and body of knowledge yet see no productive connection. However hard they work, their effort will come to nothing.

#### How to Develop the Ability to Connect

Mastery is not magical. Once the black box opens, we too have the opportunity to become masters. Connection accelerates development and creates leverage. Several paths follow naturally from the discussion above.

**First, hold a hammer.** Without sufficient love and investment in something, without intense focus and thought, almost nothing will carry meaning. I watched Zhang Jigang's interview ten years ago. Had I not since acquired writing as a “hammer,” I might never have remembered the story.

**Second, acquire abundant input.** Through reading or life experience, broader knowledge and richer experience raise the probability of a successful connection. An empty mind and colorless life can hardly create brilliant associations. The finest cook cannot prepare a meal without rice, while even an awkward cook can invent something in a room full of ingredients. Travel more, observe more, read more, reflect more. No road in life is walked in vain; every step counts.

**Third, remain curious.** James Watt wondered why steam lifted a kettle lid; Newton wondered why an apple fell. The world's greatest ideas lie hidden in ordinary things, and the least conspicuous may produce the deepest insight through connection. This is difficult for adults. Unless we cleanse the mind and preserve a child's purity, everything feels familiar and escapes notice. Growth is not merely cognitive improvement but cultivation of the self.

**Finally, repeat one question.** Some phrases become unforgettable after one hearing. Li Xiaolai's has always remained with me: “Where else can this principle be used?” Many experts learn this way. When Li Shanyou, founder of Chaos University, encounters a useful idea in a book, he stops and looks for connections—for other phenomena the theory can explain. He refuses to stop until he finds five.

Such learners consciously or unconsciously follow one rule: whenever an insight, view, or fact affects them, they maximize its usefulness by connecting it elsewhere and allowing it to change their action. Make the question your own refrain: where else can this principle be used?

#### Everything Depends on Initiative

Watt connected the kettle lid, Newton the apple, Einstein the clock, and Kekulé the snake. Great methods have always existed, but very few people use them. Once the black box is open, we must not continue overlooking it and trust to luck for an accidental entrance.

We learn the word “connection” when young and hear it countless times, but who imagined it was a ladder to advancement? Beginning today, know it again. Use it deliberately and pass it on. Let it remain neither hidden nor vague. Some who use it proactively will surely become exceptional.

Looking toward the future, I seem to see a great wave of thoughtful people rising continually along the path to higher knowledge and skill.

### Section 4: Systems—Building a Personal Cognitive System Is Actually Simple

How can we tell whether someone is truly capable?

If a person's system of knowledge can solve the varied problems they encounter, that person must be formidable.

Charlie Munger, one of the greatest modern investment thinkers, reportedly drew on hundreds of mental models to address investment judgments. Ray Dalio, founder of Bridgewater Associates, developed more than five hundred principles for life and work, eventually publishing a book of more than five hundred pages. Many prominent executives, political leaders, authors, and exceptional students belong in the same category. Their knowledge systems are decisive advantages. If we borrow those systems, can we not improve rapidly too?

It is an attractive idea.

Consequently, learning the systems of experts has become fashionable. Some people speak obsessively in concepts and models as though knowing the labels gave them an entire system. More diligent learners read, draw mind maps, and delight in arranging a “perfect” framework. Others collect systems from many authorities, recite them fluently, and even integrate them, feeling that they have seen through everything.

The fantasy is beautiful; reality is different. After all this effort, their problems remain and improvement is slight. Beyond knowing more, nothing changes. They finally conclude that building a knowledge system is extremely difficult and that their present accumulation is insufficient; perhaps they will try again after learning more.

In fact, building a personal knowledge system is astonishingly simple. Once I explain it, you will know I am not boasting. Rigorous science supports the approach, and my own practice demonstrates it. Let us begin a different journey toward a personal system.

#### The Difference Between Knowledge and Cognition

Look carefully at the section title. I did not write “knowledge system,” but “cognitive system.” Knowledge and cognition are different to me. A passage from Wan Weigang expresses the distinction.

A high examination score does not mean you possess knowledge. Neither does eloquent conversation over tea. Knowledge may be useful in such settings, but little concrete gain or loss is at stake, so the exercise is largely intellectual play. Genuine knowledge appears when the situation is unclear, no one tells you what to do, and a mistaken judgment carries adverse consequences—yet knowledge gives you the courage to decide. This does not mean that only practical knowledge counts. It means knowledge becomes yours only when it helps you make a real decision.

This explanation moved me because it breaks the conventional idea of knowledge. Many people, myself once included, regard knowledge as the concepts, formulas, principles, cases, and arguments found in books, organized into systems. We believe at least two things: that academic systems are fixed, universal, and available for everyone to learn; and that top students or famous teachers must command a complete framework and panorama, which explains their ease, so copying their systems provides a shortcut. During formal education, these assumptions are difficult to challenge. They become habits of thought that we carry without question into personal development: find the most authoritative and certain system, then learn and copy it.

This confuses knowledge with cognition. Many people search for an optimal system and try to absorb it whole, ignoring the difference between another person's system and their own needs. **The purpose of personal growth is no longer knowing and understanding, but judging and choosing.** As Wan Weigang says, real knowledge is not something you know but something you can use to make sound choices and solve actual problems. This is the crucial distinction between an academic knowledge system and a personal one.

**In personal growth, therefore, there is no optimal, certain, authoritative cognitive system—only the system best suited to our current state.** Knowledge does not necessarily produce cognitive ability, but cognitive ability necessarily contains effective knowledge: knowledge that helps us judge, choose, act, change, and solve real problems. To avoid confusion, I will use “cognitive system” in this sense below.

#### Learn Only What Moves You

Beginners urgently want a system because they possess only fragments and struggle to integrate them for complex situations. Without awareness, they confuse learning facts with developing cognition and approach another person's system as they would an academic curriculum. They become obsessed with comprehensive mastery and wholesale copying, fearing that anything less will waste the entire effort.

Many share this anxiety and one common trait: **they care intensely about formal completeness.** Readers discussing methods with me often say that after every book they systematically reconstruct the author's framework, write notes, extract highlights, and draw a mind map—as though only this could prove that they had read seriously. Then they ask: “Which mind-mapping tool do you use?”

I never know how to answer. I do not draw mind maps or deliberately reconstruct an author's framework. While reading, I do only one thing: **search for points that move me.** I mark them and fill the margins with related thoughts. After finishing, I put the book aside for several days, then ask: “Which point affected me most?” It may be a theory, a case, or a single sentence. If it truly moves me and produces real change, the book has delivered extraordinary value. I do not regret forgetting the rest. And what has the author's complete system to do with me?

People are surprised by this method. It seems unreliable and wasteful: with a complete system available, why collect only one or two fragments? Am I misleading them? Be patient; the method has a scientific basis.

The circle-of-competence principle tells us that ability cannot develop by leaps. It expands little by little from its existing boundary, and the optimal region is the edge of the comfort zone.

Cognition is an ability and follows the same law. Knowledge at the edge of our cognitive circle matches our actual needs most closely, so it is more likely to affect us and connect with what we already know. Much within another person's system, however sound, may lie too far from our present cognition or need and therefore belong to the difficulty zone (see Figure 5-4).

Unless another person's system happens to match our own cognitive circle, trying to absorb it whole produces limited learning and wastes time (see Figure 5-5).

How much do you remember from the books, notes, and mind maps you have made? Most has probably blurred. Ask which ideas you truly use in everyday life, and the answer is likely smaller still.

We do not need complete command of another person's system. We need the knowledge that affects us most and lies closest to our needs (see Figure 5-6).

Such fragments bind closely with us, gradually become part of us, and eventually weave a personal cognitive network.

**A system is essentially an integration of scattered, independent knowledge, concepts, and views into methods for engaging with the world through a distinctive perspective.** Look closely and every person's cognitive system differs. Experts continually collect information in their field that affects their particular concerns, process and integrate it, and form a unique system. We should attempt the same.

This is the truth of building a personal cognitive system: **break apart the systems of many thinkers, take only the points or pieces that move you most, and join them into your own cognitive network.**

This explains why good books and elite courses do not produce the dramatic change we imagine. Their tables of contents and syllabi radiate the fragrance of a system, but may fit our present cognition and needs poorly, leaving us unable to use them. Once we understand this, systems no longer confuse us, and forgetting much of a finished book no longer causes guilt.

As our own system improves, knowledge that once lay far away moves relatively closer and begins to affect us. Temporarily setting knowledge aside is harmless; continued learning means we lose nothing. We can now break free of form and learn from everything. Reading, courses, self-reflection, and conversation all become grounded forms of growth if they touch us and solve actual problems. This makes growth both efficient and practical and can even eliminate learning anxiety.

**When something moves you, an opportunity to learn has arrived.**

#### Emotional Impact Is the Best Filter

The intuitive power of the subconscious can filter learning by detecting, through emotion, what connects most closely with our needs. Grasp that material and you remain at the edge of the comfort zone, learning efficiently and improving quickly.

I have not seen this method named elsewhere, so I call it **“impact-based learning.”** It has brought me enormous benefits in reading, reflection, and building a personal cognitive system.

I recommend daily reflection to everyone who wants to grow. It strengthens sensitivity to the details of life so that days do not rush past like water and leave nothing behind. Unlike an ordinary diary, daily reflection is not a chronological record. Notice the single event that affected you most that day, whether a positive insight or a painful experience, and review it in as much detail as possible. If one point of impact becomes a crystal of cognition, the quality and density of our lives will far exceed those of people who never reflect. Faced with life's flood of information, emotional impact is the best filter. It lets us withstand the current and walk through the world with composure and dignity.

#### Being Moved Is Not Enough

Not every emotional response is effective. You have probably read excellent articles that moved you deeply and saved them, only to forget them completely later. If asked to underline every moving passage in a book, you might cover the pages in lines, yet plainly cannot use them all. Being moved but unable to use the insight is like keeping bandages in a cabinet and forgetting them when you cut a finger. How is that different from never having been moved?

It is not. Such responses are false impact. Figure 5-7 explains why.

When new knowledge approaches the edge of our cognitive circle, it affects us. But impact does not mean the connection is strong. Without timely reinforcement, the new point may remain briefly and then “fly away.” To retain it as part of our system, we must create relationships and connections. More is better, but they appear mainly in three forms (see Figure 5-8).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_27.jpg)

Figure 5-8 Three ways to connect new knowledge effectively

**First, explain the new knowledge again in your own language. This forces your existing system to respond to it.** If you can make a concept, principle, or event clear in your own words—even to an outsider—it will very likely become part of you. People who regularly produce explanations grow quickly because they continually connect new and old knowledge.

**Second, retrieve the knowledge when it is needed. Knowledge you cannot retrieve is false impact.** I often listen to Luo Zhenyu's sixty-second audio messages, but only a few of their ideas surface when I write. Most are forgotten. Looking back, I sometimes think: this makes perfect sense—how can I have no memory of it? Knowledge that moved us then but remains entirely unavailable when needed was false impact. It was far from our real needs and can be abandoned.

If an idea surfaces while reading, writing, or speaking—even as only a faint clue whose details you cannot remember—treat it as extremely important. Knowledge retrievable at the moment of need genuinely affected you; the precious connection still exists. Reinforce it deliberately.

Readers often care only whether they understood the text and ignore ideas arising in their own minds. Those ideas are extraordinarily valuable. Letting them pass greatly reduces the effect of learning.

**Third, practice or use the knowledge regularly, because practice is the ultimate method for forming a strong connection.** Learning is not for knowing but for creating real change. Apply the knowledge and its related details begin appearing everywhere in your field of view. Then you become not only strong in cognition but powerful in action.

Ultimately you will understand that reading, writing, reflection, habit formation, skill practice, and building a cognitive system are all fundamentally the same activity: **expanding outward little by little at the edge of the comfort zone.**

Once this is clear, everything becomes simple.

### Section 5: Check-Ins—Do Not Become Obsessed; Checking In Cannot Build Your Future

Learning and personal growth are now widely fashionable.

At home, at school, and at work, people seize every moment to improve. To achieve our goals, we have created a culture of daily check-ins: rising early, fitness, running, reading, foreign languages. Whatever the field, there is a check-in group for it.

The method appears sound. Divide a large goal into small daily ones, advance a little at a time, make effort visible, and strengthen confidence. Spread the large goal across small tasks that appear painless and easy, and success seems only a matter of time.

Yet something feels wrong. A reader named A Jian described the problem: “To study, I created five daily check-in plans and kept them all going. But the moment one sequence is interrupted, I want to throw it aside and never continue.”

Is he too greedy? A perfectionist? Neither. A hidden psychological mechanism is operating. Look at people posting daily check-ins to their social feeds. They participate enthusiastically, yet very few ultimately master what they study. For most, the check-in is a passionate festival. Before long, they join another campaign or quietly stop.

This claim may irritate people currently checking in, but continue reading. There is a reasonable explanation and a better method. I do not oppose check-ins themselves; we simply must distinguish several situations.

#### Motive Displacement and Distorted Drive

WeChat Sports displays daily step counts on a ranking board. Before ranking, nothing much happens. Once people can compare positions, behavior changes. Whether or not they previously enjoyed exercise, they pour into streets, parks, and waterfronts and walk vigorously through wind and rain. At first this looks positive: daily rankings inspire exercise, improve fitness, and add a social element. The problem lies beneath.

From the moment ranking begins, the motive quietly shifts. What began as a pursuit of health and enjoyment becomes an attempt to make one's number look better on the board. Some even buy devices or use software to generate fake steps.

Daily check-ins work the same way. At the beginning, motivation comes entirely from learning and growth: imagining rising early with ease and enjoying the morning; exercising into an attractive body; loving books and becoming wise. Confidence and drive surge. Under that motivation, the check-in merely adds decoration, and action needs little willpower. Once checking in begins, however, a task mentality drops anchor.

As time passes, enthusiasm and motivation decline while learning becomes harder. More willpower is required. What happens when willpower fails? Quit openly? That would tell everyone you cannot do it. How embarrassing.

To avoid pain, the brain activates self-protection and revises its interpretation: “Learning is hard, but checking in is easy. If I complete the check-in, doesn't that mean the task is done?” “If checking in counts as completion, why choose the hard option rather than the easy one?”

This is the brain's explanatory system. The logic is absurd, but our powerful nature can force reason to defend it—and some people accept the defense. They buy step-generating gadgets and top the rankings without leaving home. They tap “check in” in an early-rising group when the alarm rings at five, then fall back asleep. They open a book, photograph it, and post the image to prove that they read that day.

These are extreme minority examples, but when willpower weakens, most people unconsciously lower the standard to complete the check-in. Quantity and quality cease to matter; completing the visible task becomes everything. Motivation moves silently from learning itself to task completion, from internal need to external form.

A Jian had not noticed this shift. He wondered why an interrupted check-in destroyed his desire to continue, because preserving the completeness of the record had replaced preserving the learning process. For learning itself, what harm does an occasional interruption do?

In more serious cases, not only the motive but the goal changes. People initially remember why they act—learning English to converse fluently with foreigners, for example. Over time, the goal shrinks into memorizing twenty words a day. They mechanically complete the count and tick the box, forgetting the original purpose and learning only for the sake of learning.

#### Cognitive Closure and Reduced Effectiveness

Depending on check-ins not only displaces motivation but reduces effectiveness through another psychological mechanism: the need for cognitive closure.

When people face an ambiguous question, they desire a definite answer. Ancient people did not know why rain fell, so the open question created discomfort. They explained it with gods of thunder and lightning or the Dragon King. The explanations lacked evidence but satisfied the need for closure. Applied to behavior, the principle is the same: **as long as a task remains unfinished, it occupies the mind and creates a desire to end it; once completed, motivation immediately approaches zero.**

Before finishing an assignment from your manager, you keep thinking about its scattered details. The instant the manager says it is acceptable, the matter ends. Once closed, the brain clears the occupied memory space; the task quickly leaves the mind, and the motive to act disappears.

The brain behaves this way because it likes certainty and dislikes the unknown. A check-in carries a built-in task mentality. Every check-in creates a need for closure. This is harmless at first, but once motivation shifts, the psychology changes.

Suppose the daily task is twenty words. If time is short, you may skim them and tell yourself you studied, closing the task so it stops bothering you. If time is abundant and you finish twenty early, the check-in closes the task and learning motivation falls to zero. You do not explore further.

**This is the check-in mentality: if you cannot learn it, pretend; if you do learn it, stop immediately.** A task-driven learner rarely displays strong initiative because attention rests on completion rather than the activity itself.

#### A Task Mentality Divides Body and Mind

A task mentality is useful in some fields, including the military, where soldiers require a powerful sense of mission. In personal learning and growth, however, it may be harmful.

While running, you think about how much time remains. While reading, how many pages are left. While memorizing words, how many more before you can stop. Attention divides and full immersion becomes difficult, preventing you from experiencing technique and enjoyment. You miss the delight of body, hands, and feet moving in a run; fail to enter the thoughts and emotions of characters; and overlook deeper relationships among words. A voice behind you continually urges: faster, faster, finish it now.

Modern people struggle to feel happy largely because of this speed and impatience. Life becomes tedious. We cannot enjoy the process; body and mind grow tense, anxious, numb, and divided.

In *21 Lessons for the 21st Century*, Yuval Noah Harari reflects deeply on human existence. Discussing life's meaning, he writes in effect that bodily sensation stands between him and the world.

The essential meaning of individual life is the unity of body and mind and awareness of the actual process of living. This idea contains Zen, philosophy, and science. In learning, complete focus and unity are indispensable. Only then can fine, powerful positive feedback emerge from the activity. A task mentality destroys that unity. The resulting discomfort deepens aversion to learning and creates a vicious cycle.

You may now want to reduce task mentality. Yet some things cannot be obtained by direct pursuit. Tell yourself to fall asleep and sleep recedes; relax the body and it may arrive naturally. Tell yourself to focus and distraction grows; think and feel wholly about the thing itself and concentration arises. Try hard to display beauty and you appear contrived; devote yourself quietly to an activity and genuine beauty appears. Great beauty is unaware of itself. The way to dissolve task mentality is similar: give your whole mind to what is before you.

#### Two Strategies for Easy Change

I do not mean to condemn all check-ins. They can be excellent tools, propelling sustained action and building momentum, which explains their popularity. But complete dependence easily becomes a cognitive trap.

Most people in check-in campaigns lack awareness. Once the initial boost ends, they fail to adjust motivation and become trapped without seeing the harm. Some do combine check-ins successfully with learning, for one simple reason: their motive never changes. The check-in remains a by-product.

How do they do it?

One small change can transform the practice: **replace checking in with recording.**

After studying, record the action without displaying it as a completed check-in. The record shows your learning trajectory and supports a weekly review (see Figure 5-9).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_28.jpg)

Figure 5-9 Replace checking in with recording

It looks similar, but recording does not create task pressure. Attention remains on the activity instead of completion.

Do not worry that the absence of external restriction will make you lazy. Everyone wants to improve; who does not wish to perform better than last time? Focus on learning and growth themselves, experience their pleasure, and strong motivation can turn passive learning active. The difference between checking in and recording sounds verbal, but it is psychologically subtle and deserves careful attention.

Use a second strategy when designing the task: **set a floor, not a ceiling.**

Instead of a daily maximum-like requirement of twenty words—which may be difficult enough that completion brings only relief—set an easy floor of five. You finish without burden and, having just entered the learning state with energy available, are likely to continue. Every additional word feels like an extra gain. The mentality changes completely. Body and mind immerse more easily because there is no concern about when the task will end.

This strategy avoids the need for closure. As long as the activity remains interesting, you can continue until it begins to feel demanding. Without a fixed ceiling, it creates much greater and more sustainable initiative than the check-in model.

It also fits deliberate practice by keeping you near the comfort zone's edge. Each session can reach a point that is slightly difficult but not overwhelming. A fixed check-in value easily becomes either boring or intimidating and prompts abandonment.

The strategy is not my invention. It comes from Stephen Guise's *Mini Habits*. To form good habits, Guise required himself to do one push-up, read one page, and write fifty words per day. This burden-free method eventually gave him a fit body, a reading habit, and a book of his own. He calls it too small to fail.

I have tested it. It works. You can try it too.

For learning, preserving internal motivation matters most. This road is actually harder than checking in, but difficult work yields something because it lies closer to the requirements of success. Preserving motivation alone is still not enough; genuine success also requires learning to create motivation.

### Section 6: Feedback—It Is Time to Explain What Learning Really Is

When Liu Chuan, author of *Cognitive Upgrade*, was a child, he had an extraordinary experience learning the electronic keyboard. Starting from zero, he passed the Grade 10 examination in two years; most children his age needed four or five. More remarkably, he learned no music theory before reaching that level, and his teacher never asked him to study it. What did the teacher do?

“The teacher demonstrated the left hand, then the right, then played them together so I roughly knew what the piece should sound like. I spent the next week trying to reproduce it, and the teacher tested me on the weekend. If I failed, I continued; if I passed, I moved to the next piece. The cycle continued for two years until the pieces I could play reached Grade 10. I encountered no music theory at all.”

Liu remains grateful to his first music teacher. Not knowing theory did not stop him performing complete pieces fluently for others and receiving immediate praise. That feedback washed over the beach of his confidence like repeated waves and kept him absorbed in the pleasure of playing.

Compare children today, many trapped for long periods in tedious foundational practice because they must learn systematic theory: notation, fingering, pitch, rhythm. Parents make them repeat exercises, usually not to perform immediately but to pass graded examinations. Without external positive feedback for so long, children gradually experience learning as an activity they resist but must complete. Parents invest large sums and children enormous time, only to travel “from introduction to abandonment.”

The contrast reveals the key difference: **timely, sustained positive feedback.**

Liu's progress is not really mysterious. Every one of us learned to speak and walk in the same way. No one begins speech with phonetic rules and letter sounds, or walking with mechanics and muscle control. We imitate and practice, speak and walk directly, continually receive environmental feedback, experience pleasure, and correct mistakes. Without knowing the principles, we learn both skills remarkably well.

Life gave us a powerful method of learning, and we gradually forgot it. With civilization and reason, learning shifted toward systematic instruction based on principles and foundations. The approach appears efficient but often emphasizes input and practice while ignoring output and feedback, making learning painful and dull. The world has suffered from arduous study long enough. It is time to return to the source.

#### Without Feedback, There Is No Learning

Most people lack awareness of output and feedback. They may be highly rational and drive themselves with extraordinary perseverance, yet still receive pain and failure.

A second-year university student using the name Wuhui once told me that he studied from morning to night, six days a week, resting one. His reward was exhaustion and helplessness. Another reader, Ms. Fu Qin, said that to survive a crisis she continually studied yoga, writing, belly dancing, spoken English, painting, tea ceremony, and other skills, yet never felt fulfilled or valuable.

Learners in similar situations, whether students or workers, believe that diligent input and ceaseless study must eventually produce mastery. Reality keeps disappointing them. They rarely consider producing something quickly in exchange for feedback and using that feedback as another source of motivation. After too long in an artificial educational system, some struggle to believe that skipping theory and practicing directly can work. They dismiss it as a clever trick and insist that strong willpower and understanding are the proper route.

Neuroscientists offer another view: sustained positive feedback truly activates the powerful capacity for action in the instinctive and emotional brains. These systems may not think; they may be short-sighted and foolish, repeatedly absorbed in games, phones, food, and sleep. **But their immense desire and emotional force are precious sources of action. If they experience pleasure in learning, they can drive us to become as absorbed in it as in entertainment.**

The rational brain is intelligent and farsighted but too weak to do the heavy work itself. Its real role is to design strategy so that instinct and emotion continually receive strong positive feedback and race happily toward the goal (see Figure 5-10).

**A scientific learning strategy therefore produces work and obtains feedback, driving instinct and emotion to “play, play, play,” rather than forcing reason to “study, study, study” through endless persistence.** The idea feels counterintuitive, but it forms an invisible dividing line between effective and ineffective learners.

Figure 5-10 The instinctive and emotional brains are the engines of learning

#### A Future Requires a Mindset of Making Things

Once we understand this, behavior changes quickly. We develop a clear, strong mindset of producing work, value output and application, refine what we make, and actively seek external feedback.

Previously, you might have learned English through a daily check-in. Now you might use it directly: translate a fine passage, consult English documentation, read an original English book, or set your phone's language to English. This creates difficulty, but solving real problems forces you to acquire missing knowledge. Learning receives immediate feedback: either you solve a problem for yourself or another person, or you create something valuable. The result is a strong sense of achievement and desire to continue.

This completes the earlier discussion of check-ins. To create new motivation, abandon pure input and find ways to use the skill or produce work directly in exchange for feedback.

Whenever readers bring me such problems, I advise them to produce something: speak, write, share a video. Do not study endlessly without output. Learning without feedback is painful and usually fails.

The familiar sayings of accomplished people should now make more sense:

· Teaching is the best learning.

· Using is the best learning.

· Output forces better input.

· Let your work speak.

Pioneers share this quality. They ignore convention while learning. Whether knowledge or a skill is new, they use it and make something immediately. At first they perform badly, but they still tinker until an artifact exists, release it for feedback, then refine and iterate.

This is the fastest effective way to learn. Two years of writing have made the truth vivid to me. I keep a folder on my computer containing screenshots of readers' comments—praise, affirmation, encouragement, and appreciation. This is not vanity. I understand how powerfully the feedback affects my capacity for action. Whenever I look at the comments, motivation surges and I can sit at the computer for hours without fatigue. I know that I am driving the emotional brain to work for me. Had my writing received no response, I cannot promise that willpower and long-term understanding alone would have carried me here. “Identify value—refine the work—exchange it for feedback” is the actual strategy and motivation behind my continued writing.

In *Leap*, Gu Dian describes an expert's breakthrough strategy: find your high-value zone, become a leading figure within a small domain, use the systemic force of that leading position to move toward the top of a larger domain, and achieve a leap. The best way to occupy such a position is to keep refining valuable work and use it to obtain feedback.

Without work and the mindset to create it, nothing begins.

#### Pain Is Feedback Too

Producing work and receiving feedback sounds wonderful, but a concern naturally appears: what if sharing brings criticism or ridicule? Would damage to confidence not make things worse? Many young people fear this. Three principles make the first step easier.

**First, sharing does not mean casually releasing unfinished work. Refine it with all your strength into the best thing your present ability can produce.** Careless words or photographs carry little value and naturally attract neither interest nor praise. Treat your work like your child: before it goes out, dress it as beautifully and carefully as possible so that it catches the eye. The standard forces rapid growth at the edge of your ability's comfort zone and follows deliberate practice.

**Second, design a sharing strategy and show the work to people less capable in that area than you.** If you refined it seriously, someone will find you impressive and praise will come. People genuinely stronger than you are usually too busy to mock you.

**Finally, treat attacks calmly and objectively.** You may still receive them; I occasionally get aggressive comments. If nothing lies behind the emotional attack, laugh and ignore it. The attacker is probably jealous and cannot answer with better work or a better argument, so they vent and abuse. Anyone can pick flaws. If the criticism contains rigorous counterevidence and accurately identifies a problem, take it seriously. Such criticism is excellent feedback. It clarifies your thinking and improves the work. To someone who genuinely wants to grow, this is not an attack but a rare form of wealth.

**All pain is a signal from life pointing toward growth.** Physical discomfort, low emotion, poor grades, and career setbacks all indicate that something is wrong—and therefore reveal where effort should go. Many people merely sink into frustrated emotion and remain anxious day and night, delaying themselves and burdening others. With a sufficiently open mind, we can become grateful for pain and setbacks; no feedback is more direct.

#### How Passive Learning Can Obtain Feedback

Students naturally ask whether this applies to them. They assume workers have ample freedom for active learning while students must learn assigned material under heavy workloads and limited time. Can feedback help in that setting? Absolutely. It is equally decisive in passive learning.

Many students try to improve by repeating material endlessly. Results rise little, while study feels mechanical and motivation disappears. Input alone is low-level diligence. Effective students actively create feedback through self-testing.

They do not reread vocabulary until every word looks familiar. They close the book and test whether they can state the meaning and pronunciation exactly and spell the word. They do not expect repeated audio to improve listening effortlessly through background exposure. They consult the transcript and repeatedly replay what they failed to understand.

For memorization and conceptual learning, self-testing is the best feedback. It instantly reveals what you know and do not know, allowing you to eliminate blind spots precisely and remain at the edge of the learning comfort zone. The effort in which everything looks familiar with the book open and disappears when it closes is the source of passivity and poor performance. Barbara Oakley also emphasizes in *A Mind for Numbers* that active recall is among the best learning methods and far superior to passive rereading.

Top students' error notebooks are another ideal form of feedback. Tests expose blind spots, which the students collect and attack, remaining continually in the stretch zone and improving fastest. They are not top students by birth. Feedback clarifies their blind spots and puts them one small step ahead. Each lead produces more praise and affirmation; classmates regard them as outstanding and they begin to see themselves as “gifted.” Small positive feedback grows into large positive feedback, and learning enters a virtuous cycle.

Many students dismiss error notebooks, never create them, never revisit them, or flee the pain they encounter there and retreat into the comfort zone. Before the emotional brain learns to enjoy studying, we sometimes must push ourselves a little.

#### May You Never Again Be Ordinary

I enjoy the television drama *Soldiers Sortie*, especially one episode.

Xu Sanduo is assigned to a remote fifth squad to guard fuel. Under normal circumstances, his military career would have ended uneventfully before he returned home. But the simple-minded Xu decides to build a road across the open ground before the barracks. The choice has no ambition behind it, yet it inadvertently creates a “work.” In the empty wasteland, the road is so conspicuous that pilots notice it. Word reaches senior commanders; Xu receives attention and begins his career in the special forces.

The story confirms Gu Dian's leap theory: refine a work → reach the top of a small hill → attract greater attention → move toward the top of a larger hill → use the system's momentum to achieve a leap in life.

Xu Sanduo produced feedback accidentally because of his nature. We can apply the principle intentionally. Stop laboring alone in obscurity. Without producing, applying, and receiving feedback, a lifetime of study will bring neither genuine growth nor opportunity. **Real learning and growth are not “effort, effort, and more effort,” but “feedback, feedback, and more feedback.”** Only continual output and feedback create real change.

When Xue Lin sees a helicopter circling above the camp in the drama, he says in confusion, “Why do I feel as though this place has become important?”

May you too become more important—and never again live an ordinary life.

**A Special Note**

Liu Chuan's method of skipping principles and moving directly into practice applies only to the beginner stage. It can take you quickly to a passing level, but intermediate and advanced work still requires systematic theory. Without it, progress cannot continue far.

As Liu admits: “At an advanced level, the weaknesses of experiential learning became clear. My composition, for example, depended entirely on inspiration. Without a method, I could only wait. My brain was also poor at transferring the same chord progression between keys. Change the key of an identical progression and I no longer knew how to play it.”

The law of feedback, however, applies throughout. Whenever output produces feedback, it naturally drives further research and exploration.

### Section 7: Rest—Perhaps You Have Not Succeeded Because You Work Too Hard

A student preparing for the postgraduate entrance examination sent a distress signal.

“Since I began preparing, I have shown unprecedented determination and perseverance. I think about studying all day and even listen to lessons while eating. During the first few months, I woke at five or six every morning. But perhaps because the campaign is so long, fatigue has set in and my self-control is now worse than before. Sometimes I feel tired and decide to relax reasonably, but after glancing at a novel I lose control completely. I abandon everything and cannot think about anything else. When I finally recover, three or four days have passed. I blame myself for wasting the time and become anxious. What confuses me most is that other students can play two games when tired and return immediately to their books. When I relax, it feels as if I fall into hell.”

The scene is familiar. At school and at work, we have all encouraged ourselves and resolved to rise to the top. We assume that extraordinary toil must make us the pride of teachers or managers and an example to classmates or colleagues. We adopt the old spirit of tying our hair to a beam or pricking ourselves with an awl: no chatting, no entertainment, not a moment wasted. However exhausted we become, we force ourselves through a little more.

Too often, a heroic beginning produces no heroic ending. After a full experience of painful self-punishment, we see little improvement and lose confidence as well. If such effort is still insufficient, perhaps we simply were not born to learn.

Observe school or work and you will always find some people who are diligent, industrious, and constantly busy yet perform only ordinarily, while others balance work and entertainment, excel, and handle everything with ease. Many factors are involved—prior knowledge, learning habits, internal drive—but the degree of sheer exertion deserves examination too. Something unexpected may be hidden there.

#### The Secret of Deliberate Rest

Recall the four elements of deliberate practice: **a clearly defined goal, extreme focus, effective feedback, and practice in the stretch zone.**

The key to effective learning is extreme focus, not a contest of endurance. Focus consumes energy, and our energy is finite, like water in a bucket. Some buckets are larger than others, but difficult work lowers the level in every one.

Here lies the problem. People who work continuously, fight for every minute, and refuse to rest inevitably experience a steadily declining energy curve (see Figure 5-11).

They begin in excellent condition and work efficiently. Once energy falls below a certain level—say 70 percent—attention starts scattering and thought slows. Continue consuming energy and efficiency declines further; distraction becomes likely.

To the person devoted to hardship, however, these signals seem to call for willpower. Others have said learning and growth require suffering, so fatigue must not lead to rest; willpower should force persistence. Is this not what effort means? They refuse to waste a minute and regard painful progress as proof: the greater the pain and persistence, the more diligent and moving the effort. Their strategy can never be rest. They force themselves harder and struggle to remain awake even while drowsy.

Figure 5-11 The energy curve of relentless effort

Such people appear exceptionally diligent while results deteriorate. The process brings pain rather than pleasure and severely drains energy. Once they relax, they have no desire to return and are more likely to sink into comfortable entertainment.

Relaxed high performers do the opposite. **They never deplete themselves excessively. As soon as energy falls, they stop and rest deliberately, rapidly raising the level in the bucket.** Their energy curve rises and falls like a wave, remaining high through repeated cycles (see Figure 5-12).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_31.jpg)

Figure 5-12 The energy curve of a relaxed learner

If energy above 70 percent constitutes the zone of efficient learning, relaxed learners spend far more time within it than relentless ones (see Figure 5-13).

Figure 5-13 Comparing the zones of efficient learning

These curves demonstrate the meaning and advantage of deliberate rest. Accumulated day after day, the advantage makes it inevitable that some people move ahead—and do so with ease. For anyone who worships toil, this is a startling reversal.

#### The Crisis of Sustained Focus

Efficient learning depends on extreme focus rather than painful support from willpower. Whoever can remain focused longer gains an advantage. Yet focus has become increasingly scarce in the information age.

In August 2018, I published an online survey about difficulties in learning and growth. It received 338 responses before closing. Although the sample was limited, several trends were obvious.

The two most common problems were “frequently becoming immersed in worry, fantasy, and anxiety” with 168 votes, and “constant distraction and inability to maintain focus” with 167. Both are forms of distraction. Many people are clearly troubled by mind-wandering, and extreme focus has become rare.

Li Dazhao taught long ago: study in earnest and play to your heart's content. In his era, both the volume of study and the amount of information were smaller, making clear boundaries easier. Today, facing immense information interference and competitive pressure, most people instinctively tell themselves to work harder. Few recognize the more scientific pattern: **extreme focus + deliberate rest, repeated.**

Focus cannot be maintained by willpower alone. Deliberate rest replenishes the energy it requires. If your mind begins wandering, put down the pen. If sleep will not come, leave the bed. Nothing is worse than forcing yourself onward while attention has already collapsed. Instead, use extreme focus plus deliberate rest: concentrate as fully as possible for a short period, then stop voluntarily when fatigue begins. This is a wiser strategy for both life and learning.

It also matches the principle of stretch-zone practice. Feeling slightly tired means reaching the edge of the energy comfort zone. Rest then, replenish promptly, and energy is used with maximum efficiency. People who force themselves through willpower without genuine focus have entered the energy difficulty zone; they experience pain rather than pleasure (see Figure 5-14).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_33.jpg)

Figure 5-14 Focus is most efficient in the stretch zone

Difficulty also affects focus. Material that is too easy creates boredom and wandering; material that is too difficult creates fear and avoidance. Work that can be reached with a small jump makes focus easiest (see Figure 5-15).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_34.jpg)

Figure 5-15 Keep learning difficulty within the stretch zone

Someone may object that real life rarely offers work at the perfect difficulty. We cannot pick and choose what teachers or managers assign. But the adjustment is not hard. Redefine the learning content to a suitable level or break the goal into smaller, concrete objectives that reduce difficulty. Whatever the task, find a way to bring it into the stretch zone. Even the thorniest assignment contains room for deliberate adjustment if we are willing to think.

#### The Root of Lost Willpower

Why can some students return immediately to study after entertainment, while others relax once and see their willpower collapse? Again, the answer is depleted energy.

Energy can, in one sense, stand in for willpower. When energetic, we face difficulty without fear and resist temptation more strongly. When depleted, we struggle with difficulty and lose resistance to temptation, because **both overcoming difficulty and resisting temptation consume willpower.**

After a full day, we often arrive home exhausted and collapse onto the sofa. Beyond checking WeChat or scrolling through Douyin for a mental massage of easy information, we want to do nothing. Emotional upheaval can prolong the depletion much further.

**A true master of self-control is not someone who knows only how to sprint, but someone skilled at deliberate rest and balance.** Such people keep energy and emotion at a high, abundant level through cycles of expenditure and recovery, avoiding low-level scarcity. Near the end of the day, we should pay particular attention to both, because temptations still await and one careless step can send us sliding downward.

#### The “Tomato” That Can Save You

Many people want the ability to focus intensely, but it cannot simply be wished into existence; it would not be scarce if it could. Focus depends on environment, matched interest, inner drive, background knowledge, accumulated habits, and more. How can ordinary people without ideal conditions develop it?

One simple, general method is to cultivate the pattern already described: extreme focus plus deliberate rest. Whenever work or study begins, focus as completely as possible, even if only briefly. As soon as insufficient energy causes the mind to wander, stop and adjust.

There is also a useful tool. The Pomodoro Technique, created by Francesco Cirillo in 1992, prescribes twenty-five minutes of highly focused work followed by five minutes of rest, repeated in cycles. It resembles high-intensity interval training.

I had known about Pomodoro for years but held two prejudices. First, strict timing seemed rigid—surely we should not stop while working enthusiastically. Second, real life contains interruptions, making timed work unrealistic.

The instant I understood deliberate rest, however, Pomodoro was the first idea that entered my mind. It contains every feature of the high-performer model: **extreme focus, deliberate rest, repeated cycles.**

I studied the method presented on the blog of Chinese time-management expert Li Can and bought the professional timer she recommended. The method and tool are excellent. When I begin reading or writing, I press the button and stop watching time, immersing myself until the alarm sounds after twenty-five minutes. Unless an exceptional matter demands interruption, everything else waits. At the alarm, I close the book or remove my hands from the keyboard immediately and begin a five-minute rest. During it, I do something unrelated—look out the window, collect laundry, open a package. I do not recommend scrolling on the phone or playing a game; these consume attention rather than restoring it.

After practicing for a period, I found that my learning endurance, working efficiency, and study efficiency all improved significantly. More importantly, I could still arrange my time actively in the evening and rarely became lost in entertaining information.

Twenty-five minutes is only a reference. Set the work interval according to your mental endurance. Some can concentrate for thirty minutes; others only fifteen. Stop voluntarily when you reach the edge of fatigue—and stop decisively. At first, people often resist because attention has not scattered visibly. Continuing usually costs more than it gains. Deliberate rest resembles drinking before you become thirsty; by the time thirst is strong, it is already late. To keep energy high, learn to stop proactively. This is a decisive point.

Many people lack an ideal, interruption-free environment, but the pattern still works. However many minutes are available, once you begin, focus as completely as possible and set everything unrelated aside.

Hard work is a broad attitude; ease is a fine-grained form of wisdom. The ordinary strategy is “make up for insufficient talent with toil.” We now know that the secret of learning lies not entirely in hard work but also in knowing how to play. May you learn with skill and style and become the high performer others admire.

## Chapter 6: The Ability to Act—Without Action, the World Is Only a Concept

### Section 1: Clarity—One Idea That Rebuilds Your Ability to Act

People often praise my capacity for action. At my age, they say, it is unusual to overcome laziness, resist temptation, rise early to exercise, read, and write while everyone else stays up late scrolling on a phone. They conclude that Zhou Ling must possess extraordinary self-control.

No. That is only the surface. My self-control is not strong at all. I may even have a stronger-than-average attraction to comfort, simplicity, novelty, and amusement. Even now, once I begin browsing on the phone, I cannot guarantee a clean escape. In the past, I had no chance.

I once spent whole days gaming and entire nights scrolling through Weibo, pulled from one trending item to the next without any thought of growth. The first thing I did after waking was pick up the phone. I always hoped to begin work in comfort, then wasted the day among trivialities. In completely discretionary time, I could do almost nothing. I knew more important work existed, yet a voice continually told me to find some amusement first. I drifted in a haze, a rudderless boat carried by life's current with no power to choose direction.

One day, after intense self-reproach, I resolved to rescue myself. The best solution I could imagine was formidable self-control. If I possessed it, surely everything would change. I tried many methods and every one failed. Human inertia was simply too powerful.

After countless failures, I discovered that fighting our nature leads nowhere. I also began to suspect that strong self-control is not the same as strong action. Later exploration confirmed it: real capacity for action does not come entirely from self-control. Once I understood this, I began rebuilding action through a new mechanism and slowly became another version of myself.

#### Treasure Each Day's Gift

You may not realize that every morning we receive a gift: clean attention. Whatever happened yesterday, a night's sleep resets your energy. Many people treat the gift casually. At the start of the day they plunge into phone messages or whatever looks interesting and become lost. It is like throwing a precious gift onto the ground. Repeated over time, this can hardly invite good fortune.

Why? Life is a complex system whose good and bad outcomes follow operating laws. In *Thinking in Systems*, Donella Meadows describes a foundational pattern called a reinforcing loop. Imagine two children arguing: one throws a punch, the other kicks harder, and every response intensifies the conflict. **Attention follows the same law. The first choice determines the direction in which behavior automatically reinforces itself.**

If the first act after waking is checking the phone, interesting messages, amusing videos, and eye-catching headlines can pull attention along a chain. Each tap creates a stronger desire for the next. The loop strengthens and attention disperses without limit. Once emotion adapts to easy amusement, it expects still more of it, creating a second reinforcing loop. The day has barely begun, yet attention and emotion are already shaped, making difficult or tedious work harder to enter (see Figure 6-1).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_35.jpg)

Figure 6-1 The reinforcing loop of attention

The reverse is also possible. If we deliberately avoid easy entertainment after waking and begin with reading, exercise, or important work, energy gathers and reinforces itself. Exercise clears the mind and fills the body with energy; important work then proceeds smoothly, and successful work further improves our state. Reading produces more questions and feelings as we continue, which strengthen the desire to read. Once the action loop gains momentum, we enter an efficient, fulfilling state and have little energy left for messages of no real consequence. **Whether attention's reinforcing loop becomes positive or negative depends largely on the initial choice.** This is the familiar principle: first things first.

There is another lesson. As Figure 6-2 shows, **the least self-control is required to make a beneficial choice at the beginning of a reinforcing loop. Once a negative loop forms, changing direction becomes much harder.**

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_36.jpg)

Figure 6-2 Choice is easiest at the start of a reinforcing loop

After a long session on Douyin while messages continue arriving, concentrating on work or study is no easy matter. Use good steel at the cutting edge: **force yourself to do the important work first. Once a positive reinforcing loop forms, powerful action follows. This is the secret to multiplying self-control and action**, and it applies to everyone.

#### Clarity Is the Real Ability to Act

Knowing the principle is not enough. Many understand it; few practice it. You may prepare to exercise, read, or complete important work early tomorrow, yet wake and reach for the phone as if possessed. Group messages, Moments, public accounts, Douyin, news—half an hour vanishes while you remain in bed. When nothing new remains, you open the apps again in hope of a surprise. Only when stimulation is exhausted and boredom fills the body do you rise listlessly, craving more stimulation to fill the emptiness. Important work is now the last thing on your mind.

The distance between knowing and doing is immense. What fills it?

The ambiguity described earlier.

Your “important work” may be no more than a rough idea about exercise, reading, or a project. You have not decided whether to run or read after waking. Even if you know which, you may not know where to run, how far or long, what to wear, or what to do if the weather is bad; which book to read, where to begin, or for how long; which part of the important work to tackle or what tools and materials it needs. **Knowing everything only approximately is fatal to action.**

First things first is therefore insufficient. We need **the power of clarity—the ability to refine a goal and make it concrete. Only clarity can rebuild our ability to act.**

#### One Method for Creating Clarity

Understanding this makes the mind clearer, but the starting point may remain elusive. Do not worry. Building clarity requires only three words: write it down.

Yes—write it down. Figure 6-3 shows my deliberately simple method.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_37.jpg)

Figure 6-3 Daily planning

Step one: take an ordinary A5 notebook and fold each page in half.

Step two: write every task for the day at the top. Empty them from the mind and number them by importance. The goals become visible and clear.

Step three: gather all available information and write on the left what you expect to do during each period. At the bottom, total “planned study time” and “available study time.” Time becomes clear too.

Step four: record actual execution on the right. At day's end, total study time and outcomes. The efficiency of time use becomes obvious.

The page contains four parts arranged roughly like a capital I with broad crossbars:

(1) Tasks

(2) Planned completion

(3) Actual completion

(4) Notes

This method includes the most frequently used functions of a time-management journal while allowing free writing and drawing. The notes area can capture inspiration or information; the notebook can be discarded when full. There is no need to spend effort on appearance, and both financial and time costs are minimal.

As Figure 6-4 shows, between February 2017 and this manuscript's deadline, I nearly filled nine notebooks. Continuous planning and recording gave me progressively greater control over free time. I can constrain myself deliberately and always know what comes next and what matters most. Even when messages distract me, a reminder quickly returns me to the path. I owe all of this to clarity.

I have shared the method with many people, but most will not try it. Some find it old-fashioned; others think holding the plan in their heads is enough and writing is redundant. Reality creates a loop: without action, they cannot experience the benefit; without experiencing the benefit, they consider the method useless. Only those who have tried understand that **writing and not writing are entirely different.** The true gap between people often lies in that final small act.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_38.jpg)

Figure 6-4 The notebooks I have used

On May 6, 2019, Amy Cao wrote: “Carefully writing down everything I need to complete each day really works. I knew the method before but did not value it and never planned seriously. After May 1, 2019, I began treating it properly. I feel much more in control and no longer remain anxious, afraid, or worried about failing to finish. The plan may change during the day, but the general direction remains in my hands.”

Writing down has this remarkable effect because it clears working memory. Empty every idea from the head and the mind instantly becomes clear. Each idea also becomes explicit and definite, placing us in a state of “no choice.” We no longer spend mental energy thinking or choosing during execution.

Action fears ambiguity above all. If many vague options remain in the mind, we must spend energy repeatedly choosing, and choice is cognitively expensive. To save effort, the brain may unconsciously select the most familiar and certain option: something easy and pleasurable but unimportant.

People also ask whether such planning makes life rigid. It does not. **The purpose is not strict obedience to a plan but knowing where you stand.** If circumstances change, adjust. The plan lets you return immediately after an unexpected task. Without it, vague goals and time make entertainment much more likely. Planning is highly effective; simply revise it when interruptions occur.

The format particularly suits people with abundant free time or indoor work. Those frequently on the move can adapt it and clarify goals by other means. I reserve ten minutes before bed to plan and a few minutes the next morning to review, checking and adjusting occasionally during work.

At the beginning of a twenty-four-hour day, spend a little time identifying and reminding yourself of the most important task. The resulting efficiency may exceed imagination.

#### Growth Is a System

The understanding and method are now clear, and you may feel delighted. I must still warn you not to treat this method as a lifeline. Improving action cannot depend on one factor such as first things first or greater clarity. Growth is a system produced by many interacting elements. If a person lacks a purpose, pursuing action alone is like climbing a tree to catch a fish.

**In one sense, having something you love matters far more than action itself.** Passion automatically brings first things first, clarity, and other useful qualities. Beyond clarity, we need the perception to find a goal, the matching ability to manage freedom, metacognition to guide everything, and more. Only by connecting them can we improve from the deepest level.

#### Everything Begins with Thinking Clearly

**When you are trapped in lethargy, laziness, or emptiness and cannot move, the brain is usually in a state of ambiguity.** It does not know what it wants; wants too many things at once and cannot identify the most important; or knows the goal but has not decided when and how to achieve it.

Whatever your state, take out pen and paper and write the goal and time. Metacognition rises quickly and motivation returns. The conclusion remains the same: the clearer the cognition, the firmer the action.

Einstein is often quoted as saying: “If I had one hour to solve a problem on which my life depended, I would spend fifty-five minutes determining the proper question. Once I knew the question, I could solve the problem in five minutes.”

Intelligent thinkers understand that clarity is the key to everything and willingly spend more time on it than anyone else. Ordinary people do the opposite, plunging into life's detailed current and drifting because it feels effortless. Thus ordinary people believe knowing is easy and doing difficult; the wise know that understanding is difficult and action easy. This deserves reflection.

If you genuinely think it through, I believe you will act and rebuild your capacity for action. Once you do, the feeling is entirely different.

### Section 2: “Fools”—The World Rewards Those Who Act Without Calculating Every Gain and Loss

I have long wondered where a person first begins cutting through fantasy and taking grounded action. My eventual answer was surprising yet reasonable: perhaps it begins when we are fortunate enough to behave like a “fool.”

I would wager that almost everyone who has bought piles of unread books, enrolled in piles of untouched courses, and carried countless desires has never deliberately completed one meaningful undertaking—forming a habit of rising early, running, or reading; developing skill in writing or drawing; earning strong grades; building a good company; or achieving a high income. This judgment comes from many people's experience.

I am no gambler and know every rule has exceptions. I rarely speak in absolutes. The wager is meant to attract your attention and thought, not provoke an argument. Too many people spend every day wanting to improve and exploring everywhere, only to make no progress and accumulate more anxiety.

I understand because I lived this way. Two thoughts continually occupied me. First, I should act only after seeing a definite outcome; if the prospect remained uncertain, I would not invest however reasonable the advice sounded. Second, if a principle or method could not change me quickly, it was not optimal, so I should keep searching until I found the best.

At the time, this seemed clever. Looking back, I was too clever for my own good. My calculation became an invisible threshold that kept me outside the door of growth.

One day, almost accidentally, I broke through. Everything began to change.

#### The Paradox of Growth

In September 2016, I subscribed on the Dedao app to Li Xiaolai's column *The Road to Financial Freedom*. Following his requirement to read without skipping a word, I made a peculiar decision: I retyped every article on a keyboard, including important comments beneath it. Four days a week for fifty-two weeks, roughly two hours a day, I “read” the entire column this way.

Never before had I read a “book” with such care. Through the practice, I thought intensely about dimensions, value, compounding, patience, metacognition, essential needs, writing, and other important concepts.

In February 2017, Cheng Jia's *Study Well* introduced me to daily reflection. Deeply affected, I began practicing. I wrote for 160 days without stopping and naturally developed the idea of a public account. In July, I launched *Clear Mind*. Those 160 daily reflections had made me examine my state and goals seriously and experience the real benefits of writing. I have maintained the habit ever since; by the manuscript deadline, I had written more than a thousand reflections. Looking back suddenly, I found that I had written my first book.

Once I foolishly typed; once I foolishly wrote. Both showed me the benefits of creating words and led me to accomplish something deliberately. Previously, small calculations and the demand to see results before acting had wasted enormous time.

This is a paradox of growth: **people who insist on seeing the result before acting often never see a result.** The overclever fear that effort may bring no return when the outcome is unclear. They refuse to act and remain forever where they began (see Figure 6-5).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_39.jpg)

Figure 6-5 A distant goal cannot be seen clearly

When a principle is sound, put aside petty calculation and move forward without obsessing over gains and losses. As you approach, the goal becomes clearer (see Figure 6-6).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_40.jpg)

Figure 6-6 Move closer and the goal becomes clear

The logic is simple, yet we overlook it. The problem is not failure to understand; it is that action changes our perspective. Before deliberately accomplishing something, the world appears flat and two-dimensional. Afterward, we can see from the side into a three-dimensional world and recognize differences in levels of understanding.

In that three-dimensional world, people at different cognitive levels take radically different views of the same question. *Deliberate Learning* describes the contrast:

You think English is useless because you cannot see where life requires it. Only after learning it well do English-related opportunities gradually appear. You think education is useless because you do not know how much it can alter the course of a life; from your current scene, a qualification looks like only another sheet of paper. You think exercise is useless precisely because you do not exercise and therefore cannot experience its value.

The world contains cognitive levels. People on one level cannot see the landscape above and judge narrowly: these ideas sound reasonable, but do not appear useful.

From where they stand, the ideas really are useless, because no one can prove an event that never occurred. The only way to break the paradox is to act, raise cognition to another level, and then judge differently.

**I have emphasized thinking clearly, but when thought is exhausted and clarity still does not come, act first on the well-founded hypotheses of those before us.** Only action brings us closer to the goal's true nature and allows clearer thought.

Many people want to find a purpose before acting. Without action, they may never find one. Low-dimensional understanding and experience cannot reveal what we truly want. Begin by following a sound hypothesis toward a higher level, and a life's purpose may gradually emerge.

**Thinking matters, but thinking without acting causes endless harm.**

Complete even one meaningful undertaking and you discover how difficult accomplishment really is.

This reveals another paradox. Before deliberately accomplishing anything, we assume achievement is easy and produce many unrealistic desires and ideas. The more desires we have, the less we accomplish (see Figure 6-7).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_41.jpg)

Figure 6-7 The cycle of “doing” and “wanting”

Only after completing something do we discover how little we can truly do. Desire shrinks, anxiety dissipates, and we can concentrate on the work at hand.

Judge by results. Once reality teaches you the truth about growth, learning anxiety, short-lived enthusiasm, and knowledge without action cease to be mysteries. You cut through fantasy, focus on one point, and act calmly because no alternative exists.

Do not covet the millionaire in their twenties or envy someone famous overnight. If their stories cannot actually change you, they are fantasies as far as you are concerned. Use action to improve yourself steadily instead. **Reality is the best judge.**

#### Crossing the Threshold

The way through these paradoxes is to act before calculating gain and loss. Some people disagree because they have acted and still cannot see results, experience benefits, or reduce desire.

If so, examine the volume of action and ask whether it crossed the threshold required for change. Effort must reach a certain level before it affects a system. Below that threshold, action produces little.

Advertising displays this effect. Too little exposure produces almost no response; the volume must cross a threshold before the audience reacts.

Action is the same. Focus and continue until you cross the threshold and see the landscape of a higher level (see Figure 6-8).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_42.jpg)

Figure 6-8 The volume of action must cross a threshold

My own experience is vivid. When building a habit, I do not use twenty-one days as the standard; I require at least six months. Anything sustained for 180 days, I believe, becomes a habit.

Rising early and running were painful at first. After passing through that stage, I experienced remarkable benefits in body and mind. Looking back, the hardest period was probably the threshold, and I am fortunate I did not quit. Now I feel uncomfortable on a day when I fail to rise early and run. When I cannot stop, I know I have crossed the threshold and reached another level.

The same path built my habits of reading, writing, and daily reflection.

#### Be a “Fool” with Sound Reasons

Quotation marks around “fool” show that I do not consider such a person truly foolish. The behavior can be intelligent. This foolishness is not blindness or impulse, but firmness grounded in principles and evidence.

Strong action comes from accepting the principle, evidence, and meaning behind an activity—not copying it without understanding because someone else praised it. The latter is genuine foolishness.

In other words, **if another person's argument is well founded, you cannot currently refute it, and you strongly want to do the thing, assume the argument is correct and act with conviction** (see Figure 6-9).

Continue thinking during practice and repeatedly test the theory through action. **If it does not fit, change it; if it fits, use it** until you genuinely succeed. By then you will not only have accomplished the task but developed your own theory and become an expert in others' eyes.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_43.jpg)

Figure 6-9 A well-founded hypothesis begins all progress

Firmly applying even a small theory can yield more than expected. I have told many readers how to improve at the edge of the comfort zone. Most agree but do nothing. A reader using the name Queen Ruru followed the method strictly and, in two weeks, raised the metronome speed for her piano examination from 160 to 192, dispelling her pre-exam anxiety.

Likewise, many readers nodded at the Pomodoro method of deliberate rest in Chapter 5 without trying it. One mother applied it with her child immediately and saw a dramatic change: “Using Pomodoro to guide holiday study during National Day was extremely effective. He set his own timer, studied, and rested by himself. Sometimes he finished all his homework in only two hours. The father-son battles over homework ended. He has more time for games and remains intensely focused while studying. The change in these few days has been enormous.”

Presented with the same principle, some call it empty inspiration and useless; others find it highly practical. If you continue acting, I believe the world will show you special favor.

### Section 3: Action—How to Solve “I Understand It, but I Still Don't Do It”

Among the world's difficult tasks, uniting knowledge and action must rank high.

Countless people cannot understand why they “know so many principles yet still fail to live well.” The confusion arises naturally and appears on almost every path of growth.

Some escape; others remain trapped. Those outside see clearly, while those inside cannot understand why great effort never produces sustained action. One belief holds them: they are not acting because they have not found the best method. Once they find it, everything will change. So they travel farther and farther along the road of collecting principles.

They read reasonable books, save reasonable articles, and feel they know everything, yet never bend down to act. They are not ready; the method may not be optimal; rash action may create detours. They fail to see that watching and waiting are themselves maximally inefficient. Worse, the more principles they know, the weaker action becomes, because a better idea always seems to await discovery.

Much knowledge and little action form a knot that tightens as we pull. We watch ourselves become giants in cognition and dwarfs in action without knowing what to do. When peers—or even younger people—achieve success through steady work, all our principles pour out as anxiety. The more we know, the more anxious we become, until helplessness makes us abandon the whole effort.

Impatient people repeatedly try to skip action and find a shortcut. Eventually they discover that this was the detour and that the real shortcut was the long, apparently inefficient road of action.

Not long ago, a reader named A Li left a classic question: “I understand all the principles but still do nothing. What should I do?” I replied: “There is one solution: do it directly.”

The answer was not wrong, but it was too simple. It deserves a fuller, clearer explanation. Having passed through the problem myself, I hope every confused life can remove the obstruction and that your struggle between knowing and doing ends here.

#### Cognition Is Actually a Skill

I too once knew without acting. Knowing satisfied me, my capacity for action was weak, and I rarely completed anything through sustained initiative. Understanding how the brain learns finally removed the obstruction. Most of us have a large blind spot here.

Scientists explain that **learning any skill fundamentally means forming connections among neurons in the brain.** Repeated activity stimulates two or more previously unrelated neurons until they form a strong association.

The principle is easy to understand. Before learning to ride a bicycle, watching another person makes it look simple: steer with the hands and alternate the feet. Try it yourself and the reality is different. Your center of gravity sways, the handlebars wander, speed remains low, fear of falling rises, and tension takes over.

There has not yet been enough practice to stimulate the relevant neurons and form strong connections. You may understand cycling intellectually, but you do not possess the skill. Only after learning and using it repeatedly in daily life do the neural connections become exceptionally strong and cycling become truly yours.

As Figure 6-10 shows, simply knowing cannot complete the feedback loop of **skill learning**. Extensive practice must form strong associations among the relevant neurons, allowing the loop to close through the point of doing.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_44.jpg)

Figure 6-10 The path of skill learning

Every skill follows this pattern: walking, speaking, drawing, playing piano. Each requires enough practice for the subconscious to execute it automatically.

During **cognitive learning**, however, a natural illusion arises: understanding a principle feels like mastering a skill. The instant we learn a fact, grasp a concept, or understand an argument, cognition genuinely improves and we may even apply it briefly. The sensation is wonderful, like discovering a new self. Merely rehearsing the idea in the mind creates positive feedback (see Figure 6-11).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_45.jpg)

Figure 6-11 The path of cognitive learning

The feedback is real in the moment, but one intense neural stimulus cannot form a strong association. The cognition remains unstable. Yet the brain has already received its reward and believes it possesses the idea, causing us to ignore or undervalue the large volume of practice still required.

Most people therefore stop cognitive learning at the stage of feeling they possess or know. Ordering a book feels wonderful, as though its knowledge already belongs to us; after delivery, we may never think of reading it. Learning the concept of metacognition makes us marvel that this is the most important human ability, but in a real situation we forget to use it. Realizing that a day without a phone would cost nothing brings sudden clarity about information's harm; days later, we put aside the book and reach for the phone again.

However good a principle is, without deliberate practice and strong neural connections it never truly affects us.

If you currently know without acting, do not blame yourself. Avoiding difficulty is human nature and is deeply inscribed in our genes. Given an easy and a hard way to obtain something, people choose the easy one. Given a short and a long feedback loop, they choose the short. This is the brain's default logic, difficult to notice without awareness.

Combining the paths of skill and cognitive learning in Figure 6-12 makes the cause of knowledge without action clear.

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_46.jpg)

Figure 6-12 Cognition is a skill

Fortunately, we can awaken. Awakening means seeing clearly, changing the default deliberately, and choosing anew. From now on, **treat cognition as a skill. When you understand a principle, do not celebrate too soon; remember the extensive practice ahead, and impatience subsides.**

#### Poor Performance at the Beginning Is Normal

Another reason people avoid action is that initial attempts go badly and produce no obvious result, so they quit.

This sounds reasonable but is absurd. Whether learning a skill or forming a habit, the brain must develop neuronal associations from few to many and weak to strong. At the beginning, when the associations are weak, poor performance is normal.

We cannot play fluently at the first piano lesson or adopt early rising effortlessly at once. When performance is poor, examine the real starting point and give neurons time to connect. If we could perform well at the beginning, what would there be to learn?

Many people act only if they believe they possess an absolute advantage, intense interest, or great talent. Otherwise they quit, like children who will play a game only when victory is guaranteed. We are not children. A mature mind accommodates initial clumsiness, continues practicing despite poor performance, and gives the brain sufficient time.

Tony and Barry Buzan, authors of *The Mind Map Book*, describe habit formation vividly.

Each time you produce a thought, biochemical and electromagnetic resistance along its neural pathway decreases slightly, like clearing a trail through a jungle. The work is arduous at first. With every additional passage, the trail becomes more complete and resistance declines, until the narrow path grows flat and broad.

Continued practice therefore makes neural associations inevitably stronger. Even when you temporarily seem to regress, do not despair; you may have entered a learning plateau.

Hope and patience live within deliberate practice and strengthening neural connections. Whether or not you initially like the activity, sustained practice makes performance improve day by day. Eventually, you will experience the pleasure of genuinely being able to do it.

#### We Can Actually Do Very Little

This discussion may discourage some readers because it repeatedly presents a harsh reality: principles cannot be practiced quickly or easily. Another fact follows: we can truly do very little. This brings us to another reason people fail to act—too many desires.

People who say “I understand but do not act” usually have no idea how much effort one real accomplishment requires. They have rarely completed something through sustained initiative; all their plans circle in the mind. Anyone who has learned piano to fluency or transformed early rising into a lifelong habit knows that accomplishment is a long process, never achieved in a single leap. We must break the illusion. The best way is to focus on actual change, because reality is the best judge. If learning creates no genuine change, what value can more learning have?

More than two years of practice have convinced me that **learning without real change is ineffective learning.** However reasonable an article or book may be, if it ultimately changes nothing in me, reading it was ineffective. When a situation requires the knowledge, I cannot retrieve any of the ideas to which I once nodded. However sound they appear, they have no real relationship with me, and I abandon them without hesitation.

**Once change becomes the highest standard for learning, the volume you consume may decrease.** When you truly understand that only one thing can be most important, every online article about focus loses its attraction. If you have not understood, each similar article still sounds persuasive and enters the saved-items folder.

**Reality and theory both tell us that understanding a hundred points is inferior to changing one. Genuine growth lies not in how many principles we know, but in how much we change.**

Abandon the fixation on knowing many principles—and sincerely celebrate doing so. The world is full of people who know without acting. Any action lets you surpass a great many of them.

For growth, **principles are promises on paper; change is hard currency.** Make change the standard in everything, and the path becomes clearer.

## Chapter 7: Emotional Capacity—Emotion Is the Wisdom of Seeing from Many Angles

### Section 1: Mental Bandwidth—Only Abundance Can Dispel Anxiety

Our generation is fortunate to experience unprecedented material abundance, advanced technology, and convenient information.

Even so, a psychology of scarcity remains in the subconscious and can emerge unexpectedly to influence choices and decisions.

Many people see nothing wrong with this, believing scarcity taught them thrift. But would you revise that view if you knew scarcity can make a person less intelligent?

#### Scarcity Makes Us Less Intelligent

No one wants to become less intelligent, but sometimes it is unavoidable. Sendhil Mullainathan, coauthor of *Scarcity*, studied sugarcane farmers in India. Before the harvest, when money was tightest, urgent concerns about subsistence occupied them constantly. They appeared impatient and short-sighted and performed worse in action, self-control, reaction speed, and IQ tests. After the harvest provided income, both cognition and action improved visibly. They became calmer, planned long-term goals, and acted toward them.

Under certain conditions, poverty does make people less intelligent—not because it reduces inherent ability, but because the scarcity it creates captures attention and lowers mental bandwidth.

Mental bandwidth is the mind's available capacity. It supports cognition, action, and self-control. When bandwidth falls, judgment fails easily. We make unwise choices, demand quick results, lose patience, and struggle to resist pleasure.

Human brains have broadly similar capacities, but scarcity pressure adds a hidden program running in the background. Though invisible, it consumes large mental resources. Someone may keep leftovers for another day and call it thrift. Someone under debt pressure may lose patience while helping a child with homework. Someone consumed by worry cannot settle down to learn. Low bandwidth prevents long-term thought, patience, and focus and biases choice toward the safest option with the quickest visible result.

The resulting short-sighted behavior intensifies scarcity. Eating spoiled leftovers may create medical bills far greater than the cost of the food. Shouting at a child adds stress. Scrolling on the phone while studying creates more worry. A vicious cycle strengthens the negative loop and makes the anxious still more anxious.

Scarcity is only one trigger. **Any stressful event occupies mental bandwidth**: tomorrow's speech, an examination deadline, fear of unemployment. **Whenever one enormous concern captures attention, we can enter a scarcity state, reduce bandwidth, and behave unwisely.**

A store announcement—“Half price today only; tomorrow it returns to 999 yuan”—may make us reach for the wallet out of fear of missing the discount. Only later do we realize the item was never necessary.

We joke that lovers have zero IQ. In passionate love, each sees only the other's virtues. Attention is completely captured and mental bandwidth consumed. “Beauty is in the eye of the beholder” describes the same effect: scarcity reduces judgment.

#### Demanding Quick Results Produces Anxiety

Material scarcity seems less important today. Many worry not about insufficient food but eating too much.

What was once a luxury is instantly available: search provides information, delivery provides food, high-speed rail provides travel, and online resources provide learning. Yet no one would accept the conclusion that ending material scarcity guarantees happiness; competitive pressure remains very real.

Modern society supplies convenience and choice alongside unprecedented speed. We feel that one careless pause will leave us behind and unconsciously seek more advantages.

Student anxiety offers a clear example. Many university readers tell me that they feel restless and cannot settle down to study. Asked about her goals, one second-year student said she wanted, within a short period, to learn rhetoric, logic, stylistics, philosophy, cognitive neuroscience, educational neuroscience, English, German, Hebrew, Japanese, artificial intelligence, and psychiatry—all at once.

She knew the task was impossible, but competitive pressure made the desire overwhelming. In ancient times, mastering one skill could sustain a person for life. Her desires were like multithreaded processes running simultaneously and consuming all bandwidth. Nothing remained for foresight, patience, action, or self-control. She could only wander in pain, unable even to perform the small task before her.

That case is extreme, but another pattern is common. Students and workers fill holidays or free time with improvement plans that leave no margin. Each time, reality falls far short of the ideal and they become trapped in entertainment instead.

The principle is the same: **facing many simultaneous tasks reduces mental bandwidth and therefore weakens action and self-control.** Experienced people restrain desire, deliberately schedule recreation alongside important work, and leave margin in the calendar. This is both scientific and wise.

The most anxious group in modern society may be adults around thirty, who stand simultaneously at three thresholds: responsibility, with parents above and children below; career, with older competitors still present and younger ones arriving; and comparison, with wealth on one side and power on the other. Some drift into this stage and suddenly discover immense family obligations, an uncertain career, and former classmates or colleagues far ahead. They awaken abruptly as anxiety surges: “There isn't enough time. Everything is too late. I can never catch up.”

Even after resolving to pursue, they easily fall into blind experiments, chaotic learning, and demands for quick results. They quit the moment no effect appears and become still more anxious. The true cause is a scarcity of success that sharply reduces bandwidth: uncertainty about life runs in the background while urgent tasks occupy every thread. Escape becomes difficult because no resources remain for foresight, patience, action, or self-control.

We now possess adequate food and clothing but face stronger competition and many additional burdens. Information is easy to search, but entertainment reaches into every gap to tempt us. Online shopping is convenient, but unnecessary desire leads us to stockpile unwanted things.

**Modern life has reduced pressure on survival while increasing pressure on self-control. Resisting temptation and desire consumes mental bandwidth.** Even people with abundant time and money fall into boredom and emptiness without enough bandwidth.

#### Only Mental Abundance Can Dispel Anxiety

Material conditions do not determine destiny. The real question is whether mental bandwidth is abundant. With enough, we retain foresight, patience, action, and self-control in any environment and can rescue ourselves as circumstances change. How can we acquire it? Self-awareness matters most. I offer five remedies; take whichever you need.

**First, remain aware of the environment and choose rationally.** Pressure most severely affects some people's perspective and foresight. To preserve both, use advanced metacognition to remain aware of the environment. Without awareness, pressure squeezes bandwidth; with deliberate awareness, we can bear pressure, concentrate mental resources, and decide rationally.

**Second, remain aware of your goals; less is more.** Knowing your purpose matters because it directly governs the use of bandwidth. Wu Jun, author of *Insight*, writes in effect: “Many people consider me a master of time and ask how to do more things at once. My secret is the opposite: do fewer things, or even nothing. I regularly examine from the perspective of an entire life what I genuinely need to do, break habitual assumptions, and reject everything whose omission would not make the sky fall.”

This is the true meaning of less is more. Knowing what we want frees us from blind haste and constant busyness and leaves time to examine ourselves and concentrate energy on what matters most.

**Third, remain aware of desire and examine decisions.** For some, excessive desire is the greatest assault on bandwidth. Reaching for the phone on waking expresses a desire for information; hoarding objects, a desire for possessions; too many social obligations, a desire for connection; overeating, a desire for food. Every emerging desire can add another process to the mind.

Observe carefully: **when many tasks and thoughts occupy the mind, our ability to act is usually weakest.** Awareness and prompt examination of desire clear bandwidth. When my mind becomes tangled, I sit with pen and paper and list every thought. Hidden background processes and visible foreground ones alike become clear. Examining them one by one immediately leaves me refreshed and ready to act.

**A true master of action is not someone capable of doing many things simultaneously, but someone who prevents that situation from arising.** Such people do not overfill schedules. In study plans and work arrangements, they preserve enough margin to face each moment calmly.

**Fourth, remain aware of emotion and decide cautiously.** Make no major decision at the height of excitement or anger. Intense joy and grief narrow bandwidth and weaken judgment.

Attend also to smaller everyday emotions—fear, worry, tension, dread—and clear them promptly. A person with abundant bandwidth is also calm.

**Fifth, remain aware of surplus and impose limits.** Appropriate margin is a precious resource for pressure and surprise, but excessive surplus is not beneficial. Abundant money can generate pointless desire; abundant time can produce inefficiency. Bandwidth may be available, but without useful life programs it is wasted.

If life has given you extraordinary abundance, impose limits and create an appropriate scarcity in order to realize yourself.

### Section 2: A Single Perspective—Bad Emotions Come from Seeing Only One Angle

In 1934, Franklin D. Roosevelt's home was burglarized. A friend wrote to comfort him and urged him not to dwell on the loss. Roosevelt replied: “Dear friend, thank you for your concern. I am safe and grateful to life. First, the thief took my property but did not harm my life. Second, he took only part of what I owned, not everything. Third, and most fortunately, he was the thief—not I.”

Roosevelt's ability to guide his own emotion was formidable. Many would dismiss the story as sentimental self-consolation, while others might say he could think so generously only because he was president. I disagree. I prefer the reverse inference: his **ability to see a problem from many angles** helped take him to the presidency.

In any difficulty, the ability to shift perspective is a key test of problem-solving. It helps us acquire wisdom, achieve in work, enlarge our outlook, and dissolve trouble. Yet many people remain unaware and treat every problem through one primitive angle.

#### The World Has Many Dimensions, but We Have Only One Pair of Eyes

While out one weekend, I found an impressive motorcycle with a sidecar and took a photograph (see Figure 7-1). Like most people, I chose an angle showing the entire machine, adequate for sharing online. Because I enjoy photography, however, I crouched and took another image from below (see Figure 7-2).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_47.jpg)

Figure 7-1

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_48.jpg)

Figure 7-2

The lower angle made the picture instantly vivid:

· small flowers on the ground created the feeling of an earthly paradise;

· a clean sky isolated and emphasized the vehicle;

· the enlarged sidecar and front wheel, with the body rising above the mountains behind, created imposing force.

The motorcycle never changed. I merely crouched and changed angle, yet the feeling became entirely different. **Every person, object, and event in the world is similarly multidimensional.** Each angle provides different information. Shift a camera slightly, move closer, or pull away, and the image changes.

In life, however, we habitually observe from the easiest and most familiar position. We stand comfortably, raise the camera, press the shutter, and assume we captured the whole. We captured only one of countless angles. Without strong awareness of this limitation, we treat a part as the whole and generate errors.

This is why the old saying advises that time reveals a person's heart. Over time, we observe someone across many contexts: angry and happy, frustrated and furious; dealing with the vulnerable and the powerful; entertaining themselves and disciplining themselves to learn. People who judge from one angle are often naive and easily hurt because they lack awareness of multidimensional understanding.

Speech alone can roughly reveal learning and character. Someone who clings fiercely to their views while refusing every other one can generally be regarded as poorly educated or weak in self-cultivation. A shallow learner perceives little beyond their original angle and clutches the first judgment, producing an equally limited character.

People of greater learning and cultivation usually express views cautiously and use words such as “perhaps” and “possibly.” This is not performative humility. The more they know, the more angles they see and the better they understand that no single sentence or view can describe the whole.

A person's temperament therefore also depends on the ability to see multiple angles. A single perspective breeds stubbornness, impatience, and fixation; multiple perspectives produce wisdom, peace, and tolerance.

The world is multidimensional, but we have one pair of eyes. Every observation, expression, and action affects only one dimension. Understanding this shows why no miraculous technique can solve every problem; any view or method normally applies only within a particular angle and range.

After writing hundreds of thousands of words on a subject, leading scholars often sincerely declare how limited their view remains. Donella Meadows, author of *Thinking in Systems*, writes in her preface: “I caution readers that this book, like all books, has biases and incompleteness. What I present may be only a small fraction of systems thinking. Explore it yourself and you will find a much larger world than the small one shown here.”

Meadows's understanding was extraordinary, yet she remained humble. To become calmer and wiser, first recognize the world's multidimensionality and inscribe that awareness deeply in the mind. Only then can change begin.

#### Become a Better Camera

The camera is a useful metaphor because it contains another meaning: cameras themselves differ.

You and I can photograph the motorcycle from the same angle and still produce different images because our lenses, sensors, and focus points differ. One person's image has a narrow field, distorted color, or poor focus; another's appears more faithful. Our environments, experiences, and learning differ in the same way, so we understand and reconstruct the same problem at different levels.

Some adults grow distant from their parents because they dislike the older generation's speech and habits or experience parental concern as interference. Many daughters-in-law conflict with mothers-in-law over child-rearing. Intimate couples become angry over different interpretations of the same event. If we understand that their “camera” differs from ours, we can see that they may not intend to oppose us and may even be doing their best.

If your camera is genuinely more advanced, practice downward compatibility: smile and let the matter pass, or show your higher-resolution image and patiently explain why it is better. Do not merely condemn the other person's poor photograph. A lower-level system cannot be upward-compatible, but guidance may help it upgrade. If you once owned an “outdated camera,” you should understand and accommodate the other position even more readily.

Remain aware of the camera. Recognize your own perspective errors and be ready both to upgrade upward and remain compatible downward. This attitude lets us improve continually and get along with others.

#### A Better Perspective Always Exists

Everyone is a photographer of life. Given the same subject, some produce beautiful images and others ordinary ones. A good photographer finds a better angle, moves around the “motorcycle,” tests perspectives, and chooses the best.

Roosevelt was an excellent photographer. After losing substantial property, he found three positive angles and quickly emerged from sadness. Another person might cling to the most miserable photograph and lament endlessly.

Do not be bound by the original angle. Shift deliberately and a new world may appear.

Different perspectives create different choices.

· Faced with half a glass of water, one person laments, “Only half remains,” while another delights, “Half still remains.”

· Faced with a setback, one becomes trapped in sorrow; another regards it as a message from life pointing toward growth.

· Faced with work, one believes they labor for the boss and avoids effort whenever possible; another regards every task as self-training and invests fully even without immediate reward.

Whatever emotional whirlpool currently surrounds you, a better angle can always be found if you are willing. **Some stare at one flaw in the best situation; others find and magnify one bright point in the worst while letting the shortcomings recede.**

Which is wiser and happier is obvious.

#### The Road to Mastery

Everyone wants to become a better “photographer,” but doing so requires practice. For years we have viewed problems through one original perspective until path dependence formed. When a child disobeys, our first response is anger and shouting rather than patiently asking for their real thoughts. When an employee performs poorly, our first response is criticism rather than calmly asking for the cause. We act emotionally without self-examination until we no longer know why anger comes so easily.

Wei Lan, author of *Against Instinct*, explains path dependence this way: repeated behavior gradually forms a dedicated “green channel” in the brain. In similar circumstances, the brain gives that behavior priority until the response becomes automatic.

This is why trouble habitually activates emotional defense and traps us in one perspective rather than activating rational analysis across many. To develop multiple perspectives, practice deliberately until a new path forms. The training is not difficult. Follow the principles below and you can gradually escape the single angle and become a master photographer of life.

**First, move often.** Move the camera and examine the problem from different positions: the child's, the elderly person's, the opponent's. Do not conclude from your own feeling alone that the child is unreasonable, the elder inconsiderate, or the opponent malicious.

When anxious or tense, imagine yourself as an outsider and observe from the third person. Many worries will appear unnecessary because others do not care about you nearly as much as you imagine. When trapped in sadness, place yourself ten years in the future and look back. Today's sorrow will seem meaningless; put it down and work.

This multidimensional observation is metacognition. It keeps self-observation aware and lends emotional intelligence to speech. We may desperately want to say something cruel, but before speaking we estimate how the words would feel from several angles, then choose the position that leaves everyone comfortable and respected. Who would want to make life difficult for such a person?

**Second, learn well.** Sometimes we cannot see an angle because we lack the knowledge to know it exists. Learn more and borrow the perspectives of accomplished thinkers. Excellent books and essays reveal their authors' distinctive angles; take them and learn to see.

**Third, remain open.** More precisely, remain objective and do not assume. **Many bad emotions arise because people treat assumptions as facts and release emotion before knowing what the other person actually thinks.** Calm communication requires removing colored lenses and subjective interpretation, finding the facts first, and learning the other person's real view. This applies to children, colleagues, employees, and managers alike. Enter with a fixed single view and an open response to objective truth becomes impossible.

Yijia offers a useful method from her marriage. First, listen selflessly to the other person's thoughts—without judgment, debate, right, or wrong—and place yourself entirely in their position to see through their eyes. Second, speak from “I,” describing only your objective feeling without accusing or prescribing. Say, “Dirty socks are scattered across the floor, and I feel tense and uncomfortable,” rather than, “Dirty socks are everywhere—doesn't that bother you?”

Good communication is objective and avoids subjective speculation, allowing both sides to leave combat mode. Release negative emotion without understanding and you pull others into a single perspective too: either they are suppressed and afraid or they resist and both sides are hurt.

Wu Jun takes an even wiser position: “With anyone, I generally begin by assuming that they are decent, kind, and honest.” Guiding openness with openness is advanced practice and the form of communication I admire.

**Fourth, seek help.** I have read pilots' checklists for handling unusual situations in flight and noticed that almost every procedure begins the same way: inform air traffic control. Why, amid an emergency, report before concentrating on the problem? Would that not waste time? Thinking about multiple perspectives gave me the answer. Danger captures a pilot's attention and reduces bandwidth, creating tunnel vision. A controller supplies an effective external perspective and helps manage the emergency.

Likewise, when an emotional or work problem defeats repeated thought, stop brooding alone. Seek external help and borrow other people's multidimensional perspectives to overcome the limits of your own.

**Fifth, exercise more.** Appropriate aerobic exercise raises dopamine, which matters for creativity and flexible thinking. Exercise helps us leave negative emotion quickly and guides the brain toward new perspectives. The worse your mood and the more stuck your thought, the more you should move.

**Sixth, reflect often.** Zhang Hui, author of *The Moat of Life*, recounts an experience of dissolving anger:

One October day, he spent all day in meetings and had an argument. He arrived home after ten at night still furious, with the day's promised article unwritten. He began writing about the anger because it left room for nothing else: its details and cause. Halfway through, relief appeared. Another angle let him understand the other person's position and circumstances and see the blind spot in his own view. The anger fell by half. The remainder of the article became an expression of release, and finishing left him deeply relaxed. Writing had soothed his mind in a way no consolation could.

I often have the same experience because I reflect daily. When depression will not disperse, I open the computer, pour out every trouble, and review it. During the process, clouds part and the sun appears. It is remarkable.

At any time, a pen or keyboard can carry you beyond one perspective and reveal more dimensions.

### Section 3: A Playful Mindset—Happy People Are Always Doing Something Else

One university experience remains unforgettable.

Graduation required every student to pass a 1,500-meter fitness test in no more than five minutes and ten seconds. To encourage training, the instructor set a rule: every physical-education class would begin with a 1,500-meter trial. Anyone achieving an excellent result could skip the remaining two hours. For a long period, I was the only person who could leave happily under the gaze of nearly a hundred teammates.

Running was not my strength. On entering university, I needed about eight minutes for 1,500 meters. As all of us pursued the five-minute-ten-second target, every attempt felt punishing: tense before, painful during, helpless afterward.

Just as despair approached, something changed. During a routine pre-class test, the whistle sounded and I shot to the front at sprinting speed. Beginning a middle-distance race this way is normally unwise because severe early exertion destroys the finish. As I prepared to slow down, however, my gaze dropped from the distance to a point about ten meters ahead. I told myself: “Don't slow yet. Wait until that point; then you can ease off.” On reaching it, my eyes moved to another point ten meters ahead. The distance looked short enough to repeat. Then I chose the next ten meters.

After several repetitions, I realized that I was not gasping. My body felt lighter. I was playing a chasing game, and attention had shifted from the oppressive number of laps remaining to one ten-meter segment at a time. Arms and legs moved with increasing ease. Without noticing, I led the second runner by nearly half a lap. The clear advantage made me forget the result and concentrate almost entirely on the pleasure of movement. I ran faster and faster. When the instructor announced my time and said I could leave, I could hardly believe it.

I repeated the method thereafter: sprint to the front and begin my solitary chasing game. I liked leading immediately because no other runner could interfere with immersion. A painful assessment became something I eagerly anticipated, and no one knew how I had suddenly improved. Even when a few others later achieved excellent times, they crossed the finish line in agony, unlike my game-like ease.

This method added much to my university life. I wrote one realization in a notebook: do not let the task itself imprison your emotion and attention. It is one of the few lessons from those years that I still remember.

Looking back, I am surprised. This was not a crude trick at all but genuine positive psychology.

#### Happiness Comes from Deliberate Control

Among the most influential ideas in modern positive psychology is Self-Determination Theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. It proposes three innate psychological needs: relatedness, competence, and autonomy (see Figure 7-3).

![img](assets/CB_33628204_epub_33628204_49.jpg)

Figure 7-3 Three innate needs in Self-Determination Theory

A happy life therefore requires:

· good relationships and the love and respect of others;

· distinctive abilities or skills that provide unique value;

· the freedom to choose and do what one wants.

The theory is simple. If people treat you well, you possess a distinctive skill, and you can do what you enjoy, would life not be delightful?

Autonomy is especially central. When we deliberately choose and control an activity, intrinsic motivation and happiness arise. Most people saw the 1,500-meter run as an imposed test to endure. I made it a game. Choice and control appeared, eventually producing both advantage and recognition. I changed only my interpretation, yet the whole situation transformed. This is the remarkable power of positive psychology.

Life confronts us with many things we do not want to do but must: fitness tests, mountains of homework, laundry, obligatory meetings, unwanted work. They create frustration and resistance because we did not choose them; external pressure did.

A life spent doing unwanted necessities becomes gray and tedious. But must pressure simply be endured? Not necessarily. Emotion and attention may only have been captured by the task itself. **Difficulty and pressure grip attention so tightly that other angles disappear.**

The world is more positive than we imagine. We think we have no choice, but many angles remain because everything is multidimensional. Behind a pessimistic appearance lies an optimistic side; behind seriousness, something playful. Our temporary blindness does not make it nonexistent. Fasten your seat belt and let us reclaim control of happiness.

#### You Are Simply Doing Something Else

Regaining control is easy. When facing something you do not want but must do, repeat one “spell”: **I am not doing this; I am simply doing something else.**

Applied elsewhere:

· I am not taking a running test; I am playing a chasing game.

· I am not doing homework; I am challenging my speed.

· I am not washing clothes; I am moving my hands and arms.

· I am not meeting a leader; I am chatting with an ordinary person.

· I am not working for my boss; I am improving myself.

The reasons may sound amusing, but do not underestimate the power of a new assumption. Once a choice exists, you realize that **the task itself is not important. You are using it to obtain another kind of pleasure and completing the task incidentally.** Psychology calls this motivational displacement.

Without awareness, motives are pulled by external things. We possess little autonomous choice and fall into doing merely for the sake of doing. Aware people notice whether their motive remains attached to something external and irrelevant to the goal. If so, they move it inward and recover choice and control. The method has two broad forms: **do it for yourself and do it for play.**

#### Do It for Yourself

The best way to create internal motivation is to make yourself better.

Consider writing. Many writers submit to platforms chasing trends because acceptance quickly brings payment and exposure. To pass review, they continually alter their style to suit the platform, chasing news, crafting clickbait titles, and producing shallow short pieces. This cannot produce genuine growth or enduring accumulation, and the pleasure of writing slowly disappears.

Writers who genuinely want to build influence are not wholly bound by payment and traffic. They write for their own growth, the needs of others, long-term value, and the creation of a world of their own. Without flowers or applause, they continue producing and growing. Feedback and rewards are unexpected delights, not demands. Because choice remains in their hands, the pen continues releasing power and eventually realizes the dream.

The principle applies to companies too. Huawei has long resisted going public partly to prevent external forces from controlling its motivation. A public listing might rapidly increase valuation, but would inevitably pull attention toward the next quarterly report.

People sensitive and committed to intrinsic motivation are different. They do not perform for external rewards or evaluation but strive for their own growth and progress. Difficulty rarely defeats them.

#### Do It for Play

If motivation can move, why not move it completely and make the task fun? This is an excellent idea.

When my daughter entered first grade, she disliked handwriting practice and became gloomy whenever it appeared. I asked: “Don't you like drawing? Why not treat this as drawing? Aren't writing and drawing both movements of a pen on paper?” Her eyes lit up: “That's right, Dad. I can treat it as drawing.” Before long, she had happily “drawn” the whole workbook.

A reader named Chengqian, who loves running, wrote a playful verse titled “Enjoyment Brings Longevity”:

Some run to grow thin,

Some run to live long,

I run to enjoy it,

Enjoying thinness and length of days.

I laughed when I first saw it. This was not merely verse; it was the psychology of motivational displacement. With attention on enjoyment, running feels different. Others persist painfully for appearance or health; he enjoys the process itself.

Beyond running, I treat many activities as play. I do not consider reading to be reading; I imagine conversations with wise people. Every book is a person, and the bookshelf is my circle of wise friends. Every few days I stand before it and decide who will speak with me next. The feeling is wonderful. If everything looked like play, who would remain miserable?

Observe carefully and another distinction appears: **doing it for yourself usually answers external pressure and requirements; doing it for play answers repetition and boredom.** To play more freely, break large, difficult tasks into small pieces, as I divided 1,500 meters into ten-meter segments. When the next action is small enough to complete easily, eagerness replaces resistance.

Stephen Guise follows the same idea in *Mini Habits*. He began with a single push-up and discovered the impulse to play. Hu Shi said: “Why fear that truth is infinite? Advancing one inch brings the joy of one inch.” Infinite truth may indeed intimidate, but attend only to the inch before you and take pleasure from it.

Growth sometimes requires looking far ahead to see meaning and generate motivation. At other times, look nearby so difficulty does not frighten you and progress remains light.

#### The World Takes the Shape of the Angle from Which We View It

Some will dismiss these methods as self-deception. In fact, humans are self-explaining animals, and we assign meaning to the world.

If action necessarily involves giving meaning, why not choose meanings that are both useful and enjoyable? At the very least, **doing it for yourself frees emotion, while doing it for play frees attention.** Once emotion and attention are free, what difficulty can stop us?

## Chapter 8: Rise Early, Meditate, Read, Write, and Run—Five Low-Cost Practices for Growth

### Section 1: Rising Early—How I Made It a Habit without Alarms, Groups, or Check-Ins

Dreams are not yet what wake me each morning, but neither is an alarm clock. Looking back, I have now lived as an early riser for four years. During that time, I have never used an alarm, joined an early-rising group, or checked in. I developed the habit naturally and entirely on my own.

Rising early has given me a great deal of discretionary time and transformed the way I live. It has had virtually no adverse effect on my health; in many respects, my health has actually improved. Seeing the proliferation of early-rising clubs, check-in groups, courses, and accountability groups, I feel it is worth sharing my experience. Perhaps it will help those who want to develop the same habit.

#### My Journey toward Early Rising

Four years ago, like many people, I enjoyed staying up late. I rarely went to bed before midnight. I would usually stare at my phone until I could no longer keep my eyes open, then drift off unhappily. My physical and mental condition was poor. I woke in a fog and remained listless during the day. I knew this undisciplined routine was unhealthy, but I simply lacked the energy to fight my inertia. I had heard about the benefits of rising early and knew that many famous people did it:

· Warren Buffett gets up at 6:45 every morning;

· Steve Jobs began work at around six;

· Pan Shiyi gets up at four, starts his morning run at six, and begins work before eight...

To me, these were extraordinary people. How could I possibly live as they did? Early rising belonged to another world—something to admire and leave at that. If someone had told me to set several alarms, check in every morning, or find people to supervise me, I would have been unmoved. I knew that an approach fueled purely by willpower would be painful and would probably fail. Then I read Japanese author Takashi Nakajima's *Wake Up at Four: The Healthiest and Most Efficient Time Management*.

Perhaps the title struck me as so extreme that I wanted to see what it was about. Four ideas in the book gave me an entirely new perspective.

First, get up at four and divide the day into three parts.

4:00 a.m.–12:00 p.m.: the first eight hours are used to complete existing work—that is, the work of an ordinary day.

12:00 p.m.–8:00 p.m.: the second eight hours are used to prepare for future work and can be regarded as an extra working day.

8:00 p.m.–4:00 a.m.: the third eight hours are for rest—still a full eight hours.

The author calls this “two seasons in one life”: getting up at four effectively gives you another working day without reducing your sleep. The argument does not withstand close scrutiny, but its apparent appeal was tempting. Merely changing the time you got up seemed to yield more usable time at no cost.

Second, the neuroscience of sleep.

The book describes how Nathaniel Kleitman and William Dement discovered through experiments that people's eyes move back and forth during sleep. From this pattern they identified rapid eye movement (REM) and non-rapid eye movement (non-REM) sleep. A healthy adult's sleep supposedly alternates in roughly ninety-minute periods of REM and non-REM sleep. The first two periods—the first three hours after falling asleep—contain high-quality sleep, with deep non-REM sleep corresponding to sound sleep. Later periods combine lighter non-REM and REM sleep. According to this pattern, waking after three, four and a half, six, or seven and a half hours leaves a person refreshed and energetic. My experiment with early rising began with a striking experience of this theory.

The night I finished the book, I fell asleep at about eleven. I stirred briefly at two in the morning, then again sometime between 3:30 and 4:00—almost exactly in line with the pattern described above. I was astonished. Once I knew about these intervals, I suddenly became more sensitive to them and began waking at their endpoints. Looking back, I had often stirred in the middle of the night before, but, not knowing that a sleep cycle had ended, I simply rolled over and went back to sleep. I tested the idea for several days, with largely consistent results.

The theory helped me understand why we can sleep for a long time yet wake feeling tired: we have awakened in the middle of a cycle rather than at its endpoint.

Third, give up the alarm clock.

Nakajima writes that an alarm clock takes no account of your sleep cycle. When the appointed time arrives, it reaches into your brain and sets off an earthquake, throwing the subconscious into turmoil. Because the alarm woke you while the deeper brain was still asleep, even eight hours can leave you feeling unrested and groggy. I also disliked being jolted awake. Compared with waking naturally, my mental state after an alarm was much worse, and much of the pain of getting out of bed came from that. So I abandoned alarms without hesitation.

Many early-rising courses now tell students to prepare several alarms: one by the bed, one in the bathroom, and another in the living room, each set a little later and louder than the one before. To me, forcing yourself up with such brute methods is torment; it makes early rising a painful experience. Once I had learned to sense the endpoints of sleep cycles—a habit that took about two weeks—I no longer worried about oversleeping or missing my normal wake-up time. The biological clock is remarkably precise.

Giving up the alarm has another advantage: it does not disturb family members or roommates, making their support easier to obtain.

Fourth, make use of the brain's peak working periods.

Beginning at dawn, the body releases adrenaline and corticosteroids, hormones that help maintain energy, and their secretion peaks at around seven in the morning. Work efficiency is then very high. About an hour after eating, the body's energy is converted into glucose and delivered to the brain, improving memory and comprehension. The brain reaches peak operating speed before declining to a low roughly four hours later. We should follow this rhythm and place our hardest work in these peak periods, achieving more with less effort. People who eat a proper breakfast also work more efficiently in the morning; after lunch, another efficiency peak appears between two and four in the afternoon.

These four ideas allowed me to begin rising early with almost no pain and to experience the changes it brought. I have continued ever since. This was probably a classic case of cognition driving action: **once an idea has become fully clear at the cognitive level, action no longer requires vast reserves of willpower.**

#### What Early Rising Has Given Me

The habit is now firmly established, and I wake naturally without an alarm. Sometimes I rise at five, sometimes at 4:30, and at the earliest at four. I am usually up no later than 5:30.

Compared with getting up at seven, I gain two hours a day. At eight working hours per day, that amounts to about ninety additional working days a year. Sustained for forty years, it is equivalent to a person working ten years without a day off. In those undisturbed hours, I can efficiently do the following.

**1. Plan.** I spend about ten minutes listing and prioritizing the day's work. This keeps my thinking clear, gives me a sense of command over my time, and prevents my work from going astray.

**2. Run.** I personally like to run soon after getting up. My brain is not fully awake then, so it can be difficult to enter demanding mental work immediately. After a run and a hot shower, however, I feel completely different: every cell in my body seems activated, and reading or writing comes easily. That state lasts throughout the morning. While others are rising groggily and heading to work, I am already full of energy. A morning run makes the body feel light all morning and improves one's resistance to winter cold. Over time, it also greatly improves physique and fitness.

Moreover, most people are still asleep, so I can enjoy the quiet of the morning alone. It is a wonderful feeling, unlike exercising at night, when repeatedly stopping to greet acquaintances can make a workout terribly inefficient.

**3. Reflect.** This is an assignment I give myself: review part of my work, sort through my thoughts, or record insights and feelings every day. It is an excellent way to improve.

**4. Read or write.** Family and work rarely leave me large blocks of time for self-improvement, so these early hours are precious. Many of my articles were written then.

**5. Tackle difficult work.** I sometimes use this period to attack hard tasks, usually with great efficiency. Going to work in the morning knowing that the hardest task is already complete makes me calm and cheerful. I can then take on forward-looking or exploratory work in a relaxed state.

These are the main gains I have received: a clear schedule, a stronger body, a better mental state, an undisturbed setting for exercise, a focused learning environment, composure at work, and sustained personal growth.

My anxiety has also diminished considerably. Long persistence has strengthened my perseverance, and, more importantly, by ten at night I am ready to climb into bed. My bad habit of staying up late has disappeared completely. Without the habit of rising early, I would probably still be staying up night after night, listless, perpetually busy, and making no progress. I do not care to imagine where that would leave me in five or ten years.

#### A Few Things to Keep in Mind

Although I developed the habit “easily,” the process was not entirely painless. At times I struggled. Building any habit is difficult. Here are some lessons from my experience.

**1. Expect a relatively painful adjustment period.** After about two weeks of excitement, I entered a period of adaptation. My routine had changed abruptly, and my body had not caught up. I sometimes woke but did not want to get up; on other days I yawned all morning, lacked energy, or developed darker circles beneath my eyes. It took roughly two months for these symptoms to ease. Once I crossed that threshold, rising early became much easier. What kept me going was the remarkable experience of waking without an alarm and the pleasure of stepping outside to enjoy the quiet morning. I would rather continue a little longer than give those things up too quickly.

**2. Proceed gradually and match the difficulty to your capacity.** First, do not force the wake-up time into place all at once. At the beginning, concentrate on sensing the endpoints of your sleep cycles and waking without an alarm. Do not pursue an excessively early hour at the cost of discomfort or pain. Second, choose activities according to your circumstances. You need not run as I do; especially in winter, that may create pressure around getting up. During my first two months, I merely strolled or walked briskly. It was pure enjoyment, helped by the mild autumn weather. I only added study and running later. Finally, make environmental transitions gradually, especially in winter. If possible, keep the room warm enough that leaving the bed requires little effort. Warm up indoors before exercising outside; I usually spend about twenty minutes warming up and wait until my body is fully awake and warm. Then even subzero temperatures do not feel cold. Matching the difficulty matters. If waking means immediately confronting a freezing environment, the pain will almost certainly make us give up.

**3. Get up according to your condition, not the clock.** Suppose you wake at four despite never having risen that early. Should you get up? My advice is that condition takes priority over time. If you are still foggy, sleep for another cycle—ninety minutes. If you feel clear and refreshed, get up. Do not worry that rising so early will exhaust you later in the morning. The body is highly adaptable; waking early on its own indicates that it is prepared.

**4. Take a midday nap.** On a day when you rise early, sleepiness will arrive around noon. A thirty-minute nap after lunch quickly restores energy, leaving you vigorous through the afternoon. The nap is important and is best not omitted.

**5. Do not disturb others.** If you do not live alone, speak with your family or roommates beforehand and assure them that you will not interrupt their rest. They will usually support you. If you plan to run or read the next morning, prepare your clothes, books, cup, computer, and other necessities the night before—and leave them outside the bedroom. Move quietly when you rise, as though merely getting up briefly during the night.

**6. Prepare in advance.** Preparing clothes is only the beginning. More importantly, decide exactly what you will do after waking. How will you adjust if it rains and you cannot run outside? What will you study if you wake half an hour late? Make contingency plans for the situations that may arise. When the mind has clear goals, plans, and steps, getting up involves less hesitation. Otherwise, it is easy to change your mind on the spot and sleep a little longer. The emotional brain strongly seeks comfort, but when the rational brain has negotiated with it in advance, resistance is much lower.

**7. Decide in advance when you may sleep later.** Early rising must account for real circumstances. Do not become anxious or guilty over the occasional late morning; simply establish your rules. I allow myself to sleep later in situations such as these:

· a physiological low point;

· a gathering kept me up too late the previous evening;

· the next day requires a long drive or another important, highly demanding activity;

· an abrupt environmental change makes early-morning activity unsuitable...

These are the lessons I have drawn from four years of practice. I hope they offer some inspiration and make your own journey more scientific and less strenuous. Of course, living environments, perseverance, understanding, and physical constitutions differ. Whether early rising suits you is something you must test and decide in light of your actual circumstances.

I believe that if something genuinely moves you, you will act. When I first read *Wake Up at Four*, one sentence struck me: successful people, upon discovering a good habit in someone else, immediately make it their own. That sentence moved me to begin. I now pass it on to you; perhaps it will become the catalyst for your own early-rising habit.

### Section 2: Meditation—One Day, You Will Unlock This Hidden Track

This is something I did not believe in at first, yet once I understood it, I became determined to practice it no matter what: **meditation**.

I know what went through your mind when you saw that word. I once reacted the same way—with surprise and disbelief. In most people's minds, meditation, sitting practice, and Zen cultivation seem to belong to those who have withdrawn from worldly striving. But I suggest that you take it seriously, because it can have a concrete effect on both your quality of life and your competitiveness.

Look into the subject briefly and you will find that many leading figures use this tool, including Bill Gates and Yuval Noah Harari. Meditation is, in a sense, a hidden fast track for growth.

Those who know the secret quietly unlock this track and pull far ahead in certain respects. If you want to rise more effectively, join me in looking behind the practice and picking up the key that opens it.

#### The Seven Little Balls You Need to Know About

What is the greatest difference in ability between ordinary people and highly capable ones? The ability to remain intensely focused for a long time. As Liu Weipeng, author of *Dark Time*, has said, the ability to enter a focused state quickly and the ability to sustain it over time are the two most important habits for efficient learning.

To students with poor grades, the concentration displayed by top students can seem extraordinary. Ordinary people lament that they lack this power, despair over their constant distraction, and may even suspect that their brains are wired differently from those of intelligent people.

That is not the case. We are all human, and our basic equipment is much the same. No one's brain is uniquely special. What differs is how people use the brain—specifically, how they use working memory. The human brain appears formidable, yet consciousness can process only a small number of items at once: on average, seven plus or minus two. Some people can hold a little more and some a little less, but the figure hovers around seven.

If this is new to you, you may think I am making it up. No scientific exposition is needed to test it, however. Try remembering a sequence of unrelated numbers or the names of unfamiliar objects. In the short term, you can usually retain only about seven; beyond that, memory begins to fail. Daily life is similar: we can generally keep only six or seven matters in mind at once. If working memory is full and a new piece of information arrives, an old one must be removed. That is why you can intend to hang up the laundry, take a courier's call, and immediately forget the clothes in the washing machine—the task has been displaced from working memory. For convenience, let us use seven as our baseline, just as a week has seven days. Imagine seven little balls in your mind, each representing part of your mental resources.

The true advantage of high-performing students is easy to see: they can keep all seven balls trained on one task for a long time, enabling high-quality, efficient learning. In the mind of a struggling student, one ball may be playing background music, another wondering what to eat that evening, and a third worrying about an approaching exam. Perhaps only three or four balls remain for study. Worse, the balls not engaged in learning may interfere with or suppress those that are, producing an effect in which 7 - 3 is less than 4. When the top student's balls work together, by contrast, 4 + 3 may become greater than 7. Imagine the gap that this difference in mental efficiency creates as it compounds day after day.

Ultimately, competition in ability is competition in how efficiently we use mental resources. Develop the capacity of even one additional ball and you gain an edge. Fortunately, this difference is not insurmountable. The brain's seven balls can be trained to pursue the same target and cooperate. Meditation is an ideal form of that training.

During meditation, we need only place all our attention on the breath—in other words, ask all seven balls to do the same thing. If one wanders, gently bring it back. Continue this practice and concentration becomes a habit, then an unconscious behavior. Even when you are not meditating, you become better at suppressing scattered thought and gathering a diffuse mind. Put differently, all seven balls become available when you need them. Now you can see how an activity that appears to do nothing and seems unrelated to study can make a person more intelligent.

Scientific research has shown that focused-attention meditation can increase cortical surface area and thicken gray matter. This suggests that the practice can make us physically more intelligent, because cortical surface area and gray-matter thickness are among the factors associated with intelligence.

When we learn skills such as piano, swimming, or gymnastics, neuron density increases in the relevant brain regions and communication among brain cells improves. Once the training stops, however, the number of those neurons begins to decline. The changes associated with meditation are more enduring.

Sit quietly with your eyes closed and attend to your breathing for at least fifteen minutes each day. You will feel the effects. It may help to think of meditation as calisthenics for the brain—which, in fact, it is—just as lifting dumbbells trains the muscles of the arms. The results are simply less visible than muscular development, so many people neither believe in the practice nor wish to try it. But after learning this much, do you need more reasons? Knowing that meditation can make the mind sharper is reason enough to experiment.

#### Emotional Equanimity Is No More Mysterious Than This

I would like to know how you picture these seven balls. In my imagination, most are extremely “light.” An unrealistic fantasy, a scene from a television drama, or a pending task can instantly lift thought away from reality. Changing the real world is difficult; changing it in fantasy costs virtually nothing. When reality frustrates or confines us, we unconsciously seek comfort and freedom in a virtual world. Our instinct to avoid hardship and seek ease finds a ready outlet.

The preface to *Flow* offers an analogy: a person may appear to be sitting quietly while, inside, the mind is a waterfall and countless thoughts pour in. The brain resembles gas in a boiling pot. Thoughts are neither constrained nor connected; each races merrily in its own direction. The inner world is chaotic, with very high entropy.

This is precisely what distraction, mind-wandering, and proliferating fantasies feel like. I can almost picture the little balls ricocheting around the brain's small “room” like Ping-Pong balls. Such disorder makes us restless and impatient, unable to stay with what is before us and eager only for something easier or more entertaining.

Some people carry this habit into bed and automatically switch on a mode of rampant thinking. Distracting thoughts multiply, and peaceful sleep becomes impossible. Although they lie in bed, the mind resembles a kettle that has just begun to boil. Thought will not settle, and they may remain awake all night.

Other balls are very “heavy,” like iron spheres lodged in a corner of the room. Their weight comes from immense pressures—financial strain, career difficulty, emotional crisis, social fear, and more. Heavy feelings sit in the brain like boulders and refuse to leave. People can neither drive them away nor bear to touch them, so the pressures occupy mental bandwidth for long periods, leaving them unable to work and chronically unhappy.

Many people cannot escape either the balls that are too light or those that are too heavy, and they suffer greatly as a result. Some blindly turn to every kind of self-styled spiritual mentor, spend large sums, and still find no solution. In fact, you need no one's help and need spend no money to rescue yourself. Begin meditating and practice regularly; little by little, you can emerge from emotional difficulty and become calm and steady in the face of events.

Concentration gives weight to balls that are too light and allows them to settle. Balls that are too heavy must be faced and accepted during meditation; otherwise, concentration is impossible. Once faced and accepted, hidden pressures become less tormenting. This is also why I have always encouraged people to write down what troubles them. Once written, tension, worry, apprehension, and fear have nowhere to hide beneath clear observation, and the balls naturally grow lighter. With continued practice, the balls in the mind acquire an optimal weight: stable yet controllable. Attention and emotion can then remain in a relatively ideal state.

Meditation, then, is not merely the spiritual cultivation we imagine it to be. When we examine it through knowledge, we discover that this seemingly intangible skill can become a means of winning.

### Section 3: Reading—How to Truly Fall in Love with Books

People make decisions on two levels.

The first is an “emotional decision.” You see someone exercising, taking photographs, or painting; your adrenaline surges, and you immediately declare that you want to do it too.

The second is a “rational decision.” A rational decision may express the same desire, but it means that you have already thought through why you want to do it, how you will proceed, and what difficulties you may encounter.

People accustomed to emotional decisions tend to abandon things halfway. Those who make rational decisions are more likely to turn ideas into reality.

Reading is no exception. When people grow dissatisfied with their present condition and want to become better, the first thing they often think of is reading. Many wise people are voracious readers; books clearly gave them something different, so reading more must surely be good. A choice driven solely by this intense desire is an emotional decision.

The thought that reading can improve us seems to place hope itself within reach. We cannot resist asking others for reading lists and then rushing online or to a bookstore on a buying spree. The moment we take a book from the shelf or click the payment button is incomparable: merely possessing the books seems to make their knowledge ours. Yet when we actually open one, all interest disappears. Difficult theories, abstract logic, tedious examples, black ink on white pages—the experience is worlds away from the fantasy and far less effortless or entertaining than scrolling on a phone. Within days, we can no longer make ourselves turn the pages. What first inspired delight now looks repellent. I suspect your own shelves hold more than a few unopened or dust-covered books.

Another group does somewhat better. They persist, reading enormously and quickly—more than a hundred books a year—and seem genuinely addicted to books. Yet one thing leaves them dissatisfied: despite reading so much, they have not changed at all. Their minds may even be more confused than before.

Reading is beneficial, but it contains many traps. Wanting to read does not mean knowing how. Much of the time, we are engaged in “fake reading” without realizing that a lower-level emotional decision produced it. If this troubles you, join me in making a rational decision and learning to love reading for real.

#### Look at Reading from Another Angle

Futurist Kevin Kelly once told a story while discussing how to become an expert in a field quickly. A friend wanted to enter an entirely new field but had no experience. What could he do? He attended every industry conference he could, listened to experts speak, and seized opportunities to exchange ideas with them and ask questions. Over three years, he spoke with nearly all the leading experts in the field. Through continual learning and accumulation, he gradually began publishing views of his own. At first, they largely synthesized other people's ideas; eventually, he developed his own. Three years later, he too was an expert, and people began paying to invite him to speak at forums.

The lesson can be reduced to one sentence: **the best way to become an expert in an industry quickly is to speak with its experts and learn from them directly.** That may be the highest-level strategy for growth. In reality, however, ordinary people rarely have the opportunity or resources to do it.

What can we do? Read.

Books are the best medium for transmitting ideas. The finest thinking can be found in them. If you **choose your books well**, you can reach the leading ideas in an industry at very low cost. Books record those ideas clearly and accurately, in concise, refined form; some have even been distilled and tested over centuries. Yet you can obtain them directly for the price of a modest book. Seen this way, reading is no longer the repetitive act of scanning black marks on white paper. Every book is effectively an interview with an eminent person, a conversation with a first-rate expert.

Such a conversation requires no costly journey, imposes no time limit, and never tests the other person's patience. The best ideas are available whenever you want them. What could be more agreeable? **Reading is the least expensive way to obtain the highest-level strategies for growth.** It is one of the best paths available to anyone who wants to improve.

A book may also contain a life experience, a marvelous encounter, or a flight of imagination. When we pick up *Man's Search for Meaning*, we can follow Viktor Frankl into a Nazi concentration camp and experience rebirth amid despair. When we open *The Three-Body Problem*, we can enter the magnificent cosmic civilization imagined by Liu Cixin...

**Where our feet cannot take us, words can; what our eyes cannot see, words can show us.** Words carry us across time and space to converse with the greatest thinkers of millennia past. Time and distance cease to bind us. Such power is not easily acquired, but reading places it in our hands.

Without books, we can think only from what we ourselves have seen and heard. To read continuously—and continuously read good books—is to share a circle of friends with the finest minds of every age and nation.

Pay attention and you will also discover that **almost every book reveals how a wise person sees things, weighs alternatives, and makes decisions**. Read enough of them and their superior perspectives begin to improve your own ability to choose. And is not each person's fate the outcome of countless choices? Reading changes destiny by first changing cognition and choice.

Look again at the books around you. Do they still seem like mere objects?

#### Reading Creates High-Density Thought

In ancient times, our ancestors learned to remember dangerous situations so that they could react quickly when needed. If they had paused to decide whether a beast was dangerous every time they encountered one, they might have been eaten long ago. The brain works by thinking something through once, storing it, and retrieving the memory when the same situation returns. It does not need to think again, because thought is slow and costly for the brain. The brain cleverly simplifies complexity, but there is a side effect: we increasingly rely on retrieved memories—or habits—to make decisions.

Humans naturally seek ease and comfort. Without awareness, we avoid effort whenever laziness will do, which makes most people instinctively resistant to thought. Yet civilization has moved from the ancient world into technological and information ages. In modern society, the fundamental difference between people lies in cognitive ability, and cognitive ability depends heavily on the capacity to think. Thought is the core competitive ability on which we stand in modern life. Far-sighted people therefore train themselves deliberately, trying to increase the density of thought in each day.

Charlie Munger once said that every intelligent person he had known read every day, without exception. By comparison, the density of our own thinking is low. Interacting with others, arranging schedules, organizing events, driving, and using phones usually involve nothing more than calling up old memory modules and following habitual responses. Genuine thought occupies very little of our time. How, then, can we rapidly increase our daily density of thought and become more competitive in the future?

Read.

Reading allows our minds to engage the finest thought at any time, understand a subject deeply and comprehensively, and connect it fully with our own lives. Ordinary life rarely produces such a state of mind, yet picking up a book can create it at once. The more time we spend reading, the less we spend on empty entertainment, and the denser our thinking becomes. Over years, those who persist in high-density thought pull far away from those habituated to low-density thought. That is why we now find ourselves looking up to the wise.

#### Reading Is a Skilled Craft

Anyone can pick up a book, but that does not make reading a low-threshold activity. The opposite is true: reading is a craft. Poor technique traps us in inefficient effort. To truly love reading, keep your eyes open and avoid these common mistakes.

**1. Learn to choose books before you read them.** Beginners often ask accomplished people for reading lists. There is nothing wrong with that, but a better starting point is to ask yourself: “What is the most urgent problem I need to solve right now?” Everyone's needs differ. If a book does not fit yours, reading easily becomes an end in itself. If a book immediately helps solve your most pressing real problem, however, you feel the pleasure and value of reading at once, which encourages you to continue. Use reading lists as references, not as your only standard.

Choose books whose difficulty places you just at the edge of your comfort zone: a little challenging, but still within comprehension. No matter how highly others praise a book, if it feels too difficult and holds no interest for you, do not force yourself through it. Some information gap almost certainly remains between you and the book. Forcing it will be painful and can quickly extinguish your desire to read. Especially at the beginning, match interest, difficulty, and need as closely as possible.

When you choose a book well, finishing it usually makes you want to continue. Also notice the books an author repeatedly mentions in a work you admire. These references often provide clues to your next good book.

**Choosing what to read is more important than reading itself.** Books are food for the mind; what we consume shows in what we become. If we indiscriminately read whatever comes to hand, we may “read ourselves sick,” in which case not reading would have been better. Be vigilant when choosing. Shallow content packaged by commercial promotion can do harm. Books tested by time are usually the safer choice.

**2. Read in order to change. Many people assume that finishing a book completes the reading process. In fact, reading is only the beginning. Reflection after reading and practice after reflection matter more than reading itself—particularly with nonfiction. Many people remain on the surface. While reading, they think every passage sounds profound; after finishing, they abandon the book and rush to the next. Reading that is satisfied with mere input has a direct consequence: when they reopen the book later, it is as though they had never seen it. Every trace has vanished. To read a book well often requires several times as much time for reflection and practice, followed by producing something of your own—perhaps an essay, perhaps a new habit. This process demands far more effort than reading itself.**

In terms of weight, volume of reading < volume of thought < volume of action < degree of change. Reading is merely the most superficial behavior. The ultimate goal is to change yourself through thought and action. You might read an entire book about meditation and learn a hundred benefits and a hundred methods, but if you never practice, you gain far less than someone who knows one benefit and one method yet meditates for ten minutes every day.

This also answers another question: depth matters more than speed, and quality matters more than quantity. Reading more and faster is not necessarily good; it may be an illusion of self-satisfaction. If you merely sweep your eyes across words without understanding, in what sense is that efficient? If you learn principles without undergoing any substantive change, what meaning does the reading have? There is no harm in reading slowly. Even if you finish only one book a month, understanding it thoroughly and producing significant change is far better than finishing a book every three days and gaining nothing.

**Keep your eyes fixed on the fundamental goal of change, and many obstacles to reading disappear immediately.** You need not care how much you remember. Even if you forget the entire book, it has not been wasted if one idea or sentence moved you and changed something in your life. Faced with an ocean of knowledge, you therefore have no need for anxiety. Before long, you will be able to look calmly at the people around you who anxiously chase speed-reading records and collect masses of knowledge unrelated to their actual needs. If you reach that state, you have escaped most of reading's common traps.

**3. Advanced methods.** Avoiding the traps merely returns you to level ground. If you want to go further, two suggestions deserve particular attention. **First, pay special attention to associations that arise while you read.** If one idea recalls another piece of knowledge or sparks a connection, notice it and write it down. A connection means that a knowledge network is forming or becoming stronger, and it may even create new knowledge. This is one of the core methods of learning. **Second, reading and writing belong together.** If you can explain what you learned in your own words after reading—and even teach it to someone else—the knowledge will become firmly established in your mind.

Reading offers equality, hope, and opportunity to everyone. If you want to become different, spend a lifetime exploring and practicing it.

### Section 4: Writing—Thank You, Mr. Feynman

Before the internet, it was difficult for an ordinary person to receive extensive feedback through writing. Even if an article appeared in a newspaper or magazine, the author had to wait patiently for readers' letters or phone calls. Today, a light click of the mouse can bring a comment or a “like” within seconds. If an article is good enough, feedback may arrive like a tide.

Whenever I experience this pleasure, I silently give thanks for the age we live in. Its benefits include not only timely responses but frequent surprises. I often receive comments like these:

· Isn't this the Feynman Technique?

· This seems like the Feynman learning method.

· It works on the same principle as the Feynman Technique.

· A masterful use of the Feynman Technique.

It is embarrassing to admit that these comments initially left me bewildered. At the time, I was poorly informed and knew neither who Feynman was nor what the Feynman Technique meant. I hurried to educate myself and discovered how famous both “Mr. Feynman” and his “technique” were in academic circles. The idea that my writing might be associated with him made me a little proud. It also made me curious: how had I unknowingly used a method resembling his? I wanted to understand why.

#### Mr. Feynman and the Feynman Technique

Let us begin with Mr. Feynman. He was a formidable physicist. How formidable? He won the Nobel Prize in Physics, the highest academic honor currently available in science. His achievements owed something not only to intense curiosity and tenacity but also, I believe, to his distinctive habits of thought.

That brings us to his father, Melville, who had a gift for teaching a child how to think. On one occasion, for example, he read young Feynman a description of dinosaurs from the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*: “This dinosaur was twenty-five feet high and its head was six feet across.” He stopped and explained what the sentence meant: if the creature stood in their front yard, it would be tall enough to put its head through an upstairs window. But because its head was slightly wider than the window, forcing it inside would break the frame. With that explanation, an unfamiliar concept acquired a familiar point of reference.

Melville constantly translated knowledge into something practically meaningful in his own words. Without realizing it, Feynman learned a powerful skill from his father: translation. **Whatever you study, work out what it is really saying and what it means in practice, then explain it again in your own words.**

Melville would also ask questions such as this: “Suppose Martians came to Earth but never slept. If they asked you, ‘What is sleep?’ how would you answer?” The question sounds simple, but it is not easy to answer. Try it and you will discover how difficult it is to explain something clearly to a person with no background knowledge.

Through such deliberate and incidental training, Feynman acquired a distinctive way of thinking. In his physics work, he required colleagues reporting to him or explaining something new to use the simplest possible language. If an explanation became needlessly elaborate or complex, the speaker had not understood the subject thoroughly. The Feynman Technique is simply this: **use your own language and the simplest possible words to explain something clearly—ideally so clearly that even a layperson can understand it.**

That is all. Could the famous Feynman Technique really be nothing more intricate or sophisticated? I initially thought there must be more to it, but after consulting a large amount of material, this was my conclusion. Perhaps the greatest truths are simple; we have merely grown accustomed to complication.

#### A Fortunate Accident

Unlike Feynman, I was not fortunate enough to have a father who taught me through vivid stories from childhood. But I was fortunate in another way: I could read for myself.

In November 2016, I read Liu Weipeng's *Dark Time*. One idea from the book has remained with me ever since.

You cannot stand on the eleventh floor, assume that your reader is on the tenth, and expect the reader to understand merely because you describe what is on the eleventh. Your reader is on the first floor. You must understand exactly how the other ten floors beneath your feet were built. This forces you to reconsider, thoroughly and deeply, what you know or previously believed to be correct. The less your audience understands, the deeper your reflection must go.

Perhaps Liu Weipeng did not know the term “Feynman Technique” either. But sustained exploration of learning eventually brings different paths to the same destination. By happy accident, I encountered this valuable way of thinking and began using the simple yet advanced technique without realizing it. From then on, I vaguely understood that even a layperson should be able to understand what I wrote.

That same year, Luo Zhenyu's “sewing on buttons” method of learning, mentioned earlier, also affected me deeply. He did not refer to the Feynman Technique, but the underlying logic was identical: explain new concepts in your own language.

Looking back, Liu Weipeng's and Luo Zhenyu's accounts of writing and reading were themselves perfectly aligned with the Feynman Technique. Neither relied on abstract concepts; they used vivid analogies—an eleven-story building and sewing on buttons—that people could grasp at once and remember. My present writing style developed under the guidance of that awareness. Objectively, though, my understanding and use of the ability remain limited. Sometimes I say too much and fail to be simple; at other times, I cannot explain something completely in my own words.

Even so, I have experienced the method's power. The good news is that I can now identify and apply it deliberately instead of stumbling into it in a haze.

#### Use Simple Language

Writing is only one limited expression of the Feynman Technique. In fact, the technique is a broadly applicable method of learning because it reaches the fundamental way human beings receive information. If you understand the basic structure of the human brain, you know that two little figures live within it at once: the rational and the intuitive. The rational figure is more advanced, but the intuitive figure is more powerful. Most of the time, intuition governs our behavior—including the way we receive information.

This makes it easy to understand why everyone naturally prefers things that are easy, pleasant, and simple. When reading a book or article, we are usually more willing to hear a story than a lecture. Once a writer understands this, I believe the method of creation inevitably changes.

For example, **first use a suitable story to capture the intuitive figure's interest and attention, then let it pass the principle you wish to express on to the rational figure.** This is an excellent strategy; both figures leave satisfied.

Books that communicate knowledge and principles should be especially careful not to pile up abstract concepts merely to appear profound, leaving readers lost in a fog. Charts, models, and terminology from the opening page may not bother the rational figure, but the intuitive one quickly grows impatient, takes the rational figure by the hand, and says, “This is boring. Let's go.” The intuitive figure is strong, which is why didactic writing struggles to hold readers. Of course, we should not become clickbait merchants either, attracting people with a headline and offering no substance. That would leave the rational figure dissatisfied.

Writing as though you were chatting makes an article feel natural. Many beginning writers treat writing with excessive solemnity and soon begin to preach. If you imagine instead that you are speaking with an old friend, the process changes. Conversation is relaxed, and everyone is willing to take part. You cannot be too grave or condescending, nor can you talk only about yourself. You must observe how the other person feels. Good writing is conversation, and good conversation is writing.

After all the explanation, the rule is simple: never use complicated language where simple language will do. This is one core of the Feynman Technique. Yet simplicity means more than ease; it also means brevity and vividness.

I once considered myself well versed in “deliberate practice.” I often guided people's reading with terms such as “information gap” and “the edge of the comfort zone,” sometimes using the image of something within reach if you jump. I thought this rather impressive. Then I encountered the writer Mu Xin's advice on reading: “When you begin, read shallowly—so shallowly that from the outset you can look down on the text from above.” It was a revelation. In a few plain, accessible words, with no abstract concepts or technical terms, he captured the essence of deliberate practice.

On another occasion, a reader named Qingtian asked me about the relationship among the prefrontal cortex, metacognition, and the rational brain. Inspiration struck, and I offered an analogy: the prefrontal cortex is the “physical body” of the rational brain and metacognition. This time I felt I had regained some ground, because finding a fitting analogy is both rare and precious.

Most of us underestimate analogy, treating it merely as a figure of speech in literature. In fact, it is a mode of thought and a cognitive tool. George Lakoff, a founder of cognitive linguistics, has defined and praised analogy in this way: understanding one thing through another is the essence of learning. Human beings can explain the unknown only through what they already know; it is difficult to understand from nothing something we have never encountered. Analogy is the bridge between the unknown and the known.

Use fitting analogies in your writing and you can simplify a host of concepts, enabling people to accept and understand them almost magically. The eleven-story building, sewing on buttons, and the two little figures in the mind all help you grasp complex principles with little mental effort. No wonder Li Xiaolai follows a standing rule in his writing: use abundant analogies; apart from analogy and parallelism, avoid rhetorical devices whenever possible. Practice this principle regularly and you will gradually discover that not only do you write better—you learn better too.

#### Use Your Own Language

Another core of the Feynman Technique is to “express it in your own language.” This is even more important and remarkable than using simple language. Only when we explain what we have learned in our own words do we truly mobilize our existing knowledge, weave loose information into a tight system and network, and perhaps create new understanding. Put differently, **expressing something anew in your own language is like mobilizing an army of thousands at your command.**

Unfortunately, many writers neglect this point and remain for years at the level of “stating knowledge,” never reaching the level of “transforming knowledge.” Some finish a book, list its structure and ideas, and consider their written output complete. That is far from enough. At best, they have moved someone else's knowledge to a new location. They have acquired more troops and horses but cannot command them, which makes them useless.

Good writing must reinterpret what you learn in your own language. It is difficult, and at first you will certainly do it poorly, but it will carry you into the hall of deep learning and accelerate your progress.

Recall Wang Yunwu's method of teaching himself English, discussed earlier. Expressing or reinterpreting something in your own language is deep learning, and it is also a powerful instrument for writing in depth.

Someone will inevitably object: earlier writers have already covered many of these ideas, and I cannot surpass them by writing them again, so what is the point? Scalers, author of *Deliberate Learning*, answers the question this way.

What you understand for yourself sprouts and grows from within your own system. What you see in a book can easily remain at the level of taking a note, drawing a line, sketching a figure, or circling a passage and imagining that you understand it. Do not be afraid that it has already been written. Every one of us can trace a distinct path through this world.

Anyone who truly wants to grow must therefore learn to write. Learning that consists of reading without writing is incomplete and inefficient. **And writing that does not restate ideas in your own language is useless.**

For this reason, we should all ultimately become teachers—not to acquire the title of lecturer, but to learn better ourselves, because teaching is the best form of learning. Teaching compels us to explain something in our own language and in the simplest terms, clearly enough for even an outsider to understand. Writing has the special advantage of allowing us to adjust and revise repeatedly while practicing this skill, until we are satisfied.

#### Thank You, Mr. Feynman

One day, my brother-in-law Chen Ping invited me to see his newly renovated villa. The moment I stepped inside, I realized that writing and renovating a house are essentially the same. The structure of the house is like our thought; its decoration is like our expression. Simple language creates comfort, while one's own language gives personality. When people enter a house that is both comfortable and distinctive, they want to remain and then begin to notice its intelligent, coherent structure. Otherwise, however sound the layout may be, few people will want to linger in a bare, unfinished room.

I seem to have found another decent analogy, though I remain far behind Mr. Feynman. If you are interested, read *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*. You will meet a different kind of Feynman: intelligent, candid, passionate, and uninhibited—half brilliant scholar, half comic performer. He repeatedly overturns expectations in the most surprising places, making people laugh and moving them deeply. I firmly believe that such a life deserves to be known.

I am deeply fortunate that my path crossed his and that he left us this wise idea: the Feynman Technique.

So I would like to take this opportunity to say: thank you, Mr. Feynman!

### Section 5: Exercise—If the Soul Wants to Travel Far, the Body Must Be on the Road

“A strong body, a simple mind.” Who knows how many people this saying has misled?

At first, it contained some truth. In ancient times, living and educational conditions were limited. Manual laborers had to spend long hours working to survive and possessed little time or money for learning, so their general level of education was low. Scholars seeking success in the imperial examinations, meanwhile, could only bury themselves in books indoors. With relatively little physical training, they often appeared frail.

Perhaps people observed this objective pattern and naturally coined the description “a strong body, a simple mind.” Or perhaps scholars, protecting the dignity of their group, preferred to promote such a view. It implied both that strong laborers were nothing special and that there was no shame in a scholar's frailty because intelligence mattered more than anything else.

Language, however, acts back upon thought. A statement describing a phenomenon—the *what*—can be mistaken by those who do not understand it for an explanation of cause—the *why*. A physically strong person may think, “Perhaps I was not born to study.” A good student may think, “It does not matter if I never exercise; developing my limbs might make my mind dull.” The sum of physical and mental power appears fixed: a larger share on one side must leave less on the other. But is that really true? Once the fog clears, the truth may astonish you.

#### Good Things Tend to Be Positively Correlated

The British scientist Francis Galton introduced an important statistical concept: correlation. He observed that when a person's intelligence was high, other aspects of life also tended to be favorable, including self-discipline, economic standing, and physical condition. In other words, good things are often positively correlated. Can we infer that a sound body and sound mind are also positively correlated? I believe we can.

Exercise regulates many of the body's hormones, brings us toward an optimal state, and fills the body's internal ecosystem with energy and vitality. **In people who exercise regularly, that ecosystem resembles a clear spring; in the sedentary, it is more like stagnant water.** Over time, people unwilling to exercise become more vulnerable to anxiety, depression, despondency, and other negative states. Toxins produced by stress damage connections among the brain's billions of nerve cells and gradually cause parts of the brain to atrophy. In this sense, a long-term lack of exercise may make a person “stupider.”

The other, almost unbelievable good news is this: **exercise can cause the brain to grow more new neurons, which means that exercise can physically make us “smarter.”** The brains we inherit do not all begin at the same level. In the same region, one person may have more nerve cells than another, so children display clear differences in talent for language, spatial patterns, music, and other abilities. Learning and development gradually narrow these physiological gaps, leaving effort as the main arena of competition. Yet neuroscience indicates that exercise can trigger neurogenesis. Because the brain regions responsible for attention, awareness, and movement overlap extensively, exercise can also directly and physically improve concentration, self-control, thought, and more.

We can therefore make the following deduction: exercise improves not only physique and mental condition but brain function as well. It strengthens attention, memory, comprehension, and self-control, thereby improving learning, enabling greater achievement, and bringing access to more resources.

Exercise is the starting point for many factors positively associated with a happy life.

#### The Effective Pattern Is “Exercise + Learning”

Even after accepting this conclusion, a doubt remains: why do so many people exercise enthusiastically without displaying this positive correlation? The question deserves investigation, and fortunately the reason is supported by evidence.

Scientific research has confirmed that exercise can produce new neurons in the brain, but those neurons must develop axons and dendrites before they become functioning nerve cells. Put simply, a newborn neuron resembles a tree: it must grow branches and leaves in order to survive (see Figure 8-1).

**Exercise itself is therefore not the key; what you do afterward and the stimulation provided by the environment are what matter.** The effective pattern is to undertake intense, difficult mental activity within one or two hours after exercise—reading, solving problems, memorizing, writing, programming, and so forth. Complex physical skills such as dance or piano can serve the same function, as can unfamiliar social experiences involving new settings, people, or things. Such stimulation encourages new neurons to continue growing. In other words, after exercise, the brain must be thoroughly tested or challenged if it is to keep becoming “smarter.”

The “exercise + learning” pattern must also be sustained, because a new neuron generally requires twenty-eight days to grow to maturity. This is excellent news for knowledge workers. Persist in the pattern and your mind quietly becomes more agile. Neural connections multiply, signal pathways widen, responses accelerate, and learning becomes easier—like a computer whose working memory is continually expanded and whose hardware keeps growing more powerful.

The same is especially true for students, whose primary occupation is already mental work. If they supplement it with “exercise + learning” and place difficult material after physical activity, they can improve the effectiveness of study. Schools that value sport often have students with strong overall abilities. If you have children, do not keep them shut in a room studying all day. Regularly “drive” them outside to run and jump before returning to their lessons; it can be extremely beneficial.

**This is the crucial weakness of most people who exercise: they lack the awareness and habit of actively learning afterward.** They usually watch television, scroll on their phones, play games, shop, attend gatherings, chat with friends, or go directly to sleep—activities that demand no thought or simply feel comfortable. Regrettably, the hard-won new neurons then disappear, and with them goes an opportunity to become “smarter.”

#### How to Exercise Properly

This good news may already have you reaching for your running shoes, but wait. Learning how to exercise scientifically may help you more. Effective exercise is neither torturing yourself at high intensity nor strolling idly outdoors. It means maintaining an appropriate heart rate. For aerobic exercise aimed at weight loss, professional guidance recommends remaining between 60 and 80 percent of maximum heart rate—right at the edge of the body's comfort zone. Half an hour a day can produce excellent results.

If that sounds troublesome, use a simple rule: **during aerobic exercise, keep yourself slightly out of breath.** While running, for example, maintain a fast enough pace to become a little breathless for one or two minutes, then switch to brisk walking while you recover. Repeat. Almost everyone can manage this amount of activity.

When exercise comes up, I suspect most people think of running. For better results, however, combine it with complex movement. After a ten-minute aerobic warm-up, practice yoga, dance, gymnastics, tai chi, or a similar activity. Complex movement recruits nerve cells throughout the brain. The more intricate the activity, the more intricate the synaptic connections and the denser their growth. **A good exercise routine therefore contains both aerobic exercise and complex movement** (see Figure 8-2).

#### A Stronger Body, a Stronger Mind

Two million years ago, humans lived by hunting and gathering. To find food, our ancestors had to walk an average of eight to sixteen kilometers every day. Only in the past ten thousand years did humanity enter agricultural civilization, and only in the last century did material abundance free us from expending so much energy in search of food.

People generally believe that exercise exists to make the body healthier and more attractive. Posters in gyms and workout photos on social media reinforce that idea. In fact, **the greater significance of exercise lies not in training the body but in training the brain.** It not only makes people more optimistic but also makes the mind more agile, ultimately improving both health and cognition.

Once people grow accustomed to sitting, however, they no longer want to move and unknowingly enter a downward spiral in quality of life: the lower and more dejected they feel, the less they want to exercise; the less they exercise, the lower and more dejected they become. The best way to break the cycle is to work up a sweat—put on running shoes and sprint, pick up a racket and swing...

Fortunately, more and more people are awakening. They encourage one another to learn and exercise, and a literary maxim has become popular: body and soul—one of them must always be on the road.

Language influences thought. “A strong body, a simple mind” should more reasonably become “a strong body, a stronger mind.” Nor must we choose between body and soul. We cannot study without exercising, exercise without studying, or alternate between the two according to our moods. I believe you will now prefer this formulation: if the soul wants to travel far, the body must be on the road.

The clearer the cognition, the firmer the action.

Beginning now, give your exercise plan a new meaning.

# Conclusion: A First-Class Life Is Not Wealth but Awareness

About ten years ago, several close friends and I were talking about things that had happened in the past. To my surprise, I had no memory of many details they mentioned: the month in which something occurred, who had been present, how those people were related, and what they had done. I vaguely remembered the event itself, but many details sounded new, as though I had not been there. My memory has never been good, and I even actively forget unpleasant experiences. But that conversation made me ask a question I could no longer avoid: what had I done with all those years?

The question frightened me. A vacuum seemed to open before me. I could not recall what I had actually done over the previous several years or name even one or two memorable events. I had lived each day according to routine, yet it seemed I had done nothing. Life had flowed past like a silent stream, and when I looked closely, it had left no trace. At that moment I felt profoundly weightless and experienced anxiety for the first time.

To ease that anxiety and keep myself from living like a fool, I decided to do something. On New Year's Day 2010, I prepared a planner and made my first entry. From that day on, my life began to leave a clear record, and I have never stopped. On New Year's Day 2014, I switched to a phone log—the system's built-in calendar—because an electronic record was easier to carry, update, and search.

Without my noticing, ten years passed. This is the longest practice I have ever sustained deliberately. Later, in *A Strange Life*, I learned about Alexander Lyubishchev, who tracked his use of time for fifty-six years and lived a remarkable life. In one respect, I had been doing the same thing: recording my schedule in detail.

My command of time remains far inferior to Lyubishchev's. For me, the greatest value of tracking time is that it makes me aware of time's existence and allows me to live with greater certainty. My memory is still poor. When friends bring up something from the past, I may initially have nothing to add, but seconds later I can state the details with remarkable accuracy: the date and hour, who was present, where we went, and what we did. My friends can hardly believe it. Yet that was about the limit of the practice. It was a habit that gave me an external memory; it did not make my awareness of life especially distinctive.

Then, in February 2017, I read Cheng Jia's *Study Properly* and decided to begin a “daily reflection.” Who could have expected this inconspicuous practice to open an entirely new world?

#### From Logging to Reflecting

Keeping a schedule did not make me as accomplished as Lyubishchev, but it **increased my sensitivity to time**. Whenever I recorded wasted hours—two hours lost to my phone, for example—I felt guilty and instinctively wanted to reduce the waste. Such entries were hardly honorable. Who wants a life composed of trivialities like these? At other times, I was extraordinarily busy, often to the point of confusion. Looking at page after crowded page, I could not help asking, “What exactly am I busy doing?” Much of the busyness was passive response to external pressure, not active pursuit of anything I wanted. If outside arrangements continually pulled me along, what meaning did a “full” day really have?

Most of us want a richer and more vivid life. Under the natural scrutiny of my chronological logs, I increasingly longed to live efficiently and meaningfully. The desire grew stronger, but for a long time it had no outlet.

When I began practicing daily reflection, I discovered a new outlet for self-awareness. It was like keeping a log in depth. With only a little time each day, I could **review the event or realization that had affected me most** and maintain a deeper awareness of life. What could be better? I began, and soon I could not stop.

On the 160th day, the thought of writing for a public WeChat account arose. Those reflections had forced me to examine my condition and goals honestly and had let me experience the benefits of writing. I became aware of more and more details in daily life and could improve myself through small things without outside assistance. The benefits are difficult to put into words. I wanted to share the practice so that more people could know and benefit from it.

#### The Great Way Is Simple

At first, I knew only that daily reflection was useful. I never imagined how useful it would become. Looking back now, I am surprised by the number of fundamental principles hidden inside this small practice. This is often what it means for the great way to be simple: it is so simple that you refuse to believe it could be the way. Let me first explain my method.

It is straightforward: notice whatever touches you most during the day. It may be a joyful insight or a painful perplexity. If it strikes a spark in your mind, pick it up and review it in writing. The review itself usually has only three parts:

① Describe what happened, so that you can later reconstruct the scene;

② Analyze the cause, asking “why” repeatedly until a deeper insight emerges;

③ Identify an improvement, distilling it whenever possible into one cognitive lesson or one action.

That is all. A daily reflection may contain only a few sentences or several thousand words. Follow the movement of your mind and the demands of the situation. The point is to see the problem more clearly and change.

Once, for example, I felt exhausted after driving. In that day's reflection, I realized that parts of my body unconsciously remained tense and rigid behind the wheel. From then on, I deliberately reminded myself to relax, use the least force needed for each movement, and start and stop the car as smoothly as possible. Before long, driving became a pleasure. My family also said that riding with me had become much more comfortable because the sudden stops and hard braking were gone.

On another occasion, my supervisor criticized me in harsh language, and I immediately wanted to strike back. In my reflection that day, I examined his criticism carefully and concluded that he had identified the problem accurately. His mood had been poor, but his intention was sound; moreover, he had the same temper with most people and was not singling me out. I felt relieved at once and learned a useful principle: **always separate another person's emotion from the substance of that person's opinion. Then even the worst encounter can teach you something useful.**

Before the Lantern Festival in 2019, my wife, daughter, and I visited the Confucius Temple area in Nanjing. By the time we arrived, we were exhausted and wanted somewhere to rest. Every nearby business with seats was packed, and none had room for three people, so we dragged ourselves through what remained of the outing. In that day's reflection, I suddenly realized that we could have rested separately. Pizza Hut had two seats and KFC had one. Our goal was to rest, not necessarily to rest together. A mental set had trapped us. Through reflection, I examined and improved my choice; the next time I meet a similar problem, I will be less rigid.

If you practice reflection, you will inevitably begin attending to body, emotion, and thought, continually refining and improving yourself. Inspiration, sudden understanding, and creative ideas will also appear. Practice, and you will discover a great deal.

A reflective life is like picking up one shining stone each day from the stream of time and polishing it carefully. Before long, we possess a whole bag of cognitive crystals—the marks and crystallized essence of our lives. **Built upon these crystals, the quality and density of our lives will far exceed those of people who never reflect.**

We may even attain a higher cognitive level than our peers while still young. Those who travel without reflecting may stand in the river of life for years and still emerge empty-handed.

This is why I cannot resist recommending daily reflection to my readers. They soon report subtle changes in their lives. A reader named Yinian wrote: “Recently I have been writing several reflections every day, observing my daily behavior, emotions, and decisions, and actively looking for ways to improve. I am changing myself little by little through small things. The experience is grounding and beautiful, and my inner state has become much steadier. I understand the value of certain things, know what I want, and know what to do. I have become grounded and diligent.” It is no exaggeration to say that anyone confronting a problem can use reflection as a catalyst. Write it down and review it, and many problems begin to resolve themselves.

Many people, of course, do not believe reflection can be so powerful. Perhaps they have never practiced it, perhaps they practiced incorrectly, or perhaps they do not understand its underlying principles. Once I explain them, however, the realization will be immediate. Let me present them one by one.

#### A Sudden Understanding

Daily reflection contains at least three fundamental principles.

**First, it follows the “triggered learning method.”** This method is scientific and important, and it is the first threshold daily reflection must cross.

Because I already kept a schedule, I did not focus my reflections on the day's itinerary. I focused instead on the points that affected me most. This fortunately allowed me to avoid the trap of treating a diary as reflection.

Many people keep journals, but mostly record what they did or pour out fragments of emotion. Such a journal is not reflection; the two differ fundamentally. It lacks a point of inner impact, rarely analyzes causes deeply, and produces no distilled action. It merely releases emotion within the comfort zone and therefore cannot generate substantial progress. Good reflection notices whatever touches us most—pain, delight, something we cannot stop thinking about. These impressions lie at the edge of our comfort zone for growth, and growth is fastest when we learn at that edge.

**Second, it uses metacognition.** The essence of metacognition is to examine our feelings and thoughts, discover deficiencies, and improve them, correcting cognitive bias at the lowest possible cost. Daily reflection is precisely such self-examination. It gives us ample time to replay a brief thought process, locate its flaws, refine it, and find a better perspective. It also activates the rational brain, clears emotional blind spots, and changes instinctive default choices. The next time we meet a similar problem, we are less likely to drown in emotion or find ourselves unable to make a better decision. Long practice greatly improves cognition, emotional maturity, and the ability to choose.

Metacognition is humanity's ultimate capacity. Once it is activated, self-awakening begins. I was fortunate to move from confusion toward awakening, and daily reflection was what opened metacognition for me.

**Third, it follows the principles of deliberate practice.** One essence of deliberate practice is to learn with a clear objective. Practicing piano does not mean mindlessly playing the same passage again and again; it means returning repeatedly to a precisely identified problem. That is how progress accelerates. Life works the same way. If we merely drift and pass day after day without thought, we do little more than age. But if we **live with key principles in mind**, we grow rapidly.

Consider the earlier reflection on driving. Once we distill the principle of remaining relaxed, we can remind ourselves before the next drive and keep watching the issue throughout the journey until good habits form. People who do not reflect never notice such principles. They remain habitually tense and rigid, stopping and braking abruptly without realizing it.

This way of growing applies in any setting. A person without the key principles and one who has mastered them progress at entirely different rates. They may live outwardly similar lives, but one remains still while the other continually changes. Within a few years, the gap becomes immense.

These three principles are enough to make us take daily reflection seriously, but at least three more benefits remain to be explored.

**First, it can save portions of our lives.** An insight sometimes flashes into the mind—a realization about life that leaves us exhilarated and refreshed. If we fail to record it, it may vanish almost at once. Some time later the realization returns, and we exclaim, “I thought of that before!” The exclamation tells us that we have wasted part of our lives, **because an insight may appear in an instant, but behind it lies the accumulation of lived experience**. We failed to capture it the first time and have had to repeat the process. If we miss it again, it will return again, and our lives become inefficient through repetition. Whenever an idea appears, record it promptly, even if only as one or two keywords. Organize and polish it later. Enlarging that understanding is equivalent to saving a portion of life.

**Second, it improves our sensitivity to detail.** In January 2017, I encountered the idea of “the ability to sense subtle change” in Li Xiaolai's Zhihu post “A Different World.” He said this capacity was exceptionally important everywhere. I had not yet begun daily reflection. The idea seemed reasonable, but only after practicing reflection myself did I fully appreciate how right it was.

Without reflection, life inevitably becomes coarse. Like me ten years ago, we may do many things without being aware of them. Reflection heightens our perception of life and lets us capture impressions and connections in small events: a movement, a sentence, a scene, a choice, an emotion. Any of these can produce insight. Simply carrying the intention to reflect each day greatly increases awareness because we must discover material in what we live.

The finer our perception, the finer our self-improvement. **The better the reflection, the smaller and subtler its point of focus usually is.** Everyone can notice what is broad and obvious; not everyone can sense minute changes. That sensitivity reveals the reflector's level.

**Third, it enables us to face pain.** Life consists of little more than joy, ordinariness, and pain. Everyone likes joy, yet easily forgets and fails to cherish it. Ordinariness numbs us and erodes awareness. Pain alone is something everyone tries to avoid.

Confusion about life, declining grades, heartbreak, weak self-control, fear of difficulty—our first response to pain is distress. What happens next is the dividing line of growth. A minority face the pain and reflect on their mistakes; most flee and sink into negative emotion.

Reflection carries an inherent power to face pain. Reflect and record, and you discover that **pain is not merely something bad—it is a signal and prompt for growth sent to us by life itself.** As in the earlier example, when I faced my supervisor's criticism, I learned to separate opinion from emotion. Otherwise, resentment would have kept me trapped and consumed my energy without producing growth.

Anyone facing a problem can therefore use reflection as a catalyst. Write it down and review it, and an answer will naturally emerge. In time, we may even react this way: when difficulty or discomfort arrives, we feel a private flicker of pleasure because another opportunity for self-improvement has appeared. What a fine state of life that is. It can dissolve many troubles—if you are willing to practice it.

#### Begin to Act

The preceding discussion is almost a complete guide to reflection. If you are willing to begin reflecting each day, I will naturally be pleased. From observing many practitioners, however, I have identified several precautions worth knowing in advance.

**1. Do not become bound by form.** Many people assume that daily reflection means never missing a day and become discouraged enough to quit after a single interruption. In fact, “daily” is only a reminder to continue. Missing an occasional day does not matter. Record a keyword and organize it when time permits. If one day brings no feeling worth recording, it is fine to write nothing. Others become excessively formal, composing each reflection as a polished article until the effort drains their motivation.

Remember: **the ultimate purpose of reflection is change, not perfection of form.** If a single sentence changes you, the reflection has fulfilled its purpose.

**2. Distill a cognitive lesson or an action whenever possible.** Do not become immersed in emotional venting or rambling. Because reflection ultimately aims at change, **extract specific and actionable lessons and steps whenever possible** so that they can guide your future life. Otherwise reflection easily turns into an ordinary diary and loses much of its effect.

**3. Keep an action list.** Once reflections accumulate, many action points are easily forgotten. Create a separate list of the most important ones and review it from time to time. This helps sustain action.

**4. Be radically honest with yourself.** Reflection is for you, so you need not care what others think. Especially when reflecting on pain, **be completely honest and dig out your truest thoughts**. Even if those thoughts make you feel deeply embarrassed or ashamed, speak them to yourself, acknowledge them, and accept them. Honesty and acceptance of your imperfect self are what permit rebirth.

**5. Read widely.** Some people live relatively uneventful lives, while others initially lack enough perceptual sensitivity to notice moments of inner impact. In that case, read. Good books contain thought of great density. A conversation with a wise person will always bring ideas and information that touch you. Be patient and keep practicing; sensitivity will naturally grow.

**6. Choose a suitable medium.** I do not recommend paper because it is difficult to search. Use electronic documents or note-taking tools such as Shimo Docs or Evernote.

#### Live a First-Class Life

I have long wanted to know where awakening begins. I now have a rough answer: awareness. Once a person can perceive what is good and what is not, that person will inevitably make new choices.

Whenever I walk through a crowd and see someone slouching with rounded shoulders, I instinctively remind myself to raise my chest and head so that I do not display the same lack of vitality. Yet many people around me do not care and may unconsciously imitate or be assimilated by what they see. In the same environment, some drift with the current while others step outside it and perceive its harmful influence. The difference arises from the degree of individual awareness.

Awareness has been a discipline of the exceptional since ancient times.

Zengzi examined himself three times a day; Benjamin Franklin reviewed thirteen virtues daily. Today, we lack neither food nor clothing, and many people are affluent, yet they do not necessarily live first-class lives. Without awareness, a person surrounded by fine clothes and rich food may still feel neither happiness nor joy and may remain trapped by boredom, emptiness, and pain.

In other words, even without immense wealth, you can heighten your ability to perceive the world and improve yourself by increasing awareness. With awareness, we can gradually begin to live first-class lives, even if that life does not arrive quickly.

# Afterword: Change Together, Move Forward Together

In June 2016, I read Li Xiaolai's *Stanford University's Course on Entrepreneurship and Growth*. I no longer remember what the book said, but one sentence has remained with me: “If what you want does not yet exist, create it yourself.” Reading often works this way. You pass through page after page, and in the end one or two sentences move and change you. The purpose of reading has then been fulfilled. I am gratified that I have now fulfilled this wish.

On the path of personal change and growth, I had always hoped to find a book on cognitive awakening that would bring me a sudden, clarifying realization. Unfortunately, I never found one that satisfied me completely. So a thought arose: why not write one myself? Less than three years later, this labor of conviction is before you.

I believe this book will become a visible landmark in the field of personal growth, guiding more people who want to grow but have lost their way. I even believe it can reach at least fifty years into the future. Its theories concern fundamental mechanisms, its subjects arise from genuine needs, and its ideas have been tested in practice by both me and many readers. As long as the mechanisms of human evolution remain unchanged, every generation will encounter many of the same confusions while growing.

This book does not discuss business or the workplace, nor does it offer ingenious tricks. Many examples concern ordinary activities such as reading, writing, and running. For precisely that reason, it can serve as a methodology with lasting relevance for ordinary people. As a reader named Wei Jiamin commented: “Qingnao's articles analyze things scientifically. They contain no motivational broth and draw no class divisions; anyone can benefit from reading them.” Whatever your circumstances, if you make sincere connections and act steadily, you will be able to reduce anxiety, accomplish things, and realize the dreams in your heart.

If this book helps you—if even one idea touches and genuinely changes you—it has fulfilled its mission. Whenever possible, I look forward to hearing your response.

The book naturally has shortcomings. Some ideas recur across chapters; some topics may not be classified ideally; and my expression may sometimes be verbose. The reason is that fundamental concepts resemble a network spread beneath the ground, linked in every direction. The same concept can explain many phenomena, so intersections are unavoidable. More importantly, my ability as a writer is limited, and I have not thought through and organized every problem deeply enough. I hope you will be tolerant and offer suggestions. I will receive them humbly and continue to revise. Some subjects may also seem scattered—the cluster of rising early, meditating, reading, writing, and running, for example—but in essence they all revolve around the central aim of improving cognitive ability. I make this explicit to prevent misunderstanding.

I had another, more private motive for writing this book. Because my work has kept me in another city for years, I have often been absent while my daughter was growing up. Time is a one-way road; once some things are missed, they cannot be recovered. To compensate in some small way for that lost companionship, I offer this book to her as a special gift. When life confuses her in the future, I hope she will know that her father has always stood behind her: “I know you will understand me in the end. I will strive to be your example, and you will surely become an example to many others.”

I would also like to explain the meaning of the cover image.

Blue and red are the book's two thematic colors. They represent the rational and intuitive powers of the brain. The white space between them faintly forms a human brain, symbolizing that when a person learns to use knowledge and wisdom to guide both rationality and intuition, cognitive awakening becomes possible. I hope this book helps people leave confusion behind and, through thought, gain lucid cognition, definite goals, clear paths, and unburdened emotions.

At the end of the book, allow me to express my gratitude.

First, I thank the age and fate. Had I been born a few years earlier or later, or had the course of my life differed even slightly, this book might never have come into being. Whatever a person achieves, the larger background of historical moment, luck, and environment cannot be ignored. To see only one's own effort and sacrifice is narrow and unobjective. Second, I thank my wife, who has carried so much for me; without her support, I could not have completed the book. I also thank Kou Jiaying for discovering me, Chen Rui for the introduction, editor Chen Suran for her discerning eye, and Posts & Telecom Press for its generous support. Your enthusiasm brought this book into the world. I owe special thanks to teachers Weilan, Wang Shimin, Shi Beichen, Yijia, and Yi Ren Yongcheng for lifting up an unknown author and recognizing *Cognitive Awakening*, allowing it to reach and help more people through your influence. Above all, I thank you—every one of my readers. Your eventual insights, changes, and responses are my greatest and truest reward.

May this book illuminate your inner world and become a lighthouse on the road ahead. And may more people discover it, awaken together, and move forward side by side.
